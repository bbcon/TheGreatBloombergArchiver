Burning Down the House <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3Z2LhwMprLQ>

It’s not the best idea to goad markets into betting against you. Tuesday saw 
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announce that“I am the house now.” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/bessent-dares-traders-to-bet-against-yen-i-am-the-house-now?cmpid=BBD091026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8684>
 He was talking about the yen, which he’s trying to strengthen, but he also 
said that the increase in the Treasury’s bond buyback program was to treat a 
“fever” in the bond market. He’s on record that he wants yields to be lower.

After that buildup, Wednesday’s announcement that buybacks were being tripled 
wasn’t enough. The market responded with disappointment 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-09/us-more-than-doubles-long-dated-debt-buyback-size-to-6-billion?cmpid=BBD091026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8684>
 that he hadn’t used even more shock and awe, and pushed up the 10-year 
Treasury yield to 4.84%, its highest in three years. Bessent has revealed that 
he’s prepared to intervene, and it was predictable that the market would 
respond by testing just how far he’d go. The impact echoed in stocks:

It will be interesting to see what Bessent does next, but the day’s hectic 
events also highlight another conundrum. Bond yields have been rising steadily 
without tipping over the stock market. Even Wednesday, the S&P 500 clawed back 
much of its fall. Could there be a tipping point ahead at which higher yields 
finally bring down stocks, and could yields hit 5% for the 10-year Treasury?

That level — closer now than it has been in three years — is widely cited as 
the potential watershed because it’s a round number, but also because it would 
be the highest in almost two decades. Most major markets are already at their 
highest since the Global Financial Crisis, but the US is still below the 5% it 
briefly reached in 2023. If the 10-year passes 5% and stays above it this time, 
some much more alarming scenarios become much more plausible:

The key reason investors feel able to look through rising bond yields is, 
inevitably, the AI buildout. That is seen as an external factor that 
counteracts the strong macroeconomic headwinds. To quote Freya Beamish and 
Davide Oneglia of TS Lombard:
When tech companies are chasing the notion of infinite demand, it is quite 
hard to slow them down with a few basis points, particularly as the process of 
leveraging up has only just begun in big tech, though there are some clear 
front-runners. While the financing loop works, it is self-reinforcing.
Spectacular earnings growth has so far more than counterbalanced the tighter 
multiples that have come with the shift in the bond market. And investors seem 
convinced that it can continue. The latest survey of global asset allocators by 
Absolute Strategy Research finds comfortable majorities believing both that 
yields will continue to rise and that the stock market will be higher a year 
from now. Earnings growth is seen as making stocks immune to a rising cost of 
money, although Absolute Strategy’s David Bowers cautions that “some investors 
are viewing this corporate earnings/margins story in isolation” and 
underestimating the effects that AI investment could have in raising interest 
rates and inflation.

If any stock market is particularly at risk from rising bond yields, it is the 
US. That’s because a huge chunk of American companies’ value is tied up in 
future earnings. These will be automatically rendered cheaper by higher bond 
yields, as they would have to be discounted at a higher rate. Michel Lerner of 
UBS’ HOLT service illustrates this in the following chart, which shows the 
percentage of each market’s enterprise value that comes from future investment. 
The US, in red, is far higher than anyone else and well above its own historic 
average, while the UK, in black, seems to have lost the confidence of the 
market:

The vulnerability is evident. Higher yields must inevitably increase the focus 
on firms with weaker balance sheets and on the exceptionally rosy forecasts 
currently embedded in US valuations. But investors know this, and remain 
confident. A 5% 10-year yield could hasten a decisive downturn for equities — 
but it’s much more likely to stem from an end to the rosy earnings assumptions.

$100 Oil: The Sequel

This was a round number that we thought we could forget about. Early in 
Wednesday trading, Brent crude topped $100 per barrel for the first time in two 
months. The price for November delivery shows that the market never expected 
the price to be so high this late in the year. There’s confidence that the 
price will retreat closer to $80 next summer, but this is an alarming 
development that nobody expected even weeks ago:

Enduring high crude prices have their most significant impact on the refined 
products that consumers experience directly. According to the American 
Automobile Association, the average diesel price is at a record, exceeding the 
previous one set after the Ukraine invasion. Enduring high crude prices ensure 
that these critical inputs for industry and agriculture will also stay 
elevated. Gasoline, particularly politically sensitive, is below its peaks this 
year but has topped $4 per gallon:


High oil prices mechanically increase headline inflation in the short term. As 
this simple model from Absolute Strategy Research illustrates, if gasoline 
stays where it is for the next six months, US headline inflation should rise to 
4.9% — a level that would more or less force the Federal Reserve to raise rates:

This seems truly horrendous news. Higher oil prices act like a tax hike to 
brake growth, and also require rate increases that weigh on growth. But somehow 
markets aren’t seeing it that way. One-year inflation breakevens are barely 
half their level when the $100 mark was first breached back in March:

And the stock market now believes it can live with higher oil. For the first 
month of the Iran conflict, Brent and the S&P 500 acted as mirror images, with 
stocks gaining only when Brent fell. That relationship is over. Global stocks 
are now 14% above their level when oil first hit $100::

Why so blase? At one level, the risk of really serious all-out Vietnam- or 
Iraq-style war on the ground has diminished.The US plainly doesn’t want to do 
that 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-09-08/trump-s-iran-war-forgot-about-nukes?cmpid=BBD091026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8684>
. Escalation to destroying Gulf oil infrastructure is also yet to happen. And 
global stocks have prospered over the last six months.

But the Strait of Hormuz is closed off in a way that seemed unlikely during 
the brief thaw two months ago. Prediction markets reflect that hopes of a 
reopening any time this year are draining away, and oil futures disprove the 
positive assumptions of traders closest to the action. If most nightmarish 
scenarios are off the table, some extremely bad ones remain:

“The market isn’t just pricing today’s disruption, it’s pricing the odds of 
more disruption to come,” warned Mark Malek of Siebert Financial. Even though 
the stock market implies that the energy shock will not derail the economy or 
earnings, he cautioned: “Markets can simultaneously be rational about long-term 
fundamentals and complacent about low-probability risks.”



Private Stress

Private credit is no longer in the eye of the storm, but that doesn’t mean the 
storm is over. Redemption requests at Blackstone Private Credit Fund, among the 
first Business Development Companies (BDCs) to experience heavy withdrawals, 
remain near 10% of shares outstanding. That’s neither an improvement nor a 
deterioration from the prior quarter. From a glass-half-full perspective, 
Barclays’ Peter Troisi sees the stability as tolerable, especially given its 
relatively wide spreads.

For BDC spreads to extend their rally — which have narrowed almost 100 basis 
points from their widest of the year — Troisi argues that investors need to see 
withdrawal requests decline:

Almost a year after JPMorgan’s Jamie Dimon famously complained of cockroach 
infestation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-14/dimon-s-cockroach-fear-revives-threat-of-growing-credit-cracks?cmpid=BBD091026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8684>
, there are signs of financial distress in rising defaults. Fitch’s broad 
measure of defaults has shot up to 6% from 5% in 2024 as publicly traded 
business development companies’ also lost value.

It remains unclear how the credit cycle might evolve as more debt comes to 
market.Credit investors are set to be offered 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/buyout-debt-worth-138-billion-is-set-to-land-in-credit-market?cmpid=BBD091026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8684>
 more than $138 billion of buyout debt in the coming months, with US issuance 
at its highest level since 2007 and European issuance at its highest since 
2021. New supply is already visible in real time. At least seven companies 
began loan repricing marketing this week.

Private credit has funded 82% of buyout deals so far in 2026, up from 61% in 
2019. The question now is whether it can keep absorbing such a large share of 
the LBO market as debt supply swells.

Even as appetite remains strong, the Blue Owl/Loparex episode 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-05/blue-owl-slashes-private-loan-to-near-zero-amid-bankruptcy-risk?cmpid=BBD091026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8684>
, where loans marked at par and near-90 cents moved to near zero within months, 
is a case study of what happens when LBO debt in private credit portfolios 
deteriorates. That was driven by the “SaaSpocalypse,” the thesis that AI could 
disrupt the business models of software-as-a-service companies.

Another risk is that the portion of the $138 billion pipeline for debt where 
banks and private-credit firms share exposure could come under pressure as more 
companies struggle to repay their debts. Steve Caprio at Deutsche Bank points 
out that over 80% of B-rated debt sits in floating-rate leveraged loans or 
private credit, which leaves those borrowers in a rough spot with a big wall of 
maturities coming due.

Direct lending loans rarely trade, and their prices are hard to pin down, so 
weakening credits can go unnoticed for a while. If the markdowns arrive all at 
once, lenders will finally have to put a number on the damage. It’s still too 
soon to declare that the storm — or the cockroach infestation — is over.

—Richard Abbey
