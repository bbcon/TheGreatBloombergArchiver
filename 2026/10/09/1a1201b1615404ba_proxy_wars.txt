The foreign rivalries that have helped fuel Sudan’s devastating war are 
threatening to engulf 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/why-ethiopia-eritrea-tensions-pose-another-threat-to-red-sea-shipping?cmpid=BBD100926_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9919>
 a second major Horn of Africa nation.

Renewed fighting in Ethiopia collides with a widening contest for influence 
over the Red Sea, Nile River waters and the region’s vital trade routes.

Ethiopia’s conflict has rapidly escalated since the Tigray People’s Liberation 
Front joined forces with six other militia groups seeking to challenge Prime 
Minister Abiy Ahmed’s government.

The violence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-08/us-issues-ethiopia-travel-advisory-cites-long-range-attacks?cmpid=BBD100926_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9919>
 threatens to revive a civil war that killed hundreds of thousands of people 
before a 2022 peace agreement. It also raises the very real prospect of outside 
powers exploiting Ethiopia’s internal divisions.

The danger extends beyond Ethiopia’s borders. Relations with Eritrea, once 
Abiy’s wartime ally, have deteriorated over Addis Ababa’s demand for access to 
the Red Sea and accusations that Asmara is backing anti-government forces.

Ethiopia says Eritrean troops crossed into Tigray this week, prompting 
Ethiopiandrone strikes <https://acleddata.com/country/ethiopia> and deepening 
fears of a wider confrontation, according to the conflict monitor Acled.

Egypt has emerged as another potential adversary.

Cairo regards Ethiopia’s Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on a major Nile 
tributary as a strategic threat to its water supplies and has strengthened 
military and diplomatic ties with Eritrea and other neighbors toconstrain Addis 
Ababa 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/two-blasts-heard-in-ethiopian-capital-as-drone-flights-suspended?cmpid=BBD100926_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9919>
.

Sudan’s army-aligned government has also accused Ethiopia of facilitating 
attacks on its territory, while Khartoum’s warring factions receive support 
from competing foreign actors.

The United Arab Emirates, meanwhile, has cultivated close ties with Abiy’s 
government as it expands its economic and geopolitical footprint across the 
Horn of Africa and beyond.

Abu Dhabi has been accused by United Nations investigators of supplying 
Sudan’s paramilitary Rapid Support Forces, allegations it denies.

But the proxy wars in the Horn 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/ethiopia-plans-somalia-troop-pullout-to-fight-rebels-back-home?cmpid=BBD100926_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9919>
 have inevitably placed the UAE and Ethiopia on the opposite side of a regional 
divide from Egypt and other governments aligned with Sudan’s military.

The result is now an increasingly interconnected web of hostilities in which 
arms, drones, commercial interests and strategic ports are all at play.

Sudan has demonstrated how external intervention can prolong a war and 
devastate a country. Ethiopia’s renewed fighting risks turning that model into 
a regional disaster.— Simon Marks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AU8TVLVuBn8/simon-marks?sref=LlL4jr17&cmpid=BBD100926_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9919>
