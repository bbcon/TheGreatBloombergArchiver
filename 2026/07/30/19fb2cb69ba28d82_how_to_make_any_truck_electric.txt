Making trucking electric

By Akshat Rathi 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUdOobfNzkQ/akshat-rathi?cmpid=BBD073026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7159>

Trucking is big business in the US, but it relies on pricey fuel that produces 
a lot of planet-warming emissions. Electric trucks are an obvious answer, 
however their sticker price is two or more times as expensive as traditional 
freight trucks. Even if the cost were to fall, it would take time to replace 
existing rigs.

Robotics startup Revoy <https://www.revoy.com/> thinks it has a solution to 
allow any truck to go electric for part of its journey. The San Francisco-based 
company has built a detachable unit with an electric motor and batteries that 
sits between the truck’s tractor and trailer. It has just raised $27 million in 
a Series A round to scale up its operations.
The Revoy plug-in device in Indio, California.Photographer: Ethan Noah 
Roy/Bloomberg
On a full charge, Revoy’s electric dolly can lug an 80,000-pound truck for 
more than 200 miles. The company’s dream is to eventually build out a network 
of hubs along roadways, where truckers could swap in a fresh device. Revoy 
estimates that the unit can reduce carbon emissions by as much as 85%.

“Our technology allows you to compete with diesel at cost, which is not true 
yet for other electric trucks in the US,” said Peter Reinhardt, chief executive 
officer of Revoy.

Read more: Goldman Says Diesel at ‘Epicenter’ of Squeeze as Refineries Hit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/goldman-says-diesel-at-epicenter-of-squeeze-as-refineries-hit?cmpid=BBD073026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7159>

The cost to operate Revoy’s system would be equivalent to powering a truck 
with diesel, based on prices before the US war with Iran, according to 
Reinhardt, who is also CEO of carbon-removal company Charm Industrial Inc. The 
average retailprice <bbg://securities/NGAPGRPD%20/GP> of diesel in the US 
remains about 40% above the pre-war level of about $3.80 per gallon.

Revoy would extend the life of existing diesel freight trucks, a different 
proposition than the fully electric heavy-duty trucks on sale from 
manufacturers including Tesla Inc. and Volvo AB. Sales of those vehicles in the 
US in the first half of this year were lower than the same period last year 
despite higher diesel prices, according to BloombergNEF. Sales may increase in 
the second half with Tesla reporting a pipeline of 1,200 orders in May, the 
researcher said in a June report, citingdata 
<https://californiahvip.org/#voucher-map> from a California voucher program. 

The new entrants are going after a freight truck market with revenue of about 
$900 billion in 2024, according to theAmerican Trucking Association 
<https://www.trucking.org/economics-and-industry-data>. The 3 million registered
 <https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/policyinformation/statistics/2023/pdf/vm1.pdf> 
heavy-duty trucks in the US together drive about 60,000 miles a year, 
generating roughly 300 million tons of carbon dioxide, or about 6% of US annual 
emissions, according to data compiled by Bloomberg. Fuel makes up more than 
one-fifth of owners’ operating costs.

Carbon exploration

By Antony Sguazzin 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/ABwnnzA9x6k/antony-sguazzin?cmpid=BBD073026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7159>
 andLaura Millan 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD073026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7159>

The Congo Basin is the world’s biggest tropical storehouse of carbon, its 
forest stretching almost across the African continent and covering an area 
larger than India.

Yet little is known about it.

Now a group of almost 200 scientists, who collectively make up the Science 
Panel for the Congo Basin <https://www.spcongobasin.org/>, is trying to change 
that. The panel, which launched its first report in Brazzaville in the Congo 
Republic in May, aims to secure funding to train 1,000 PhDs over the next 
decade.
A road runs through a dense area of primary forest in Nyanga, Gabon, in 2022.
Photographer: Guillem Sartorio/Bloomberg
Ramping up research is key to understanding and protecting the expanse, which 
dictates the weather patterns of a continent, is home to a fifth of the world’s 
living species and absorbs as much CO2 as Germany emits annually, experts say. 
The forest is at risk from slash-and-burn agriculture, where farmers set fires 
to make room for crops, as well as logging, mining anddemand for charcoal 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-11-01/video-the-race-to-replace-charcoal-africa-s-forest-killing-fuel?cmpid=BBD073026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7159>
, used by growing populations for cooking.

“We need an investment in better understanding the Congo basin and in creating 
a generation of Congo Basin scientists who will be the ones to defend the 
ecosystem,” said the panel’s Special Envoy Lee White, a UK-born zoologist who 
has served as Gabon’s environment minister. “I’m convinced that if we lose the 
Congo basin, Africa is not viable.”

Sunshine State’s solar surprise

By Zahra Hirji 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVypnhGtqak/zahra-hirji?cmpid=BBD073026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7159>

While the residential solar market is collapsing nationwide 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-15/us-residential-solar-installations-set-to-stall-for-years?cmpid=BBD073026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7159>
 after President Donald Trump ended a popular tax credit in December, one state 
is proving to be a surprising exception: Florida.
A rooftop solar array on a Florida home. Source: Brilliant Harvest, LLC
A big reason why is a sweeping permitting law 
<https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2025/683/ByVersion> that took effect in 
the state last summer, experts said, with new rules aimed at making it both 
faster and cheaper to install new rooftop solar.

Florida installed more than 110 megawatts of home solar in the first three 
months of the year andBloombergNEF recently called it the fastest-growing state 
<https://www.bnef.com/insights/39693> for residential solar in the country.

And more is coming: In the first 13 weeks of the year, Florida led the nation 
with approved permits for at least 450 megawatts-worth of home solar projects — 
a fourfold increase compared to the same period in the previous two years, 
according todata <https://www.bnef.com/shorts/td9wugkjh6v400> compiled by 
BloombergNEF and Construction Monitor. In other states, meanwhile, the number 
of applications was lower than in prior years.

“We’re doing better than I had braced for and expected as an industry in 
Florida with the tax credit going away,” said Jacob Traverso, director of 
operations at the Florida-based contractorSolar Source 
<https://www.solarsource.com/about>.

New fires erupt in Europe

By Joe Wertz 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXOtj9qZPQc/joe-wertz?cmpid=BBD073026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7159>
 andPaul Tugwell 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AOXQCpopcGQ/paul-tugwell?cmpid=BBD073026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7159>

Europe’s wildfire threat is shifting as new blazes force evacuations from 
southeast England to the Greek island of Crete, while cooler weather helps 
firefighters gain ground in southwest France.

The continent’s fourth major heat wave this year is moving east and is set to 
fade Thursday in southwestern France, where fire crews have been battling 
blazes that forced more than 200,000 residents and tourists to evacuate near 
Bordeaux. Firefighters have made progress overnight controlling the fire, which 
has stabilized after burning about 420 square kilometers, an area four times 
the size of Paris.

The relief in southwest France is the latest fluctuation in a wildfire season 
that arrived early and accelerated quickly as successive heat waves baked 
vegetation into kindling across Europe. Climate change is increasing the risk 
of dangerous weather on the world’s fastest-warming continent, while forecasts 
for more hot, dry conditions in the weeks ahead raise the prospect the fire 
season stretching well into fall.
