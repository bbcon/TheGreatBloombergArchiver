Maybe 6.5% Is the New 5%. Or 7%

When does middle age start? If you’d asked me in my teens, I would have said 
my 30th birthday. When that date arrived, I revised my opinion with the benefit 
of extra years and said 50. Now, having just passed 60, I will reluctantly 
concede that middle age is here. It arrived a few months ago.

I don’t think I’m alone in my attitude to aging, a great and unavoidable fact 
of our humanity which we we all have to deal with, or not deal with, in our own 
way. As a rathercruel parody 
<https://www.wattpad.com/272052850-second-wind-old-generation?utm_source=mobileweb&utm_medium=reading_cta&utm_content=mobileweb&wp_page=story-reading>
 of The Who’sMy Generation <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lJW_2wLt704> has it:
People try to put is down
Just because we’re still around.
No wonder Pete Townshend, now in his 80s, hates playing the song 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QaAI9K_Knf8>. And it seems the same psychology 
is at work among asset allocators as they deal with another fact of life that 
seems just as inevitable — the inexorable rise in longer US Treasury yields.

For months, we have lived with the widespread and unquantified belief that 
5.0% on the 10-year Treasury yield 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-13/a-5-treasury-yield-is-raising-new-risks-for-markets-economy?cmpid=BBD101426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10007>
 would be the landmark, ortipping point 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-24/us-treasury-yields-hit-5-as-wall-street-faces-new-rate-regime?cmpid=BBD101426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10007>
, when the stock market had no choice but to take notice. That, after all, is 
what happened when the10-year crashed through 5% to trigger the financial 
crisis in 2007 <https://www.ft.com/content/8ea6e6f2-1cfb-11dc-9b58-000b5df10621>
. Now, it’s been and gone, like a 30th birthday, and equity investors are still 
partying like a group of twentysomethings.

At what point will they have to take rising 10-year yields 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-09-15/markets-blinked-at-5-yields-they-ll-be-back?cmpid=BBD101426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10007>
 seriously? The latest Bank of America survey of global fund managers asked the 
question, and found that 64% think it won’t come until 6%, with many thinking 
it will need to go well beyond that:

If we can wait that long, that would help explain the calm emanating from 
stock markets. Even more importantly, a majority of fund managers are confident 
that 6% yields will never happen (at least not in this cycle). A number of 
redoubtable bond market bears have said in recent weeks that they’re shifting 
toward positivity, withPimco’s Daniel Ivascyn trimming a long-standing 
underweight in Treasuries 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-22/pimco-trims-underweight-on-long-us-bonds-as-yields-top-5?cmpid=BBD101426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10007>
, while BlackRock’sJean Boivin and Rick Rieder have turned bullish on 
shorter-term bonds 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/blackrock-turns-bullish-on-short-term-bonds-downgrades-stocks?cmpid=BBD101426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10007>
. The broader mass of fund managers seems to agree with them. A slim majority 
once more expects longer-term rates to reduce from here, not rise, a state of 
affairs seldom seen in this century:

Abetting this is a growing sense that the angst about hawkishness at the 
Federal Reserve, which set in in a big way after Chairman Kevin Warsh’sspeech 
at Jackson Hole 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-08-28/jackson-hole-federal-reserve-chair-kevin-warsh-must-raise-rates?cmpid=BBD101426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10007>
 in August, has been badly overdone. When the New York Fed PresidentJohn 
Williams 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/fed-s-williams-currently-sees-no-need-for-rate-hike-in-2027?cmpid=BBD101426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10007>
 told Bloomberg on Wednesday that thereneedn’t be any rate hikes next year 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/fed-s-williams-interview-with-bloomberg-news-full-transcript?cmpid=BBD101426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10007>
, he confirmed a gentle easing in expectations embodied in the futures market. 
Further calming would bring that much more support to equities, confirming that 
surviving the date with destiny at 5% was all the mattered:

The other big impulse continues to be tech sector earnings which, with all due 
deference to the big banks who have just reported, now dwarf all others in 
importance. Ajay Rajadhyaksha of Barclays lays out how important semiconductors 
alone have become, accounting for almost a fifth of the S&P 500’s market cap, 
and more than a third of aggregate earnings growth:

Thankfully, just as fund managers are braced for bond yields to start 
declining, they’re also positioned for this incredible growth to continue. 
Earnings expectations for the SOX semiconductor index, as measured by 
Bloomberg, have done nothing but rise:

Arguably, the key will be capital expenditures. A few companies are spending 
enormous sums of money and have separated themselves from all others, as shown 
in this remarkable chart, drawn with the aid of Bloomberg’s AskB AI tool:

If this extraordinary phenomenon continues, then fund managers’ optimism will 
have been vindicated. But it’s asking a lot. Indeed, putting off the moment 
when share prices at last react to the tectonic shift in bonds looks barely any 
less far-fetched than an attempt to postpone the onset of middle age 
permanently.

Banking Blues

Wall Street banks were hoping to redeem themselves after underperforming the 
broader market. But they were disappointed as the relativelystrong earnings 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/bond-traders-eclipsed-by-equities-in-rare-wall-street-reversal?cmpid=BBD101426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10007>
 they announced on Tuesday failed to boost their share prices. Ahead of this 
week, the group was down 6% since the second quarter, even as the broader 
markets rose 4.6%. Apart from JPMorgan, which reported earnings below 
estimates, Citigroup, Goldman Sachs, and Wells Fargo all either met or beat 
estimates. It made little difference for investors:

Why so bad? We still await a full set of results from all six major banks — 
Bank of America and Morgan Stanley are set to report within hours of your 
receiving this — for the clearest take from investors, but the KBW Bank Index’s 
lackluster performance is already hard to ignore:

It wasn’t as though these results were without their bright spots. JPMorgan, 
Citigroup and Wells Fargo reported a combined $56.5 billion in net interest 
income for the third quarter, a fifth straight quarterly record for the profits 
earned from lending, banks’ traditional engine:

The Fed’s return to tighter monetary policy should boost net interest income 
further unless it goes so far as to dissuade customers from borrowing. JPMorgan 
is guiding to expect higher interest income next year, which suggests 
confidence in continuing customer appetite. The other risk is that the Fed 
forces theyield curve into inversion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2022-12-06/what-the-inverted-yield-curve-says-about-the-next-recession?cmpid=BBD101426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10007>
 (so longer bonds yield less than short-term ones), which is a classic sign of 
recession but also makes it far harder for banks to make money from lending.

A look at banks’ relative performance on the stock market suggests that a 
flattening yield curve over the last few months as bonds have sold off has 
soured investors:

The greater problems came in trading, an increasingly important business for 
the traditional leading banks. They suffered a broad-based shortfall in 
fixed-income, currencies and commodities revenue compared to estimates. Of the 
four that reported, only Citi topped analysts’ estimates, while JPMorgan’s fell 
4% year-on-year, the first decline in over two years.

Bloomberg Intelligence’s Herman Chan says that falling fixed-income trading 
revenue might reflect the higher-for-longer rate outlook.

For now, the takeaway from the fitful re-steepening of the yield curve amid 
the bond market ructions is that stock markets no longer necessarily buy into 
the assumption that higher rates will benefit banks. Rather, they appear to be 
weighing potential higher rates against the risks they pose to bank balance 
sheets. Perhaps this helps explain why optimistic 2007 net interest income 
forecasts haven’t helped banks’ share prices much.

Meanwhile, equities trading desks brought in a record haul, making up for the 
disappointment. The combination of market volatility and AI-driven equity 
issuance continues to create a great environment for them to generate revenues. 
Goldman’s recorded a 72% revenue increase over the previous quarter, leading 
peers and topping estimates. JPMorgan’s revenue rose almost 50%, with notable 
outperformance in prime and derivatives. Citigroup came in with a 14% increase, 
falling below analyst estimates.

They’re not the only ones glad that the equity bull run is continuing as high 
yields make life harder — and hoping very much that it can continue.

—Richard Abbey

Survival Tips

It was very sad to read today of the death by suicide of RJ Mitte 
<https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/13/arts/television/rj-mitte-death-reactions-bryan-cranston.html>
, at the age of 34. He was famous for playing Walter White Jr., son of one of 
the greatest anti-heroes in modern drama, inBreaking Bad. Mitte, like his 
character in the show,suffered from cerebral palsy 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cc-cbcJVw00>, and his relationship with his 
parents as his father moves ever deeper into a life of extreme crime was always 
beautifully and movingly rendered.

Breaking Bad 
<https://www.nytimes.com/video/arts/television/100000011062762/how-breaking-bad-became-the-best-show-of-the-21st-century.html>
 cametop of the poll 
<https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/arts/television/best-tv-shows-21st-century.html>
 in theNew York Times’ recent table of the best TV shows of this century 
<https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/27/podcasts/the-daily/the-best-tv-shows-of-the-21st-century.html>
, beatingThe Wire and Mad Men. When I asked readers which golden age drama I 
should binge while recovering from surgery a few years ago, it was far more 
widely recommended than evenThe Sopranos 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bzVeLjj6Ao8> (which started in 1999 and wasn’t 
eligible for the Times’ list). I haven’t regretted watching every episode; it’s 
beautiful and in its twisted way suffused with genius. If you haven’t 
binge-watched it yet, you should. Mitte, as you can see here 
<https://www.youtube.com/shorts/1VxjtZvaTro>, here 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H-oNnWl-opE> and here 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ds7frvE5tGo>, was a vital member of a 
remarkable ensemble. Rest in peace, RJ.
