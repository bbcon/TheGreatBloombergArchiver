Friday, September 25, 2026

US

  • US 30-year mortgage rates rose above 7% for the first time since January 2025, reflecting the broad Treasury selloff that pushed the 10-year yield to 5.18%–5.19%, a multi-decade high.
  • The dollar is on its best streak since March as markets price further Fed rate hikes to combat persistent above-target inflation.
  • Meta shares have surged 36% in September following the Muse app release, putting the stock within ~1% of a $2 trillion market cap.
  • Brightline, operator of the Miami-to-Orlando passenger rail corridor, filed for bankruptcy after failing to service its project debt.

Bond Market and Monetary Policy

The defining domestic financial story remains the ongoing Treasury selloff. The 10-year yield briefly touched 5.19% before retreating one basis point on news of potential US-Iran Strait of Hormuz negotiations, having risen more than 20 basis points over just the prior two sessions. Treasury Secretary Bessent has attributed the selloff primarily to elevated oil prices, arguing yields will normalize once the Iran conflict resolves. That framing is contested: structural forces — a $40 trillion federal debt load, persistent fiscal deficits, the AI-driven capex boom, and a Fed that has maintained a restrictive stance in response to inflation running above target — all appear to be contributing independently. More than half of respondents in a recent Markets Pulse survey expect 30-year yields to reach 6% before year-end, a level last seen during the dot-com bust. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate has crossed 7%, its highest since January 2025, compressing housing affordability further.

Equities and Corporate Developments

S&P 500 futures rose 0.3% as oil prices declined and Hormuz optimism lifted sentiment. Meta's 36% September gain is the stock's strongest monthly performance since 2013, driven by investor validation of the company's AI spending via the Muse app launch. The dollar's sustained strengthening streak adds a headwind for multinationals. Trump's economic advisers are studying a short-term diesel export ban, a development with meaningful implications for energy markets and refining margins. ---

Japan: Currency and Policy

The yen's move — USD/JPY from near multi-decade lows toward ¥158 — reflects a confluence of jawboning and a shifting interest rate differential narrative. Goldman Sachs has reversed its bearish yen stance, now forecasting appreciation to ¥150. The BOJ's accelerating price trend indicator is consistent with this view, suggesting underlying inflation dynamics may justify policy normalization sooner than previously expected. Markets remain alert to the risk of outright currency intervention.

China: Summit Outcomes and Trade

The Trump-Xi summit delivered more in symbolism than substance. The most consequential pre-announced development was a two-month extension of the bilateral trade truce — shorter than some had anticipated. No agreements on tariff reductions or AI communication channels materialized. Xi called explicitly for Trump to "oppose" Taiwan independence, language stronger than Washington's established position. Bloomberg Economics does not rule out further progress at prospective November and December summits. ANZ is overhauling its Hong Kong and Singapore banking operations, with at least 10 senior departures by October. ---

Europe

  • UK food export volumes fell 12% in H1 2026, the weakest first-half performance since the pandemic, as US tariffs weighed on agricultural producers.
  • UK consumer confidence barely rose in September, and Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey warned it is becoming harder to avoid a rate hike.
  • Dutch Prime Minister Jetten endorsed Klaas Knot as a strong ECB presidential candidate amid accelerating leadership turnover at the institution.

ECB Leadership Vacuum

Schnabel's early departure creates an immediate vacancy within the ECB Executive Board, intensifying pressure on eurozone governments to act on succession planning with fewer than three months before her exit. With President Lagarde and Chief Economist Lane both scheduled to depart in 2027 — and Lagarde having hinted at an earlier exit — a coordinated package deal on ECB leadership now appears increasingly probable. The institution faces this transition against a backdrop of elevated inflation, a global bond selloff, and unresolved fiscal fragmentation risks across the eurozone.

United Kingdom

The 12% drop in UK food export volumes in H1 represents a meaningful structural challenge for British producers, with US tariffs identified as the primary driver. Concurrently, food imports are near record highs, raising questions about supply-chain dependency. The Bank of England's position has grown more hawkish: Governor Bailey's comments that a rate hike is becoming difficult to avoid reflect persistent domestic inflation pressures even as growth remains fragile. Banks are actively exploiting cheap BOE repo financing to run profitable bond trades, a dynamic that bears monitoring from a financial stability perspective. ---

  • Argentina's President Milei downplayed concerns over slowing economic growth, expressing confidence about his 2027 re-election prospects despite approval ratings near their lowest levels.

Brazil: Electoral and Economic Backdrop

The electoral margin between Lula and Bolsonaro has narrowed materially following disclosures about Bolsonaro's ties to a jailed banker implicated in fraud. At 47% to 45% in a runoff scenario, the outcome remains genuinely uncertain. Simultaneously, the government has revised its 2026 growth forecast lower, introducing a complication for Lula's campaign narrative. Fiscal sustainability and central bank credibility will be central themes regardless of the election outcome.

Regional Energy Investment

South America is emerging as the oil industry's preferred exploration destination as geopolitical disruption in the Middle East and structural demand growth incentivize reserve replenishment. Chevron has expanded its Brazilian resource footprint roughly sixfold over two years. ExxonMobil cited the Equatorial Margin as a priority. IEA upstream spending is projected at $546 billion in 2026, with South American growth partly offsetting Middle East and North American declines. Milei separately indicated that Falkland Island residents' rights would be respected in any hypothetical sovereignty arrangement — comments made at the Economic Club of New York that drew attention without shifting the bilateral diplomatic posture meaningfully. ---

Global

  • Brent crude fell toward $105/barrel and WTI to ~$93 (–1.6%) on reports that US and Iranian negotiators are exploring a phased deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Global government bond yields remain at multi-decade highs; the average yield on sovereign debt worldwide is near 4%, the highest since 2007.
  • US retail diesel prices exceeded $6.50/gallon for the first time, driven by refinery capacity losses in both the Middle East and Russia; Trump advisers are studying a short-term diesel export ban.
  • France pledged to send troops to defend Saudi oil infrastructure as Houthi forces claimed responsibility for ballistic missile strikes on Yanbu and Taif.

Strait of Hormuz and Energy Markets

The potential US-Iran framework being discussed on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly represents the most significant near-term catalyst for energy markets. A phased agreement reopening the Strait would materially relieve the oil price pressure that has been a primary driver of the global bond selloff. Treasury Secretary Bessent's thesis — that yields will decline once the Iran conflict resolves — would gain credibility under such a scenario. However, simultaneous Houthi attacks on Saudi oil infrastructure and France's decision to deploy troops to defend those facilities underscore that regional instability extends well beyond the bilateral US-Iran dimension. Any Hormuz deal, if achieved, remains fragile.

Global Bond Regime Shift

Beyond the energy shock, a structural argument for sustainably higher rates is gaining traction. The convergence of elevated sovereign debt loads, AI-driven capital expenditure, fiscal deficits across major economies, and central banks constrained by above-target inflation has pushed the global cost of capital to levels not seen in nearly two decades. Whether this represents a cyclical peak or a durable regime shift — as Vanguard and others now contend — remains genuinely uncertain, and the resolution of the Hormuz situation will be an important near-term test of that thesis.
