A contentious meeting

Renewed tensions in the Middle East, an AI-driven demand boom and new tariffs 
from the Trump administration are makingthis week’s Fed rate decision 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-26/fed-faces-growing-pressure-to-hike-rates-as-price-risks-rebound?cmpid=072726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=7011>
 a close — and contentious — call.

Soaring energy prices prompted investors to boost their bets on a rate hike on 
Wednesday to about to 40% at one point last week.As it stands now, bond traders 
are pricing in roughlya one-in-three chance of an increase 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-26/bond-traders-on-edge-as-risks-of-fed-rate-hike-this-week-mount?cmpid=072726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=7011>
. The yield on the 10-year US Treasury note stood Friday at the highest since 
January 2025 before today’s pullback.

“Roughly one third of the banks we work with are positioning for further rate 
increases, while the rest are hedging against cuts,” said Pradeep Bhatia of 
Derivative Path, which helps financial institutions hedge rates and currency 
risks. “That kind of bifurcation tells you the market has stopped trying to 
predict the Fed and started preparing for both outcomes.”

As well as muddying the rates picture, the surge in oil prices has also 
wreaked havoc with the so-called Trump Trade 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-25/the-trump-trade-is-turning-into-a-loser-in-the-stock-market?cmpid=072726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=7011>
.

Ned Davis Research’s Trump Trade Index — which consists of a dozen 
exchange-traded funds expected to benefit from White House policies on 
homebuilding, defense spending and the re-shoring of manufacturing — has 
slumped about 16% since May after clobbering the S&P 500 at the beginning of 
the year.

“All this is tied to the Iran war and inflation,” said Pat Tschosik of Ned 
Davis Research. “Between some sort of tariff, or war, or supply chain 
disruption, could we just go three months without some sort of supply shock?” —

The week ahead

Central bankers outside Washington are also set to reveal just how worried 
they are about a return of oil to about $100 a barrel.

Three days of Group of Seven interest rate decisions, starting with the Fed on 
Wednesday, followed by the Bank of England and Bank of Japan, may show varying 
degrees of vigilance at the prospect of more energy-driven inflation, even if 
none is predicted to act on that for now.

Together with the ECB’s signal of its readiness to hike interest rates again, 
investor bets point to possible moves as soon as September within much of that 
club, even if economists are less sure.

There’s plenty more news to preoccupy traders this week, especially in the 
stock market, with earnings due from hundreds of big companies including Apple, 
Coca-Cola, Microsoft, Starbucks, ExxonMobil and Meta.

Confused by the Greeks?

Investors will soon have another way to bet on the hottest stocks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-26/traders-are-getting-a-new-tool-to-wager-on-the-biggest-us-stocks?cmpid=072726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=7011>
 from Nvidia to SpaceX, without trading a single share.

CME Group will launch single-stock futures Monday, allowing investors to hedge 
or speculate on more than 50 of the largest US companies. 

Single-stock futures failed to gain traction in the US after their first 
launch 24 years ago. But CME, the world’s largest derivatives exchange, is 
betting the rise of retail trading and today’s environment of hot IPOs with 
limited share availability will make them a success this time around.

Unlike options, single-stock futures provide leveraged exposure without 
requiring the knowledge of concepts like theta, gamma and delta — the Greek 
letters that represent how factors such as time or stock prices affect the 
derivatives’ pricing.

Their simplicity over options should make them appealing to retail investors, 
according to Martin Franchi of NinjaTrader, a futures broker.

“There could be someone who’s perhaps too confused by all the Greeks and 
everything, and says this is just a simpler way,” he said. “With the retail 
participation in markets today, a lot of things are just going to be different 
about single-stock futures this time.” —Bernard Goyder 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXSztklFffk/bernard-goyder?cmpid=072726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=7011>
