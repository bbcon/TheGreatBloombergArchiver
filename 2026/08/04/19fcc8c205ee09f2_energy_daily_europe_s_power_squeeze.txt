Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Will Mathis looks at the trouble low river levels spell for Europe’s 
energy system.

It’s only the start of August and Europe’s waterways are already so hot and 
dry that the energy system is starting to crack.

Throw a dart at a map of the continent and it’s likely you’ll hit a point of 
water-related stress.

In Germany, the Rhine is at its lowest level on record 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-04/rhine-set-for-more-disruption-after-hitting-lowest-on-record?cmpid=BBBXT080426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260804&utm_content=7330>
. In France, nuclear power plants are shutting down at a record pace due to 
high river temperatures. In Scandinavia and the Alps, hydro reserves are 
unusually depleted.

The unprecedented strain on power supply 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-01/europe-s-endless-heat-waves-imperil-once-reliable-power-sources?cmpid=BBBXT080426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260804&utm_content=7330>
 comes as record-setting heat drives demand for air conditioning. Prices have 
been surging in the evenings when solar panel output starts to wane.

One of the most extreme examples this week came from Hungary, where prices 
rose over €500 a megawatt-hour after extremely low Danube River levels forced 
the country to turn off thePaks nuclear power plant 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-01/hungary-heads-to-nuclear-shutdown-in-test-for-economy-and-magyar?cmpid=BBBXT080426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260804&utm_content=7330>
 over the weekend.
Visitors walk across an exposed section of riverbed during low water levels on 
the Danube River in Rasova, Romania.Photographer: Andrei Pungovschi/Bloomberg
Things could still get worse. Historical figures show the Danube reaches its 
annual low point in August or September, according to Laszlo Nagy, the deputy 
director of the Paks plant, who added that it takes about a week to bring 
reactors online once water levels become adequate again.

In the Nordics, anything short of a historic amount of precipitation for the 
rest of the year will leave hydro stocks well below normal heading into winter. 
That would give Europe fewer fallback options for power generation at a time 
when natural gas stocks are also exceptionally low and prices highly volatile 
due to disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz.

Longer term, Europe may have to get used to summers of stress. Climate change 
has turned the region into the fastest-warming continent, and even with the 
rapid rise of renewable power and electric vehicles, demand for fossil fuels 
globally continues to grow.

“We have to plan our power system so that this might be our new reality,” said 
Zsuzsanna Pató, power system lead for Europe at the non-profit Regulatory 
Assistance Project. “We don’t usually have such hot spells, but we just have to 
get used to it.”

—Will Mathis, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Monthly liquefied natural gas imports into northwest Europe, Italy and Spain 
reached 4.9 million metric tons in July, down 34% from a year earlier and 
continuing a downward trend since April, according to aBloombergNEF 
<bbg://news/stories/TJ8MSWKIJH8G> analysis. Higher prices linked to the US-Iran 
war have intensified competition for cargoes, and Europe will likely need to 
increase LNG imports in the coming months to prepare for winter, even as 
uncertainty over the conflict persists.
