Selling Treasuries

On the margins, Treasury bonds are looking less attractive to some long-term 
holders. The evidence: Norway’s sovereign wealth fund is proposing tocut its 
holdings of government debt 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/norway-wealth-fund-proposes-trimming-government-bond-holdings?cmpid=090426_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260904&utm_content=8499>
, a move that could affect US securities the most.

The move revives memories from January, when the $31.5 trillion Treasury bond 
market wasabuzz with the idea 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-19/-weaponizing-10-trillion-of-us-assets-is-tough-ask-for-europe?cmpid=090426_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260904&utm_content=8499>
 that foreign pension funds were on the verge ofreducing their holdings of US 
debt 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-20/danish-pension-fund-akademikerpension-to-exit-us-treasuries?cmpid=090426_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260904&utm_content=8499>
. The thinking was that Donald Trump’s policies — from threatening to take over 
Greenland to trade disputes to profligate spending — were creating too much 
risk.

As it turns out, there was no broad selloff of Treasuries. But bond yields 
have surged anyway, pushed up by inflation, the size of the debt and deficits 
and a flood of corporate securities.

The proposal by Norges Bank Investment Management would imply a decrease of 
about $75 billion in Treasury holdings, according to Bloomberg calculations. 
The fund said it wants to invest in other, higher-yielding securities.

“NBIM isn’t making a direct call on US fiscal sustainability,” said Kenneth 
Crompton at National Australia Bank. “They’re arguing that they already own 
enough government bonds to satisfy liquidity needs, and that a long-horizon 
investor should harvest a broader set of fixed-income risk premia.” —Phil 
Serafino

Taking a shine to gold

Some of the world’s biggest money managers have rebuilt their gold holdings 
after prices dropped 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/world-s-biggest-money-managers-are-rebuilding-gold-positions?cmpid=090426_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260904&utm_content=8499>
, betting that long-term drivers of the precious metal will endure even as the 
Fed takes a more assertive stance on inflation.

Asset manager Amundi, for example, bought bullion on the expectation it will 
return to $5,000 an ounce by year-end.

“Gold is an asset that we consider to be cheap, a good hedge and reasonably 
liquid,” said Amundi’s Lorenzo Portelli. Greater visibility over the Fed’s 
interest-rate path would be needed, he said, before the firm would consider 
adding to last month’s purchases.

Expectations for higher rates are undermining support for bullion, an asset 
that tends to be less favored when yields rise, because it doesn’t pay 
interest. —Yihui Xie 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWVUEW611s0/yihui-xie?cmpid=090426_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260904&utm_content=8499>
