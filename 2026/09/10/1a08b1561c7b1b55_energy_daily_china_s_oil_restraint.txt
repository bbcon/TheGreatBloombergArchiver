Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Rong Wei Neo considers how Chinese buyers will respond to the latest 
surge in crude.

Oil is back above $100 a barrel 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-sept-9?cmpid=BBBXT091026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8745>
 and buyers are asking again whether China will take action that would restrain 
soaring prices.

For now at least, Beijing is doing the opposite.

Brent has surged on the latest escalation in fighting in the Persian Gulf, 
just as many industry heavyweights were in Singapore for a major conference. 
That added urgency to conversations that were already dominated by the US-Iran 
war and the Strait of Hormuz.

One question kept coming up — will Chinese buyers step back from the market and
slash imports 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-26/china-s-collapsing-crude-oil-imports-to-fall-further-in-june?cmpid=BBBXT091026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8745>
 as they did when crude prices soared early in the conflict?

That would mark a reversal from recent weeks, in which China’s refiners have 
made a notable return to the global oil market. They are processing greater 
volumes of crude at home and buying cargoes everywhere fromRussia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-10/asking-price-for-russian-oil-soars-as-china-runs-out-of-options?cmpid=BBBXT091026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8745>
 to Argentina, undeterred bysoaring premiums 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-07/china-oil-demand-revival-spurs-price-spikes-from-congo-to-brazil?cmpid=BBBXT091026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8745>
 for some grades.
A Sinopec service station in Shanghai.Photographer: Qilai Shen/Bloomberg
Despite the hopes of oil consumers and central bankers, Beijing may not be 
able to restrain prices by disappearing from the market a second time.

The refiners that cut their run rates and drew down inventories earlier in the 
year aren’t able to do so indefinitely. It’s also unclear whether Chinese 
authorities would be willing to reimpose curbs on fuel exports, which 
previously played such a big role in reducing the country’s crude imports.

There’s little doubt that some Chinese oil demand has been permanently lost. 
Sinopec’s research arm said at the conference that itexpects consumption to fall
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-09/china-oil-demand-to-shrink-this-year-sinopec-research-arm-says?cmpid=BBBXT091026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260910&utm_content=8745>
 by 600,000 barrels a day this year, partly due to high prices encouraging 
consumers to switch to electric vehicles or public transport.

But that represents an acceleration of the country’s gradual move away from 
fossil fuels, not the kind of drastic demand reduction that can offset a supply 
disruption on the scale of the closure of the Strait of Hormuz.

—Rong Wei Neo, Bloomberg News
