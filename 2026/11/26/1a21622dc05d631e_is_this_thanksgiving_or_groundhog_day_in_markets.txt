Groundhog Thanksgiving

Is this Thanksgiving or Groundhog Day? Americans signed off for their annual 
autumn festival by staging another massive selloff, just like the ones on 
Tuesday, and last Friday and Thursday. Then before they left to eat turkey, 
they staged a bounce that felt a lot like the rapturous Monday reception of the 
news that Jensen Huang had been reinstated as CEO of Nvidia.

There were no developments on a par with that jaw-dropping turn of events, but 
there was economic news that, in the current context, many found unwelcome.

The latest reading for US gross domestic product found it growing at an 
annualized rate of more than 4% in the third quarter. That’s one of the best 
showings in the last three decades (the wild fluctuations caused by the 
pandemic stoppage are excluded from the chart below for legibility):

Now for the bad part. This was unwelcome because the market’s difficulties 
have been driven by liquidity drying up, and there’s a widespread tendency to 
blame the Federal Reserve. Yet growth like this makes it surpassingly hard to 
argue that interest rates are too high.

More problematic was the PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditure) version of 
inflation, compiled as part of the GDP calculations, which strongly 
contradicted the popular argument that current stickiness is a direct byproduct 
of the oil price. Jerome Powell, as chairman, used to emphasise “supercore” 
inflation (services excluding housing) as a measure of underlying pricing 
pressures. Oil has no direct effect on this — and yet the PCE confirmed the 
earlier Consumer Price Index finding that supercore is above the Fed’s zone of 
tolerance:

The central bank can’t back down in such circumstances. A rate hike when the 
Federal Open Market Committee meets next month is priced in by fed funds 
futures as a virtual certainty. Exceptional volatility in rate expectations 
over the next year continues. In the immediate wake of the midterms, when the 
stock market sold off, futures priced in only one hike next year. They now 
expect more than two — which is still sharply reduced from the rates scare at 
the end of September when traders were braced for a protracted tightening cycle:

Does this make sense? Carl Weinberg of High Frequency Economics argued:
If you are wondering if economic growth is strong enough to convince the FOMC 
that the economy can withstand one or more increases in policy interest rates, 
you need not read any further: It can, and this report confirms that assessment.
But what of markets? A number of commentators continue to push aggressively 
for the Fed to send some clear signal that it wouldn’t go too far — not unlike 
the “Powell Pivot” that followed a similar market break eight years ago.Robin 
Brooks of the Brookings Institution argued 
<https://robinjbrooks.substack.com/p/we-are-heading-for-full-meltdown?utm_source=post-email-title&publication_id=4981217&post_id=226527041&utm_campaign=email-post-title&isFreemail=true&r=1tnx7&triedRedirect=true&utm_medium=email>
:
Markets are heading for full meltdown 
<https://substack.com/redirect/68bb71c4-783e-42de-be76-f8b7de610dc1?j=eyJ1IjoiMXRueDcifQ.dl-62i96ZXezSAfUKMASRioe9qpjzHuIpL-3C_eAPNY>
 because the Fed is hiking into falling oil prices, a policyerror 
<https://robinjbrooks.substack.com/p/the-fed-is-making-a-terrible-mistake> 
that’s giving us a super-charged rise in real rates... The Fed’s blackout 
period ahead of the Dec. 9 meeting starts this Saturday. It seems increasingly 
likely that we go into this without a dovish pivot, in which case the wheels 
might really start coming off in markets. If that’s where we’re headed, we 
might well end up with no hike on Dec. 9 as markets go full meltdown.
He pointed out that the continuing fall of risk assets following Huang’s 
return suggested that the market’s problems could be attributed to overtight 
money. That argument was weakened somewhat after markets staged their big 
reversal. Gold and Bitcoin, both liquidity-sensitive, staged a dive but ended 
up with gains for the day. Stocks made up nearly all their losses, and the 
10-year Treasury yield, having dipped to 4.8% as investors gobbled up bonds, 
rebounded to 4.86%. It was anexceptionally difficult day 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-25/stomach-churning-swings-rock-wall-street-as-ai-doubts-creep-in?cmpid=BBD112626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11871>
:

Was this the moment when the market made a bottom from which it can now 
advance? While dramatic, that’s still not clear. Evidently, there were buyers 
at the prices reached amid the Wednesday morning selloff. But the SOX 
semiconductors index remains 17% down since news broke of Huang’s initial 
dismissal. At the worst on Wednesday, it was down 22%, but that doesn’t yet 
mean it’s out of the woods. Nvidia’s continuing travails demonstrate problems 
stretching far beyond the extraordinary soap opera around its CEO:

As for longer-term trends, the dominant trade of the last four years has been 
to buy technology stocks and sell duration — long-dated bonds, which fare worse 
when the economy is growing strongly. There have been only two significant 
interruptions in that trend.

This is the third, and the Huang reinstatement was intended as the “put” that 
would draw a line under it. Even after Wednesday’s rebound, the trend hasn’t 
been regained. Nor has there been the immediate impact that followedMarch’s 
announcements of peace feelers in the Iran war, 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-04-01/iran-oil-four-passover-questions-as-the-war-s-endgame-nears?cmpid=BBD112626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11871>
 nor of President DonaldTrump’s pause on his Liberation Day tariffs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2025-04-10/bully-or-chicken-playing-trump-with-game-theory?cmpid=BBD112626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11871>
. It’s tempting to say that the bond market has worked its magic again — spikes 
in long-term yields have a long history of dislodging other markets as 
investors wonder whether prices really make sense once the cost of long-term 
money has risen. Since September’s“bondpocalypse,” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-09-29/what-s-going-to-break-in-the-bondpocalypse?cmpid=BBD112626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11871>
 when the 10-year Treasury yield topped 5.25%, certainties have been shattered.

For the longer term, earnings are the key. The biggest story of 2026 has been 
the extraordinary growth in tech groups’ profits (which in itself suggests that 
monetary conditions cannot be too tight at the moment).

Those estimates have ceased to accelerate, but they’re not (yet) coming down. 
Can we rely on that to continue? Comparing the valuations that the market is 
currently putting on the past year’s Nasdaq 100 earnings per share 
(conventional trailing price/earnings ratio) and those for the next 12 months 
(the forward p/e) suggests that there must be some anxiety over this. The 
forward multiple looks very, very cheap:

About two months remain before the next download of earnings results. The 
selloff makes little sense unless there are serious doubts about current 
forecasts. If companies don’t start to guide brokers a little lower in the 
coming weeks, such valuations should begin to tempt more buyers out of the 
woodwork.

Reduced forecasts might also increase the chances of aid from a more lenient 
Fed. To quote David Bowers of Absolute Strategy Research:
Strong corporate earnings are often inflationary because they involve higher 
margins. Central banks need to address core inflation, there’s a tightening 
going on, and I don’t think a falling Nasdaq is necessarily going to change 
that.
As it stands, this selloff isn’t deep enough on its own to have macroeconomic 
effects and justify central bank intervention. The collapsing internet bubble 
in 2000 didn’t deter the Fed from hiking rates — although they cut to zero 
during the selloff of 2008. Bowers argued: “We need to see that this [is] a 
financial crisis rather than just taking froth off the AI bubble. We need to 
see that it’s 2008, not 2000.”

Financing structures around AI remain opaque and circular, with Nvidia as a 
critical linchpin. It’s possible that such problems will emerge. Unless they 
do, it will be hard for the Fed to ease with inflation so far above target.

Chris Watling of Longview Economics argued that so long as the Fed stays 
relatively resolute, that gives confidence on long-term inflation expectations 
and allows 10-year yields to come down. He described November’s volatility as 
“typical for a mid-cycle correction... We’ve been building towards this and you 
typically get a lot of sideways volatility after a chop like this.”

He predicted that the market would continue to try to bully the Fed, but 
Wednesday’s recovery for risk assets had reduced pressure on the central bank 
for now.

There will probably be a few more Groundhog Days before this volatility is 
over. If confidence in tech earnings remains intact, there should be buyers 
before long. And much more aggressive selling would be needed before the Fed is 
inspired to come to the rescue.

And now off to eat turkey.
