A week can feel like a long time in geopolitics these days. But every so often 
we should remind ourselves that even in our short-attention-span world, it’s 
the enduring trends that really matter when it comes to power.

It’s summer and so it’s natural to look to baseball storylines for 
perspective. My resurgent Boston Red Sox offer an example.

Over the weekend, a nine-game win streak came to an end. It followed an 
earlier 15-game unbeaten run, matching the franchise record set by the 1946 
team. Since late June the team and its scrappy roster of young players and 
overlooked veterans has been a surprise behemoth. And yet thanks to a woeful 
start to the 162-game season, that run was good enough only to give them the 
seventh best record in baseball by the end of play on Sunday.

Iran Latest: Trump Hints US Will Let Economic Pressure on Iran Do the Work 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-10/trump-hints-us-will-let-economic-pressure-on-iran-do-the-work?cmpid=BBD081026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7497>

The story isn’t over. A few weeks of summer joy is welcome. But fans’ feelings 
will look a lot different if the Red Sox can’t deliver in October when 
championships are won.

Which brings us to American industrial might and geopolitics.

Few sectors have drawn more political attention over the past decade than 
American manufacturing. So last week when the Institute of Supply Management 
released itsmonthly report 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/us-manufacturing-activity-expands-at-strongest-pace-since-2022?cmpid=BBD081026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7497>
 showing the best month in four years in July, the signal drawn by many was 
that it offered proof of an industrial resurgence. An increase of 5,000 in 
factory employment in Friday’s otherwise weak jobs report seemed to confirm 
that narrative.

We often forget what data actually shows us, however. The ISM’s monthly survey 
measures short-term sentiment. It tracks how purchasing managers feel about 
this month versus the one before. Which means that if this month is marginally 
better than a lousy one before it, the index points up.

Until January of this year, the ISM’s manufacturing index had spent almost 
three years virtually exclusively in contraction territory. What the ISM is 
really showing so far this year is a short-term bounce in sentiment from low 
levels.

But it’s where things go in the longer term that really matters in 
geopolitics. And that’s where fixating on short-term data can mask the real 
effects of policies and the direction of power.

Is American manufacturing in a better place to compete with China and others 
than it was a year ago? Or a decade ago when Trump was first running for the 
presidency?

Coming Up: US CPI to Show Inflationary Pressures Cooling Some 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-08/us-cpi-to-show-inflationary-pressures-cooling-some?cmpid=BBD081026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7497>

That, of course, depends on where you look. Making brass instruments 
<https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/08/01/conn-selmer-factory-closes-ohio-despite-trumps-manufacturing-push>
 in Ohio is not a growth industry. Firms producing essential gas turbines amid a
data center boom 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-22/ge-vernova-shares-climb-on-strong-orders-power-demand-boom?cmpid=BBD081026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7497>
 are doing very well, however.

Overall, manufacturing employment of 12.6 million people in July was down by a 
net 62,000 from January last year when President Donald Trump returned to 
power. It is up by almost 270,000 people if you go back to July 2016 when he 
was first running for president. But, again, it depends on where you look. 
There are 25,000 fewer people working infactories in Michigan 
<https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MIMFG> today than a decade ago. The longer 
view is grimmer still. There are almost five million fewer Americansworking in 
manufacturing <https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MANEMP> than when China 
joined the World Trade Organization in 2001.

The counter-argument once used by defenders of globalization and now the Trump 
administration is that industrial production and productivity are up. But US 
industrial production today is roughly where it stood in late 2007, according 
to theFederal Reserve index <https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/INDPRO> that 
tracks it.

Plus productivity doesn’t vote. Workers do. And having fewer people laboring 
harder isn’t a recipe for political success even if it’s good economics.

Yen Intervention

One of the biggest questions in the global economy today is whether short-term 
policies like tariffs and subsidies can shift long-term trends.

A week ago a unique US intervention to support the yen was driving discussions 
of American power. But can aninterventionist US Treasury 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-05/treasury-secretary-scott-bessent-draws-on-hedge-fund-tactics-to-help-rescue-yen?cmpid=BBD081026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7497>
 really fight off the effects of structural trends like Japan’s aging 
demographics?

Odd Lots Podcast: Brad Setser on the US’s Yen Intervention 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/audio/2026-08-06/odd-lots-brad-setser-on-the-us-s-yen-intervention-podcast?cmpid=BBD081026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7497>

Reality often looks different when you zoom out. In a study 
<https://www.piie.com/publications/policy-briefs/2026/made-china-global-supply-chains-and-limits-us-decoupling>
 released last week, economists Mary Lovely and Christine Wan at the Peterson 
Institute for International Economics found that tariffs and other policies had 
done very little to reduce US economic dependency on China between 2017 and 
2024 if you examine supply chains closely.

Even as Trump announces new investments 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-07/trump-touts-3-billion-in-minerals-projects-to-counter-china?cmpid=BBD081026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7497>
 in rare earth mines, there are major questions over how effective Washington’s 
policies will be at reducing China’s chokehold on the essential industrial 
inputs.

In a research note <> available to Bloomberg Terminal subscribers, 
geoeconomics analyst Chris Kennedy last week pointed out that hedge funds have 
started to bet against the US’s critical minerals push. Because at best, new 
mines will come online in the 2030s and even that timeline looks fragile.

Dirty; Low Margins

“Despite the all-of-government approach, the critical minerals industry is a 
difficult one,” Kennedy wrote. “It’s capital intensive and interest rates are 
rising. It’s dirty and takes years to bring new projects online,” he went on. 
“And it’s low margin — that is, if you can find customers.”

Read More: Trump Is Rewriting Corporate Norms With 211-Mile Arctic Road 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-31/alaska-road-permit-shows-how-trump-upends-business-norms?cmpid=BBD081026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7497>

Trump’s efforts to calm the relationship with China also aren’t helping. 
“Stability buys the US government and industry time to invest in 
diversification efforts, but it also reduces the urgency of those efforts,” 
Kennedy said.

It is healthy to live in the moment. Winning streaks — even those that merely 
retrace slumps — are good. Still, every so often we ought to remind ourselves 
that today’s victories may not be what they seem. Especially when it comes to 
geopolitics, economics and power. Oh, and baseball, too.
