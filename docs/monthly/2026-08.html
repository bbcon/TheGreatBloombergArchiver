Monthly Brief

August 2026

US

  • July payrolls contracted 23,000, sharply missing the +80,000 consensus — the third consecutive summer of employment weakness — driven by a 60,000 decline in local government employment, though initial jobless claims remained near historic lows.
  • July CPI landed exactly in line with consensus at core +0.2% MoM and +2.5% YoY, the slowest since March 2021, while core PCE tracked near 3.3% YoY — still a full percentage point above target heading into Jackson Hole.
  • The FOMC held at 3½–3¾% in a 9–3 vote on July 30, with Chair Warsh's press conference producing one of the sharpest post-meeting yield-curve steepenings in decades; 30-year Treasury yields hit 5.33% by mid-August, the highest since 2007.
  • US public debt topped $40 trillion for the first time; Treasury Secretary Bessent announced expanded buybacks of long-dated debt on August 21, pulling the 30-year down approximately 8bp but triggering a public rebuke from Stanley Druckenmiller.
  • Nvidia guided to roughly $108 billion in current-quarter revenue and approximately 70% fiscal 2028 revenue growth, well above the 45% consensus, adding $442 billion in market value on the announcement; the August composite PMI rose to 56, its highest since April 2022, beating consensus.

Labor Market and Consumer

July's payrolls miss was exacerbated by seasonal distortion and local government employment exhausting post-pandemic federal teaching assistance. The unemployment rate fell alongside the contraction because workers exited the labor force rather than found jobs: prime-age participation slipped to 83.4%, on track for its first annual decline since 2020. Initial claims near their lowest since 1969 confirm the problem is reduced hiring rather than elevated firing — a distinction that complicates the Fed's reaction function. The personal saving rate fell to 2.7% in June, less than half its level two years ago, raising structural questions about the durability of consumer expenditure once savings buffers are exhausted.

Federal Reserve and Monetary Policy

Warsh's July 30 press conference was the month's most consequential policy event. His failure to explain why three dissenting hike votes were overruled, or to articulate a clear reaction function, left markets without guidance at precisely the moment the data were most ambiguous. Goldman Sachs subsequently concluded a September hike is "very unlikely," citing softer retail sales, weaker employment, and slowing inflation, while the Cleveland Fed maintained a hawkish tone and markets assigned only 34% probability to another move this year. Warsh's August 29 Jackson Hole keynote — his first speaking entirely in his own voice — is the month's dominant unresolved policy question. The 10-year held near 4.68% after Bessent's buyback announcement, down modestly from 4.75%, though skeptics argue intervention cannot durably suppress yields absent genuine fiscal consolidation.

AI Earnings and Capex Debate

The month's dominant equity narrative was a meaningful shift in how markets evaluate AI capital expenditure. Microsoft's mid-July decision to cut full-year capex by $15 billion was rewarded with its largest single-day market cap gain since 2008. Meta's opposite trajectory — raising its capex floor to $130 billion as free cash flow fell 91% year-over-year — was punished. Nvidia's late-August guidance resolved, at least temporarily, whether AI infrastructure demand was fading: it was not. The S&P 500 reached a new all-time high during the month, with Q2 earnings growth of 47% providing fundamental support, though Barclays data show the top six technology groups now account for 8.4% of investment-grade duration-times-spread risk, exceeding the six largest banks. JPMorgan estimates $4.1 trillion of $5.5 trillion in projected AI capex will be debt-financed, making the buildout acutely sensitive to the Fed's path. ---

Asia

  • China's July CPI rose only 0.5% YoY — the weakest in six months and below the 1.0% prior month, missing expectations — while July activity data missed across the board: industrial output at 4.5% YoY, retail sales at 0.6%, and fixed-asset investment falling 6.7% YoY.
  • Japan's Q2 GDP grew an annualized 1.1%, missing the 2% consensus, as consumption flatlined; BOJ underlying inflation rose 2.3% YoY in July, above target and above consensus, cementing near-full market pricing of a September rate hike.
  • South Korea's Kospi fell as much as 39% from its June peak before surging 22% over 10 sessions and re-entering bull market territory, though it remained approximately 25% below its high — the most dramatic single-market dislocation of the period.
  • China's July exports climbed approximately 24% YoY, sharply above consensus, led by chip shipments up 117% and computer exports up 67%, contrasting starkly with the domestic demand picture.
  • The Bank of Japan spent a record $96.4 billion on yen intervention over the month; Japanese 30-year yields rose above 4% for the first time in 27 years, and the yen hovered near ¥159 per dollar, close to a 40-year low.

China: Two-Speed Economy

China's August data describe an economy operating at fundamentally different speeds across its external and domestic sectors. Export growth of approximately 24% YoY, driven by AI-related electronics, contrasts with CPI at 0.5%, services PMI of 50.4 — the softest in nearly two years — and broad-based July activity weakness that prompted BNP Paribas, Macquarie, and Pantheon economists to conclude growth is running below the lower end of Beijing's 4.5%–5% target. Premier Li Qiang called for intensified support measures and widened use of a $1.6 trillion housing provident fund, but the policy response has been measured rather than large-scale. Beijing appears to be threading a narrow path ahead of an October Communist Party meeting: supporting AI-related equities for strategic reasons while tightening compliance on the margin debt balance, which retreated from above 3 trillion yuan to 2.6 trillion yuan after brokerage restrictions were imposed. Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max release, built on 2.4 trillion parameters and ranking competitively against US frontier models, reinforces that Chinese AI development is narrowing the capability gap at materially lower computational cost.

Japan: Policy at an Inflection Point

Japan faces a genuinely difficult policy configuration. The yen near a 40-year low argues for rate increases; GDP growth missing consensus at an annualized 1.1% and household spending falling for a seventh consecutive month argue against tightening. The BOJ's July meeting summary flagged rising upside inflation risks, one board member raised the possibility of an accelerated tightening pace, and by late August overnight index swaps implied approximately 80% probability of a September 18 hike. A $52.8 billion coordinated US-Japan intervention in late July — the largest single-day operation on record — had surrendered nearly half its gains by early August, illustrating the structural limits of intervention against an unchanged rate differential. The Bessent-Takaichi rift adds political complexity: Treasury Secretary Bessent regards BOJ tightening as essential to yen stability, while Prime Minister Takaichi remains wary of moving too quickly given fragile consumption.

Regional Divergence

Southeast Asia's manufacturing PMI rose to 52.8 in July, its fastest expansion since the Hormuz conflict began, while Singapore upgraded its 2026 growth forecast to as high as 5.5% on AI-driven trade momentum. The Philippines, by contrast, faces the most acute macro tension in the region: Q2 GDP of 2.3% badly missed the 2.9% consensus and stands sharply below 5.4% a year earlier, while inflation at 6.2% runs more than double the government's target, forcing continued rate hikes and driving the peso past 62 per dollar to a record low. The Bank of Korea also hiked for a second consecutive meeting. South Korea's Kospi volatility — a 39% peak-to-trough decline followed by a 22% recovery — reflects the AI hardware cycle's instability: even with full-year earnings growth forecast at 220%, Samsung and SK Hynix's results missed elevated expectations and triggered severe forced selling in leveraged single-stock ETF products launched in May. ---

Europe

  • Eurozone GDP beat consensus in the July flash estimate, and Citi's European macro surprise index hit its highest level in over three years in August, supporting the case for continued equity outperformance.
  • UK CPI accelerated to 2.9% YoY in July — a four-month high, in line with consensus — while Spanish inflation surged to more than double the ECB's 2% target and French inflation quickened to its highest since May, pointing to euro-area CPI above 3% in aggregate.
  • ECB Executive Board member Isabel Schnabel warned rates must rise further, describing above-2% inflation as likely to persist for an "extended period"; markets fully priced a September hike to 2.5% by month-end.
  • UK Q2 GDP expanded 0.4% and first-half growth reached 1%, beating expectations; Germany's Q2 GDP was revised up to 0.3% QoQ from a preliminary 0.2%, with net exports the primary driver.
  • French 30-year borrowing costs climbed to 2008 levels and the France-Germany 10-year spread widened to its broadest since Mario Draghi's 2012 "whatever it takes" speech, while UK gilt yields approached 6%, their highest since 1998.

Growth and Monetary Policy

Europe entered August with more economic momentum than earlier forecasts suggested. The eurozone GDP beat provided a foundation; Germany's upward revision and a four-month streak of improving business sentiment pointed to a tentative industrial recovery, supported by June factory orders rising 3.1% month-on-month. Stoxx Europe 600 Q2 earnings growth reached 17%, the strongest in four years, with Germany, Italy, and France all at record highs. The August flash manufacturing PMI for the eurozone improved unexpectedly, led by its strongest expansion in more than four years. The ECB's policy path, however, leaves limited room for celebration: Schnabel's hawkish guidance and Kazaks's description of current inflation as "somewhat uncomfortable" confirm the September hike is effectively pre-committed. European natural gas prices are near five-month highs, with winter contracts more than double year-ago levels, and ECB Chief Economist Lane explicitly linked above-2% inflation to Iran-war energy costs — drawing a direct thread between the geopolitical conflict and the institution's tightening trajectory.

Sovereign Debt and Fiscal Stress

France is the epicenter of European bond-market stress. Lingering doubts about Paris's capacity to close its fiscal deficit — the product of a deadlocked legislature following Macron's 2024 snap election — against a presidential election less than a year away have driven the France-Germany spread to its widest since 2012. Germany's own borrowing costs have climbed toward levels last seen during the euro-area debt crisis. In the UK, a surprise £1.8 billion July budget deficit, rising corporate distress, and a NHS productivity gap that Treasury analysis found could be worth up to £32 billion annually present Chancellor Healey with a difficult October budget. Internal Treasury modeling released mid-August showed the economy would barely grow next year if Hormuz disruption persists through end-2026 — a sobering fiscal sensitivity that constrains any near-term easing of austerity.

Climate as Economic Risk

Extreme weather has emerged as a material and recurring European economic headwind in August. Rhine water levels at Kaub fell to their lowest since 1880 mid-month, stranding barge traffic and forcing chemical and steel producers to shift shipments to rail and road at higher cost — ING estimates severely restricted Rhine navigation reduces German industrial production by up to 1% in an affected month, directly threatening the translation of new orders into output. Drought was declared across almost three-quarters of England. French nuclear capacity was curtailed as river temperatures rose; Hungary's Paks facility shut temporarily after the Danube fell critically low, briefly pushing Hungarian power prices above €500/MWh. Gas storage at a 20-year seasonal low leaves Europe with limited buffer entering the heating season, with only approximately 20% of European households having air conditioning in a continent warming roughly twice as fast as the global average. ---

LatAm

  • Colombia's Pereira earthquake killed nearly 300 people, potentially pushing the budget deficit to 8% of GDP and forcing newly inaugurated President de la Espriella to seek emergency US tariff relief — the month's most acute regional macro shock.
  • Chile's central bank held its benchmark rate unchanged at 4.5% in late July, citing global oil price volatility; Brazil continued its easing cycle while Mexico, Colombia, and Chile held — reflecting differentiated inflation profiles across the region.
  • Copper rallied toward record highs on AI data-center and EV demand, driving a six-week advance in Chilean and Peruvian equities; Bank of America forecast prices above $16,000 per ton by mid-2027.
  • Mexico's sovereign spreads have widened materially following a $130 billion Pemex bailout; Brazil's government debt is projected by Societe Generale to rise past 100% of GDP.

Colombia: Earthquake and Fiscal Fragility

A 7.4-magnitude earthquake that struck Colombia's coffee-growing Pereira region on August 11 is the month's most consequential near-term development for Latin American political economy. With the death toll approaching 300 and thousands of buildings destroyed, a budget deficit already on track for 8% of GDP leaves President de la Espriella with almost no room to fund reconstruction while honoring the fiscal austerity that defined his campaign. His government's decision to accept aid from the US, Israel, El Salvador, and Ecuador while rejecting Mexico's search-and-rescue offer raised questions about the politicization of disaster relief. Whether de la Espriella's political alignment with Washington translates into meaningful reconstruction support — he formally asked the US to temporarily suspend tariffs on Colombian goods — will materially shape the government's fiscal trajectory in coming months. The Colombian peso's simultaneous rally, which one of the world's largest currency gains this period, reflects investor optimism about the new administration's macroeconomic posture even as the earthquake introduces severe fiscal complications.
