Diverging Fed Signals

For many years — if not decades — Federal Reserve watchers have been 
accustomed to hearing largely similar takes on the economy and interest rates 
from key officials. Meaning, board members in Washington and the president of 
the New York Fed, who serves as the vice chief of the rate-setting panel.

In the run-up to the Sept. 15-16 policy meeting, however, distinct messages 
emerged before Friday’s communications blackout kicked in. And that will make 
next week’s vote on rates all the more informative going forward in terms of 
how to interpret the US central bank under Kevin Warsh’s leadership.

Chairman Warsh in his Jackson Hole address on Aug. 28 was fairly hawkish 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-31/warsh-takes-a-page-from-aerospace-pioneers-to-find-the-right-stuff?cmpid=BBD090726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8575>
. The summer’s inflation readings “do not tell me that underlying trends have 
meaningfully improved,” he said. Unless the Fed is confident underlying 
inflation is moving to target “clearly and at sufficient speed,” then “we have 
work to do,” he said in remarks that shifted some Fed watchers’ rate calls.
Kevin WarshPhotographer: David Paul Morris/Bloomberg
Five days later, New York Fed President John Williams offered a much more 
dovish take 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-02/fed-s-williams-says-inflation-continuing-to-trending-down?cmpid=BBD090726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8575>
 on price trends. “The data recently has been encouraging,” he said on CNBC. “I 
am actually seeing the trend in inflation moving slowly down as some of the 
effects of the tariffs move into the rearview mirror.”

In the old days, the Fed chief and the New York Fed president would typically 
be on the exact same page. Together with the Fed board’s vice chair, this 
“troika” was viewed asa unified voice 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2023-06-01/pause-time?cmpid=BBD090726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8575>
 that represented where US central bank leadership wanted things to go.

For Krishna Guha at Evercore ISI — a former New York Fed official himself — 
the Williams remarks represented a quandary.

One hypothesis is that Williams was reading Warsh as not necessarily set on a 
September rate hike. “Under troika norms,” had he differed with Warsh, “he 
would have kept quiet,” Guha and his colleagues wrote in a note last week. So 
perhaps the Williams comments cast a fresh perspective on Warsh’s speech. After 
all, Warsh stopped short of directly signaling support for a hike, something 
that may yet prove critical.

“The alternative hypothesis is that the troika norms do not hold any more, and 
Williams is rebelling in public against a decision he sees coming but cannot 
support,” the Evercore ISI team wrote, while adding that seems unlikely.

Then on Thursday came Fed Governor Christopher Waller, a onetime rival to 
Warsh as President Donald Trump’s pick to succeed Jerome Powell at the Fed’s 
helm earlier this year. In comments that had a notable (one-day) impacton the 
bond market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/treasuries-rise-after-fed-s-waller-notes-progress-on-inflation?cmpid=BBD090726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8575>
, Waller said he’d be inclined toleave rates unchanged 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/fed-s-waller-says-september-rate-decision-hinges-on-august-cpi?cmpid=BBD090726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8575>
 as long as inflation continues to slow.

Waller’s remarks were perplexing for a different reason, as Omair Sharif at 
Inflation Insights wrote in a note Friday. He appeared to have “essentially 
flip-flopped <https://x.com/fcastofthemonth/status/2096489015675589060> on his 
inflation view in the last two months,” Sharif wrote.
Christopher WallerPhotographer: David Paul Morris/Bloomberg
Back on July 13, Waller had said he was concerned 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-13/waller-says-fed-may-need-to-raise-rates-to-tame-core-inflation?cmpid=BBD090726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8575>
 about the 12-month pace of core inflation. Running at 3.4% at the time, it had 
accelerated by more than half a percentage point since last October, he said. 
Themost recent reading 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/key-us-inflation-gauge-posts-muted-advance-spending-stalls?cmpid=BBD090726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8575>
 for July was 3.3%. So, little progress there.

But last week, Waller said the 12-month gauge is “not the best guide for where 
inflation is today.” Instead, he emphasized the three-month trend, which is 
much tamer-looking. He thereby “muddied the waters in terms of his reaction 
function,” Sharif wrote. To extend his point, that may make it harder to 
understand how Waller would vote next week depending on what this Friday’s 
consumer price index report reveals for August inflation.

JPMorgan Chase strategists summed it all up last week as “let’s play Family 
Feud,” combining references to Warsh’s regular “family fight 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-06-17/warsh-says-fomc-had-good-family-fight-on-fed-policy-video?cmpid=BBD090726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8575>
” analogy and the name of a US game show. Barclays economists borrowed from a 
film title: “FamilyFight Club <https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0137523/>.” They 
said “Warsh’s framework likely sets a higher bar for a hold” than Williams and 
Waller signaled.

It’s a new constellation for Fed watchers to read, and makes the Sept. 16 vote 
particularly meaningful.

Need-to-Know Research

China’s property-market collapse 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/china-s-property-crisis-from-evergrande-collapse-to-spiraling-prices?cmpid=BBD090726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8575>
 has been “epic,” Macquarie economists Larry Hu and Yuxiao Zhang wrote in a 
note Friday that offered some eye-catching historical comparisons.

Housing starts — that is, the number of properties builders are breaking 
ground on — have plummeted by 78% from their 2021 peak, taking them back to 
levels last seen in China in 2003, the duo calculated. That’s roughly on par 
with the 73% slide the US saw from 2005-2009 (though the American market is 
more tilted toward existing, rather than new, homes). Japan, where new 
construction dominates the residential market, saw a 65% rout from 1996 to 2025.

“China’s five-year adjustment is comparable to Japan’s two-decade bust,” Hu 
and Zhang wrote. There’s little sign of an immediate turn. “With home prices 
back at 2016 levels, rents at 2015 levels, and stagnant wage growth, potential 
homebuyers are expecting further declines in prices, rents and income. Such 
expectations are self-fulfilling, so the bottoming process is likely to be 
prolonged.”
