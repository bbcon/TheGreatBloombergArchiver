Yields jump again

The message coming from the bond market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/australian-benchmark-bond-yield-jumps-to-level-last-seen-in-2011?cmpid=090126_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8388>
 is that the Federal Reserve will have to raise rates to tackle inflation.

The 10-year Treasury yield has climbed for five days, hitting 4.78%, the 
highest since January. That’s having a ripple effect on other markets, pushing 
the yield on a global debt index to the highest since mid-2008.

A round up of the latest:

 * Jackson Hole leaves a mark. The latest catalyst is Fed Chairman Kevin 
Warsh’s tough talk in Wyoming. Wars and extreme weather are pushing upfood 
prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-31/war-weather-push-crop-prices-to-biggest-monthly-gain-since-2012?cmpid=090126_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8388>
, and expectations are building for a rate hike in Japan.
 * Economists flip their views. Barclays and Societe Generale are adding new 
rate hikes to their predictions for this year.
 * Bessent vs Warsh: The US 30-year yield has almost returned to levels from 
before Bessent announced bond buybacks.
 * Europe’s inflation quickens. Consumer price growth came in at the fastest 
since 2023 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/euro-zone-inflation-jumps-to-highest-in-almost-three-years?cmpid=090126_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8388>
, cementing the case for the ECB to hike next week.
“The bond market is not imploding, but it’s sending a very clear memo that 
stickier inflation means higher for longer,” said Prashant Newnaha of TD 
Securities.

However, as we wrote yesterday, there’s still no Wall Street consensus 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-30/bond-investors-wary-after-warsh-fans-wagers-fed-poised-to-hike?cmpid=090126_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8388>
 on the Fed’s next move. Some investors argue that the central bank can’t 
control this kind of inflation with higher rates, and Warsh would likely prefer 
to hold.

Either way, markets are getting ready for the possibility that inflation 
forces his hand. That’ll make the jobs reading later in the week even more 
important. — Lynn Thomasson

It’s not just the US

Yields in Japan and France are also moving sharply higher. Here’s a look at 
both:

Japan’s return to normality: The bond market has changed dramatically since 
the central bank ended the world’s last negative interest-rate policy in 2024.

Prices that were formerly under the thumb of the BOJ are now determined to a 
greater degree by investors. They are making decisions to buy and sell based on 
the outlook for inflation and economic growth, rather than BOJ policy.

The higher yields also reflect a general revival of the once-stagnant economy, 
which is now enjoying a surge in corporate profits and rising wages.

France is gripped by political anxiety. The next presidential election is 
eight months away and worries are high over who will succeed Emmanuel Macron.

As a result, the premium that France pays to borrow over Germany has widened 
to near the highest since Europe’s sovereign debt crisis in 2012.

The key question is how the next leader will tackle a fiscal deficit above 5%, 
surging debt costs and sputtering growth. Far-right candidate Marine Le Pen and 
far-left hopeful Jean-Luc Mélenchon are promising to take France in radically 
different directions. —John Cheng
