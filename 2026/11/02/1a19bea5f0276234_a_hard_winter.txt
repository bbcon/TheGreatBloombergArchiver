Vladimir Putin wants the world to believe Moscow doesn’t need a peace deal 
with Ukraine because its economy is resilient and its military goals are on 
track.

They’re not; winter is coming in Russia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-03/ukraine-s-surprise-counterattack-sets-back-putin-s-donbas-goals?cmpid=BBD110226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10885>
, too.

Putin’s forces suffered a record 54,000 casualties in October, Ukrainian 
President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said yesterday. European government assessments 
shared with Bloomberg show similar figures, with the proportion of troops 
killed also at a record.

Even if Putin gives little thought to casualties, the carnage isn’t just 
staggering but unsustainable, greater than the number of people that Russia is 
currently recruiting.

And Moscow has little to show for it, having made fewer territorial advances 
this year compared with 2025 and at a greater toll — and with no strategically 
significant gains.
A mobile recruitment point in Moscow in 2023.Photographer: Natalia 
Kolesnikova/AFP/Getty Images
In October, Russian forces lost ground for the second month in a row, 
according to the Institute for the Study of War.

All that is happening against the backdrop of an economy facing increased 
strain 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-27/russia-was-forced-to-cut-spending-after-running-out-of-cash?cmpid=BBD110226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10885>
 due torising war costs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-02/russia-looks-to-delay-defense-ministry-pay-amid-budget-gap?cmpid=BBD110226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10885>
 and budget deficits, sanctions and Ukrainian strikes against oil refineries.

While Moscow publicly shrugs off such setbacks, Putin has shifted tactics to 
heavily bombing Ukrainian cities and critical infrastructure with the aim of 
breaking morale and strengthening the Kremlin’s hand come the spring.

Ukraine is set to face its toughest winter yet. But much ultimately depends on 
Kyiv’s ability to defend itself.
Buildings damaged in a Russian missile attack in Zaporizhzhia, Ukraine, on 
Oct. 17.Photographer: Daria Nazarova/AFP/Getty Images
That’s where many of Ukraine’s allies and partners have fallen short, failing 
to provide the air-defense missiles needed, even if Kyiv has been more 
successful in countering Russia’s jet-powered drones.

Putin remains implacable in the face of renewed efforts to end  
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-27/putin-plans-talks-with-finland-norway-leaders-on-ukraine?cmpid=BBD110226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10885>
the fighting. Donald Trump mightthrow a curveball 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-02/us-pushes-for-russia-to-resume-gas-sales-to-europe-wsj-reports?cmpid=BBD110226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10885>
 into the process after the US midterm elections.

It seems clear though, that neither Russia nor Ukraine will get through the 
winter unscathed. Whoever emerges in the better position may well determine the 
outcome of the war. — Alberto Nardelli 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AU7VEMIerb0/alberto-nardelli?cmpid=BBD110226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10885>
