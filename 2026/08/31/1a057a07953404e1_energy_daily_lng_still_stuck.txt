Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Asian energy team leader Stephen Stapczynski looks at why, for liquefied 
natural gas, the Strait of Hormuz remains all-but closed.

US President Donald Trump has good reason to cheer the rebound in oil flows 
through the Strait of Hormuz.

Crude traffic through the waterway reached its highest level in months before 
fighting resumed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-30/us-strikes-iranian-rocket-launchers-in-first-attack-in-weeks?cmpid=BBBXT083126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8348>
 over the weekend, prompting Trump to tell Axios last week: “That sucker is 
open.”

But for liquefied natural gas, it’s a totally different story. Barely any fuel 
has gone through the chokepoint since aQatari tanker was attacked 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-01/tanker-carrying-qatari-lng-struck-while-transiting-hormuz?cmpid=BBBXT083126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8348>
 in early July.

The almost-total loss of Qatari LNG — which made up nearly a fifth of global 
exports last year — has pushed spot prices in Europe and Asia to near their 
highest levels since the conflict began. Brent oil, by contrast, is almost 30% 
below its war-time peak reached in April.

So why isn’t the super-chilled fuel following oil through the strait?
A floating storage regasification unit at an LNG terminal in Karachi, Pakistan.
Photographer: Asim Hafeez/Bloomberg
For a start, LNG tankers are scarcer. There are only about 850 of them 
globally, compared with more than 7,000 crude carriers. That makes each LNG 
vessel more valuable, and losing even one has an immediate impact on shipping 
capacity — especially since Qatar’s tankers are specially designed for their 
Ras Laffan export facility.

Ownership matters, too. A large share of the tankers serving Qatar are 
controlled by the state exporter or affiliated shipowners. They’ve taken a far 
more cautious approach since their vessel was attacked.

And LNG can’t be easily transferred between ships, as is the case with oil. So 
replicating theshuttle trade 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-24/hormuz-oil-flows-are-going-strong-as-war-drags-on?cmpid=BBBXT083126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8348>
 is proving challenging.

Every day that LNG traffic remains stalled, it tightens the market and risks 
pushing prices higher. Europe needs to replenish inventories ahead of winter, 
which is set to heighten competition with Asian buyers that have traditionally 
relied heavily on Persian Gulf supply.

Pakistan, a key intermediary between Tehran and Washington during the war, 
doesn’t appear to be betting on a quick reopening. The country, which sourced 
99% of its LNG from Qatar last year, just issued a tender to purchase a spot 
shipment for prompt delivery.

If one of Qatar’s most dependent customers is still scrambling for replacement 
supply, that’s a strong signal the market doesn’t expect LNG flows through 
Hormuz to recover any time soon.

—Stephen Stapczynski, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Strong demand has stretched gas turbine making capacity globally, lengthening 
delivery times for advanced machines, according toBloombergNEF 
<bbg://news/stories/TKMGSCKIP3K2>. GE Vernova Inc., Siemens Energy AG and 
Mitsubishi Heavy Industries Ltd. control about 66% of global manufacturing 
capacity share today, rising to roughly 70% by 2030. The BNEF researchers point 
out that if power demand softens, today’s scarcity could become tomorrow’s 
overcapacity.
