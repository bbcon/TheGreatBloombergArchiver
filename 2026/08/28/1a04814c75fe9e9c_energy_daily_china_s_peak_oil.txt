Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporters Rong Wei Neo and Dan Murtaugh explore the unexpected effects of the 
Middle East war on China’s biggest oil companies.

China has spent years trying to curb its addiction to foreign crude. Reports 
from the country’s oil majors this week show the Iran war may have helped it 
start kicking the habit.

Sinopec, the country’s top refiner, noted staggering reductions in demand for 
road fuel, and the company’s chairman became the highest-ranking official to 
make the call that China has reachedpeak oil 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-24/china-s-oil-demand-very-likely-peaked-last-year-sinopec-says?cmpid=BBBXT082826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8280>
.

Meanwhile, offshore driller Cnooc Ltd., the main source of domestic output 
growth, was able to continue its long string ofproduction increases 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/cnooc-first-half-profit-rises-after-iran-war-boosted-oil-prices?cmpid=BBBXT082826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8280>
 despite maintenance that temporarily shut some high-yielding gas platforms.

The picture that emerges is of the world’s largest oil and gas importer 
becoming less dependent on the whims of foreign suppliers and geopolitical 
rivals.

China’s crude imports were estimated to have fallen by 3.3 million barrels a 
day in the second quarter from a year earlier, according toFGE NexantECA 
<https://www.nexanteca.com/>.

Much of that may be a temporary reaction to the disruption in the Strait of 
Hormuz — a halt in stockpiling, cuts in refining and a ban on fuel exports — 
but several hundred thousand barrels at least are expectednever to come back 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-22/chinese-oil-imports-may-never-fully-recover-from-the-iran-war?cmpid=BBBXT082826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8280>
.
A Sinopec gas station in Shanghai, China.Photographer: Raul Ariano/Bloomberg
The biggest driver has been a push for electrification that’s displacing 
gasoline and diesel. When oil prices jumped, so did interest in electric 
vehicles — battery-powered cars accounted for 64% of passenger vehicles sold to 
dealers in July, while electric tractors made up more than a quarter of 
heavy-duty truckdeliveries <bbg://news/stories/TKBO4HKIUPSA> in June.

Chinese consumers are incredibly cost-sensitive — just ask anyone selling 
1-yuan (15-cent) cups ofbubble tea <https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1017322> — 
and these cheap, high-quality EVs have given them a chance to flex against high 
oil prices.

You’d think this would be bad news for oil companies. Indeed, refiners like 
Sinopec are troubled by the drop in road fuel demand and are shifting 
facilities toward producing chemicals and advanced materials like carbon fiber 
composites.

On the upstream side, though, the picture is rosier. The country still imports 
about 70% of its oil and 40% of its gas, leaving ample room to maintain or even 
grow domestic production without oversaturating the market.

China’s reduced import dependence and big investments in oil and gas storage 
also allow it to be an opportunistic buyer,stepping back 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-21/china-keeps-oil-buying-in-check-as-crude-prices-stay-above-90?cmpid=BBBXT082826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8280>
 when prices are high and pouncing on dips.

Peak oil, then, isn’t an existential problem for China’s energy companies. But 
it might prove a challenge for the foreign exporters who’ve grown reliant on 
the market.

— Rong Wei Neo and Dan Murtaugh, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

China is starting to tighten the world’s largest carbon market, lowering 
power-sector benchmarks that should reduce allowance supply while cushioning 
steel, cement and aluminum, according to BloombergNEF. The gradual approach 
could strengthen the carbon-price signal while limiting the initial cost shock 
on sectors that together account for about 65% of the country’s emissions.
