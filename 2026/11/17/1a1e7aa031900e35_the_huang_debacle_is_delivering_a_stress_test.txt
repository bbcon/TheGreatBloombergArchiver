When the Trend Is Not Your Friend

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was clear about Trump 2.0’s market aims. The 
key was a weak dollar, along with low oil prices andlow bond yields 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-26/treasury-s-battle-against-rising-bond-yields-has-started?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
 while the stock market rallied.

Even with the Nasdaq 100 down 13% in five weeks, stocks are far ahead over the 
past two years. But bond yields are too high for the administration’s comfort, 
while crude oil, still more than $90 per barrel, droveRepublicans’ midterm 
disaster 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-04/blue-tsunami-crashes-down-on-donald-trump?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
:

Easily missed is the dollar. It hasn’t been as strong as it is now, measured 
by the DXY index of major currencies, since April 2 last year — Liberation Day, 
when President Donald Trump’s announcement of sweeping tariffs on the rest of 
the world detonated the dollar’s standing:

A stronger dollar combats inflation, but counters hopes to rebuild US 
manufacturing. A corollary is thegold price 
<https://pdf.omfif.org/holding-power>, which tends to be the dollar’s inverse. 
It’s now at its lowest in more than a year, and 12.85% below its 200-day moving 
average, the widely accepted measure of the long-term trend.

The dollar hasn’t been in such a negative trend since the bear market of 2013, 
when gold sold off 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2013-04-15/the-price-of-gold-is-crashing-dot-heres-why?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
 amid the realization that the zero interest rates that followed the Global 
Financial Crisis would not, as expected, drive a return to inflation:

Shifts in trend like this suggest important moves in market sentiment. Why is 
this happening now? The simplest answer is that the October readings on US
employment 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/live-blog/2026-11-06/us-employment-report-for-october-live-updates?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
 (stronger than expected) andinflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/live-blog/2026-11-10/us-cpi-report-for-october-live-updates?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
 (higher than hoped) were seen to quash the argument that no further rate hikes 
were necessary. Bond yields had retreated after their September surge. They’re 
back near their highs, as are rate expectations. Another three hikes are priced 
for the next 12 months, with a strong chance of a fourth. As that implies rates 
will end two percentage points higher than people expected earlier this year, 
it’s no surprise that it moved the dollar and gold into new trajectories.

Two further factors are important. First, the news from everywhere else in the 
developed world makes the US look better.Europe is in trouble 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-europe-energy-woes/?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
. The latestGDP number for Japan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-15/japan-s-economy-contracts-as-bad-weather-weighs-on-consumption?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
, published Monday, shows a slight contraction of 0.1% over the last 12 months. 
That’s deeply disappointing, and gives everyone reason to buy the dollar again. 
The yen has touched 160 per dollar once more in the last few days. “Despite all 
the shocks, and the US creating them themselves, we are still in the best 
position to deal with them,” saysMarc Chandler of Bannockburn Global Forex 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-10-30/what-if-the-problem-is-too-much-capital?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
.

Second is the AI boom. We’ve just had a stress test. Badly received earnings 
were followed byNvidia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-12/the-rise-and-reckoning-of-jensen-huang-video?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
’sJensen Huang 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-16/opinion-jensen-huang-cannot-return-to-nvidia-video?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
 scandal, AI stocks tumbled — and the overall stock market is still only about 
5% below last October’s peak, not enough to prompt the Federal Reserve to

Traders in other markets no longer think that a burst AI bubble could bring 
down US rates for them. What remains is an American economy far stronger than 
anyone else (which is what Bessent and Trump were hoping for), and with it, the 
upward pressure on yields and the currency that they wanted to avoid.

AI Out of Style

The stock market has undergone an immense reshuffling over the last few weeks, 
driven by the AI trade. That we can tell. But close examination reveals just 
how powerful an economic driver artificial intelligence has become.

Since the Nasdaq 100 peaked on Oct. 5, most stocks have risen. The average S&P 
500 stock has gained about 5%. In the Russell 3000 index of the 3,000 largest 
US stocks by market cap, 1,593 companies are up since then, while 1,406 are down
. Chip stocks (measured by the SOX semiconductors index) fell 24%, mostly 
before the Huang scandal hit the headlines.

Quants argue that explaining the selloff is easy. AI has taken on such macro 
significance that its effects on the rest of the market are easily measured. 
Quant Insight summarizes them as follows:

 * Credit spreads. The buildout is largely debt-financed. When investors doubt 
the returns on AI, they doubt that debt, too. Spreads and AI stocks move 
together.
 * Metals. Data centers and power grids use a lot of them. Views on AI demand 
show up in metals prices.
 * Real rates. If AI lifts growth and the demand for capital, real yields rise.
 * Risk appetite. AI is the market’s largest and most crowded growth trade. It 
falls first when appetite for risk fades.
These four factors between them, as the chart shows, explain close to all of 
the selloffs we’ve seen:
Source: Quant Insight
But why did so many other stocks recover? As Bloomberg’s Factors to Watch 
shows, value (buying companies that look cheap compared to their fundamentals) 
has enjoyed a renaissance since the Nasdaq peak (as has, to a lesser extent, 
quality, or buying companies with strong balance sheets):

This cannot be explained directly by the fallout from AI. Mahmood Noorani, 
Quant Insight’s CEO, argues that it’s a matter of style, rather than investment 
factors. As these terms are often used interchangeably, it’s worth checking 
what he means by this:
A macro factor is something outside the stock market: real rates, credit 
spreads, metals, risk appetite. It is environmental. A style is a type of 
stock: value, momentum, quality, low vol, built by sorting stocks on their own 
traits (intrinsic to the company). Macro tells you what the world is doing. 
Style tells you what kind of stock you own.
AI has become a powerful force in the macro-economy, and that shows up in its 
immense impact on the stock market. On this occasion, it has prompted many to 
look at the kind of stock they own, and realize that there’s value to be had 
elsewhere. It’s probably not over.

Postcard From Santiago

Greetings from Chile’s glittering capital 
<https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/destination/santiago>, currently 
basking in 80-degree heat even as snow remains on the caps of the Andes, 
visible from the Bloomberg office.No country produces more copper 
<https://www.visualcapitalist.com/ranked-the-worlds-largest-copper-producers/>, 
and no one benefited more from the rise of China. Over the last 
quarter-century, global markets have priced the stock market as though it was 
one giant copper mine:

The country’s financiers have long wanted to detach themselves. Copper prices 
are cyclical, and this correlation makes the availability of capital to the 
entire economy more variable. This year, they succeeded in decoupling, but not 
in a good way:

Copper is in a nasty slump, caused by collywobbles over AI and concerns about 
China. But the fall in local stocks far precedes it. The reason: the mines in 
Chile, the world’s largest copper producer with the largest reserves, are 
aging. It takes years to develop new ones. Production byCodelco 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/codelco-chair-plans-restructuring-after-copper-reporting-scandal?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
, the huge nationalized producer, is in a marked downward trend, while a 
misreporting scandal has further battered confidence:

Copper has been buoyed this year not only by strong demand but also by the 
problems with supply that have tended to go unnoticed elsewhere — except in 
Chile, where theytop the national agenda 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-13/chile-mulls-private-sector-role-in-codelco-mines?cmpid=BBD111726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11434>
 and attempts to bring in the private sector are being mooted.

The issue focuses further attention on the way foreign investors allocate 
flows to countries like Chile, which look ever more outdated. While comfortably 
wealthier than bigger Latin American economies, with GDP per capita of about 
$35,000 compared with Mexico’s $25,000 and Brazil’s $22,000, Chile accounts for 
just 0.6% of MSCI’s Emerging Markets index, and only 6.7% of its Latin America 
index. Its 10 stocks in the EM index put it ahead of Colombia and Peru, which 
have three each — but all of these companies tend to move in line with eddies 
in sentiment toward Mexico and Brazil.

This creates problems for the three big Andean economies. Put them together 
and they have a joint population of about 100 million — still smaller than 
Mexico (130 million), but large enough to be treated as a viable third option 
by Latin American fund managers. As it is, active LatAm investors know that 
their relative performance depends overwhelmingly on how they decide to 
allocate between Brazil and Mexico. Andean economies aren’t big enough, and 
their markets aren’t deep enough, to move the needle.

As a result, Chile rises and falls in line with the top-down flows for the 
region, despite considerable idiosyncratic differences. Even the countries of 
the euro zone, which share a currency, tend to deviate more than this:

Three years ago, the stock exchanges of Chile, Colombia and Peru merged to 
create a new overarching entity <https://www.nuam.com/en> in an attempt to 
counter this. A market combining the three should command attention. Merging 
jurisdictions is difficult, and so is changing investors’ habits once set. But 
it’s hard to escape the impression that Andean opportunities are being 
overlooked. It would be good if they succeeded.
