Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Mitchell Ferman looks at how surging oil earnings driven by the war in 
the Persian Gulf are putting industry taxes back on the political agenda.

Big Oil’s war-driven windfall 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-30/exxon-reaps-record-oil-refining-profits-on-war-driven-gasoline-diesel-spikes?cmpid=BBBXT111326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261113&utm_content=11393>
 is reviving calls to claw back some of the gains.

United Nations Secretary-General António Guterres joined the chorus yesterday, 
calling the industry’s profits “morally unjustifiable” and urging governments 
to tax them to help pay for climate damage. The tools already exist, he told 
Bloomberg TV at the UN climate summit: “It’s a political decision, it’s not a 
technical problem.”

The push is gathering momentum as Western oil giants reap massive hauls. The 
five supermajors are on track for their second-most profitable year ever, 
behind only 2022 after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine sent energy prices soaring.

In Washington, Democrats’ midterm sweep has revived legislation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-04/oil-sector-girds-for-possible-windfall-tax-after-democrats-win?cmpid=BBBXT111326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261113&utm_content=11393>
 that would tax oil companies whenever Brent crude trades above its roughly 
$68-a-barrel 2025 average, with proceeds funding consumer rebates.

Rapidan Energy Group, a Washington-based research and consulting firm, puts 
the odds of Congress taxing oil windfalls — or cutting fossil-fuel tax breaks — 
at 30%.

President Donald Trump is the wildcard. The White House says cooperation with 
Democrats on such a tax hasn’t been discussed, and Trump celebrated the 
industry’s profits as an economic benefit during the midterm campaign. But he 
has also criticized ExxonMobil Holdings Corp. and Chevron Corp. for “making too 
much money” and urged them to “give some of that back to the public.”
Big Oil reaped more than $50 billion in windfall profits in the third quarter.
Photographer: Emily Elconin/Bloomberg
Pressure is building in Europe, too. Germany, Italy, Spain, Austria, Poland 
and Portugal have pushed the European Union to consider another coordinated 
levy after one imposed during the 2022 energy crisis. The EU agreed last month 
to examine the idea, though Brussels has stopped short of proposing a new tax.

Portugal has separately proposed a 33% oil-sector windfall tax, now pending in 
parliament. The UK already taxes extraordinary oil profits and is legislating a 
long-term mechanism to trigger levies during periods of high oil and gas prices.

Whether the political momentum turns into new taxes remains unclear. Oil 
companies argue such levies discourage investment needed to increase supply, 
while several European governments remain wary of another bloc-wide measure.

But with energy companies benefiting from price spikes driven by wars that 
have simultaneously raised household fuel bills, the debate over who should 
keep the windfall is back.

—Mitchell Ferman, Bloomberg News
