This is Charlie Zhu 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASchFFqzGbw/charlie-zhu?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
 in Beijing.

I’ve been covering China for decades and seen multiple episodes of government 
intervention to prop up markets over the years. This week’s measures rank among 
themost coordinated 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/china-broadens-market-rescue-with-record-inflows-into-tech-etf?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
 and forceful I’ve ever seen.

The rout started in China’s AI and semiconductor sector, where investors had 
become uneasy about stretched valuations after months of gains. The STAR 50 
Index of chipmakers and other technology firms plunged about 17% last week as 
investors unwound leveraged positions at the fastest pace since the 2015-16 
market crash. Concerns about themassive listing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-16/cxmt-s-blockbuster-ipo-212-times-covered-for-retail-investors?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
 of chipmaker CXMT added to pressures as it threatened to drain liquidity from 
the broader market.

By Friday, a closely watched sentiment indicator for the STAR 50 was at its 
most bearish since the Nasdaq-style board was launched in 2020. Politically, it 
was all a bit awkward. The selloff deepened Friday right as President Xi 
Jinping wasgiving a speech 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-16/china-s-ai-ascendance-gives-xi-a-stage-and-a-security-dilemma?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
 at the World Artificial Intelligence Conference in Shanghai, where he sought 
to use the rise of China’s AI models to bolster Beijing’s claim to a leading 
role in shaping global rules governing the technology.
A chart showing popular AI models in China at the World AI Conference in 
Shanghai.Photographer: Qilai Shen/Bloomberg
Cue the national team. A group of state-linked ETFs appeared to step in last 
Friday with their trading volume surging to the highest levels in months. That 
helped major stock indexes recover from steeper intraday losses. Two major 
state-owned investment firms, China Reform Holdings and China Chengtong 
Holdings, publicly disclosed fresh equity purchases during the weekend — their 
first such announcements since the tariff-driven market turmoil of 2025.

The next signal of support came Monday, when the ChinaAMC STAR 50 ETF 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/china-broadens-market-rescue-with-record-inflows-into-tech-etf?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
 attracted a record 13.8 billion yuan ($2 billion) of inflows. While the 
identity of buyers wasn’t disclosed, traders widely interpreted the move as 
evidence that state-backed capital was being directed toward the hardest-hit 
technology shares. The market’s response was swift. The STAR 50 Index surged 
almost 11% on Tuesday, its largest one-day jump in nearly two years. The 
ChiNext Index gained more than 7%, while the benchmark CSI 300 rose more than 
3%.

China’s largest insurers also got in on the act, pledging to increase 
investments in stocks and funds. China Life said it had purchased more than 10 
billion yuan worth of equities and investment products while promising greater 
exposure to emerging industries. Ping An Insurance and People’s Insurance 
Company (Group) of China made similar commitments.

Asset managers joined the rescue effort as well. Bosera Fund said it would 
invest its own capital into equity products under management, a step typically 
reserved for periods of acute market stress. Brokerages were also mobilized. GF 
Securities is raising its margin financing quota by 90 billion yuan, providing 
additional liquidity. And listed companies have done their part, announcing a 
wave of share buybacks in a show of support for the market.

Regulators joined in too. The top securities watchdog met investors and 
pledged to maintain market stability, rein in quant funds, and improve investor 
protection. A number of state-owned asset management companies affiliated with 
local governments stepped up stock purchases too. Meantime, state media churned 
out reports talking up markets.

Throw it all together, and it’s one of the most significant and coordinated 
interventions I can recall. The logic behind the rescue effort appears 
straightforward: Beijing can’t afford weak markets and fading investor 
enthusiasm at a time when some of the country’s most important AI and 
semiconductor companies are seeking massive amounts of capital to fund their
competition with the US 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-07-21/china-s-double-moonshot-moment-in-the-ai-tech-race?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
. Most pressingly, Beijing doesn’t want market turmoil to derail the $9.8 
billion IPO of memory-chip maker CXMT.

But there’s also a broader economic consideration. “From a macroeconomic 
perspective, market stability brings many benefits, from facilitating 
fundraising for investment critical for industries and technologies to develop 
to supporting consumption,” Bloomberg Economics’s Chang Shu wrote in anote 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/screens/DOCV%20RES%20TILMFXKK3NYF?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
.

History suggests Beijing’s intervention may prove effective 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-23/cxmt-s-frenzy-ignites-hopes-of-futher-hardware-rally-in-china?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
. Over the past decade, episodes of heavy inflows into China’s largest domestic 
ETFs have often been followed by gains in the CSI 300 Index. More importantly, 
state intervention carries a powerful signaling effect. By stepping in 
aggressively, authorities are telling investors that market stability remains a 
policy priority.

Whether the campaign can fully revive enthusiasm for China’s AI trade is less 
certain. Many investors still argue that valuations need further adjustment 
after the sector’s extraordinary rally. And BE’s Shu says a durable bull market 
and broader economic gains still depend on structural reforms that revive 
balanced growth and address weaknesses in demand and the legacy economy.

But for now, Beijing has made one thing clear: at a moment when confidence in 
technology champions was at stake, it wasn’t prepared to stand aside and let 
the market find its own bottom. Instead, it’s providing a “tech stock put.”

Moonshot Moment

It took just a matter of days for Moonshot to transform from a little-known 
Chinese AI lab into a formidable rival to Anthropic — and a key player in the 
escalating US-China tech rivalry.

The Beijing-based company — founded by a Pink Floyd-loving genius — last week 
dropped a powerful 2.8 trillion-parameter AI model, the world’s largest 
open-weight model, which goes live for public download in a few days. The
initial verdicts <https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/kimi-k3> are in: Kimi K3 
trails only the two top offerings from Anthropic and OpenAI. Complete with a 
thumbs-up from Elon Musk, many are calling Kimi’s success China’s next 
“DeepSeek moment.”
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-07-20/moonshot-launches-china-s-largest-ai-model-kimi-k3-video?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
Bloomberg’s Minmin Low explains what Moonshot’s Kimi K3 means for the global AI 
race.
Behind Moonshot’s rise — as we profiled 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-20/chinese-ai-sensation-moonshot-s-gamble-on-big-models-pays-off?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
 in our piece on reclusive founder Yang Zhilin — is an unwavering pursuit of 
scaling and a fierce rivalry with DeepSeek, whose groundbreaking R1 in early 
2025 rattled investors on concerns that low-cost, open-weight AI would 
undermine America’s massive bet on compute and high-end chips.

Moonshot is capitalizing on the hype: it’s closing a funding round valuing it 
at over $30 billion, beforelaunching another 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/china-s-moonshot-in-talks-on-pre-ipo-funds-at-50-billion-value?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
 seeking a $50 billion price tag. If it pulls that off, we’re talking about a 
more than tenfold valuation surge in less than a year. And a Hong Kongpublic 
listing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-19/china-s-moonshot-plans-ipo-in-six-months-after-ai-breakthrough?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
 could come as soon as this year.

But as the Chinese proverb goes, “the bird that sticks its head out gets shot.”

Moonshot quickly landed not on the moon, but in the crosshairs of the world’s 
superpowers. A White House officialthis week accused 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-22/white-house-official-says-moonshot-accessed-banned-nvidia-chips?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
 the Chinese company of using banned Nvidia chips to train the K3 model, while 
reviving allegations that it relied on distillation — the practice of illicitly 
extracting results from top US rivals like Anthropic’s Fable to boost its own. 
Speculation is mounting that the Trump administration could move tocut off US 
access 
<https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/secret-trump-administration-battle-fight-093005220.html>
 to Chinese AI models altogether. Moonshot hasn’t commented on either front.

While Washington tries to put the brakes on Chinese tech, Beijing isn’t 
missing a beat to pitch itself as the champion of open-source innovation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-22/china-s-ai-for-all-offensive-defies-us-containment-playbook?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
. A day after K3’s release, with crowds flocking to Moonshot’s booth at China’s 
premier tech summit, President Xi Jinping used the occasion to hail the 
nation’s progress in developing low-cost AI and called for a more open 
technological order.

Xi warned 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-17/xi-vows-to-make-ai-for-all-in-debut-at-china-s-top-tech-summit?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
 that “AI development should not be a solo performance by a single country” — a 
clear jab without explicitly naming the US. Beijing even used the annual 
gathering to launch the China-led World AI Cooperation Organization, locking in 
29 nations — including Russia and Brazil — as founding members.

Expect AI to remain a major flashpoint and take center stage when Xi and Trump 
meet in September for their second presidential pow-wow of the year.

Behind the Great Firewall

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

Over the past week, Chinese internet users have celebrated an unlikely 
literary star: a food delivery driver who won one of the country’s most 
prestigious writing awards.

Wang Jibing, 56, received the Lu Xun Literary Prize for Low Flight 
<https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-07-20/56-year-old-delivery-rider-wins-China-s-top-literary-prize-1OWaBXDj3WM/index.html>
, a collection of poems capturing the struggles and aspirations of delivery 
workers like him. “Who says spreading your wings means you have to fly high? 
Flying low is still flying,” reads the opening line of the title poem.

Published in 2024, the collection draws on interviews Wang conducted with more 
than 100 couriers in the eastern city of Kunshan. He wrote between food 
deliveries, sometimes pulling out his phone to record voice memos at traffic 
lights to capture his inspiration. “When you deliver food, you live by the 
second,” he told state media outlet The Paper.

Born into a poor family in rural Jiangsu province, Wang dropped out of 
secondary school and worked a string of manual jobs from construction to sand 
mining, before becoming a food delivery driver in 2019. Since then, he’s 
written about 6,000 poems, according to state media.
A Ele.me food deliveryman in Yancheng, China.Photographer: Jiang 
Xiaopeng/VCG/Getty Images via Visual China Group
Wang’s story resonated widely online, with many praising his literary talent, 
dedication and perseverance.

“I used to think literature was far removed from ordinary people, but Wang 
Jibing proves that poetry knows no social status,” one Weibo user wrote. “Words 
rooted in the soil hold the greatest power, and the everyday grind of daily 
life can still bloom into romantic beauty.”

State media has seized on Wang’s victory as a rare uplifting story from 
China’s cut-throat gig economy. As economic growth slows and thejob market 
softens 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/china-at-risk-of-20-youth-joblessness-as-labor-distress-spreads?cmpid=BBD072426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6867>
, millions have turned to delivery, ride-hailing and livestreaming. By one 
estimate, some 320 million people are in such “flexible employment.”

For many online, Wang’s achievement serves as a reminder that daily struggles 
can be meaningful in their own way.

“I believe true poetry is forged in real life,” another Weibo user wrote. “For 
Wang Jibing, without the trials of life, his poetry wouldn’t be so deeply 
moving.”
