**Oil Markets and the Iran Conflict**
The IEA's surplus forecast represents a meaningful inflection point in the oil market narrative that has prevailed since the conflict began. Asian refiners — in Japan, China, and Thailand — are increasing purchases of Middle Eastern crude as Strait of Hormuz flows partially recover, reducing the premium on alternative supply sources including US crude. Nevertheless, Trump's comments that the US may forgo a formal Iran deal introduce renewed uncertainty: without a structured agreement, the risk of renewed military escalation and supply disruption remains elevated. The five Western oil supermajors are on track for their second-most profitable year on record, reviving windfall-tax proposals in both the US Congress — where Democrats' midterm sweep has given fresh impetus to legislation pegging levies to Brent above its ~$68/barrel 2025 average — and in multiple European countries, with Portugal proposing a 33% sectoral levy.

**COP31 and Climate Finance**
Negotiations in Antalya are entering the technically demanding phase following the leaders' summit. The presidency's first summary document acknowledges that climate finance pledges are not being met and flags the UN Environment Programme's finding that the 1.5°C Paris target is no longer achievable. Canada's position — expanding oil and gas exports while pledging net-zero by 2050 — crystallizes the central tension between energy security imperatives, amplified by the Iran war, and emissions commitments. China's decision not to bid for COP33 hosting duties in 2028 removes a potentially consequential actor from the convening role.
