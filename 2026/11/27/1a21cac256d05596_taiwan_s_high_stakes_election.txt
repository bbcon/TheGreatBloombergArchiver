Local elections in Taiwan are set to reverberate 
<bbg://screens/NSN%20TMII53T9NJLS> way beyond its city halls.

Voters will choose more than 11,000 mayors and other officials tomorrow, a 
contest that should revolve around day-to-day issues like the provision of 
welfare services and childcare.

Yet relations with China <bbg://screens/NSN%20TD9NWMKJH6V4> will loom large 
over the vote that will test whether the ruling Democratic Progressive Party 
can replicate its victories in a record three consecutive presidential 
elections across local government.

A strong showing by the party would boost President Lai Ching-te’s standing as 
he prepares toseek another term <bbg://screens/NSN%20TO29B8T96OSI> in 2028.
An election rally in Taipei.Photographer: Cheng Yu-chen/AFP/Getty Images
While Beijing insists that Taiwan is an inalienable part of its territory, the 
DPP maintains that it is already a de facto independent state. Government 
officials accuse China of using a combination of disinformation and political 
and economic coercion to influence the election outcome.

The biggest prize <bbg://screens/NSN%20TNNIOBKK3NY9> is the capital, Taipei, 
which has traditionally leaned toward the opposition Kuomintang, or KMT, a 
party that advocates friendlier ties with China. The DPP hasn’t won in the city 
since 1994, while three of Taiwan’s democratically elected presidents have 
served as Taipei’s mayor — making the post a springboard to higher office.

KMT mayor Chiang Wan-an, one of the party’s rising stars, is seeking 
reelection and is facing an unexpectedly strong challenge from the DPP’s Puma 
Shen.

Lai personally endorsed Shen and would be a primary beneficiary of a change in 
control of the capital. That would be a setback for Beijing — it has sanctioned 
Shen over his efforts to counter Chinese influence and strengthen Taiwan’s 
defenses.

A poor showing or a loss for Chiang would meanwhile weaken his standing within 
his party, which has yet to see a clear candidate emerge to challenge Lai.

Thousands of people turned out for election rallies in Taipei and lined its 
streets to catch a glimpse of the candidates’ motorcades.

That’s an indicator of just how high the stakes are. — Yian Lee
