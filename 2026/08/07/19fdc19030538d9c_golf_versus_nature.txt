Scotland’s golf showdown

By Laura Avetisyan 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXeDWgjX_qI/laura-avetisyan?cmpid=BBD080726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7476>

A yearslong saga pitting environmentalists against plans for a remote Scottish 
golf course targeting rich Americans is just weeks from a verdict, with one 
issue dominating the debate: how best to look after a few hundred yards of sand 
dune and a small, grass-like plant.
Local ecologist Tom Dargie at Coul Links, a protected coastal sand dune 
system, in Sutherland on the east coast of Scotland.Photographer: Michael Vince 
Kim/Bloomberg
It’s the sort of real estate showdown being played out across the globe as 
investors seek to monetize sparsely populated sites and deep-pocketed tourists 
look for somewhere niche to spend their time and money. Standing against them 
is an often-powerful lobby of nature watchers and scientists.

It’s also a story not unfamiliar to Scotland after the government cleared 
Donald Trump to build a premier course on a protected area near Aberdeen almost 
two decades ago, well before he became US president. Should the 
conservationists prevail this time, it would be a significant victory for them 
and a big setback for an expanding golf tourism market estimated byFortune 
Business Insights 
<https://www.fortunebusinessinsights.com/golf-tourism-market-104986> to be 
worth about $3 billion in the UK.

Rejecting the golf development plan at Coul Links, which is part of the Loch 
Fleet Site of Special Scientific Interest, or SSSI, a diverse ecosystem next to 
the chilly waters of the North Sea, would probably kill the project for good.

The landowner, Edward Abel Smith, has an initial £20 million ($27 million) of 
US-based financing ready for release as soon as he gets the green light, a 
large part of which would to go to preserving the wetlands, grasses and dunes 
next to the proposed site. The Keiser family, whose résumé includes top-ranked 
resortBandon Dunes <https://bandondunesgolf.com/tp/?tp_variant=b&NCK=tripb> in 
Oregon, would develop a championship-level course designed by golfing royalty 
in the form of Bill Coore and two-time major title winnerBen Crenshaw 
<https://cooreandcrenshaw.com/>. The land to be developed on the SSSI amounts 
to just 1.5 hectares (3.7 acres).

There would be no members, only people wanting to knock a small ball around a 
chunk of land with heather-clad mountains in the background and the sound of 
the sea not far away. Another £30 million would go toward a new hotel and 
bolstering local infrastructure. For anyone wanting to make a weekend of it,
Royal Dornoch Golf Club <https://royaldornoch.com/> is about a 10-minute drive 
away.

But on the face of it, Communities for Coul 
<https://www.communities4coul.scot/>, or C4C, the not-for-profit group backing 
the project, has an uphill task convincing Scottish ministers that its proposal 
is for the general good.

A previous iteration in 2020 was turned down, and government advisers aren’t 
in favor of this latest update. Even so, it’s emphasizing how the course would 
preserve rather than damage the area’s ecology, create 400 sustainable jobs and 
boost a wregion losing young people to bigger cities and abroad.
A stem of Baltic rush in Coul Links, the plant at the heart of the opposition 
to the course.Photographer: Michael Vince Kim/Bloomberg
The timeline has accelerated in recent weeks, with a decision possible as soon 
as this month. Following a government request for more information about how 
the proposal ties in with regulations on protected wetlands, C4C filed an 
update noting that invasive species pose an existential threat to the entire 
dune complex if the status quo continues. Plants such as meadowsweet and 
creeping thistle have almost doubled their spread in the past five years, 
hampering the wind’s ability to sculpt and mold the dunes, the report says.

Enough room for golfers?

By Ainslie Chandler 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASHD103ge6E/ainslie-chandler?cmpid=BBD080726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7476>

Until Mark Wahlberg <bbg://people/profile/1963495> emerged as its unlikely 
champion, few people outside Sydney had heard of Moore Park Golf Course.

In 2024 the actor filmed an impassioned plea from its 10th hole, urging 
authorities to save the century-old public course from redevelopment. But after 
years of debate, the New South Wales state government is pressing ahead with 
plans to shrink the 18-hole course by about a third as it seeks to ease a 
housing affordability crisis.
Golfers on the multi-level driving range at Moore Park Golf Course.
Photographer: Robert Wallace/Alamy
The land won’t be used for new homes, though. Instead, 20 hectares (about 28 
soccer pitches) is being turned into public parkland, with walking and running 
tracks and open green space for tens of thousands of nearby residents.

The decision reflects a challenge confronting Sydney and cities around the 
world: finding room for both more homes and open space as populations grow, 
housing becomes less affordable and climate change makesurban greenery 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2021-10-22/urban-rewilding-aids-biodiversity-climate-resilience?cmpid=BBD080726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7476>
 increasingly essential to keeping neighborhoods cool.

“Taking over something like a golf course, which doesn’t really serve very 
many people’s needs, and turning part of it into a more public facility” is a 
“very progressive thing to do,” says Ian Woodcock, senior lecturer in urbanism 
at the University of Sydney. Ideally, public open space should be “accessible 
to and useful for as many different people as possible.”

Europe’s heat toll

By Eamon Farhat 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVTFIbBZVfk/eamon-farhat?cmpid=BBD080726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7476>

Extreme heat has killed more than 25,000 people across Europe this year, 
according to the latest available data compiled by Bloomberg.

Germany recorded the highest toll, with an estimated 11,900 excess deaths 
linked to heat since April, according to theRobert Koch Institute 
<https://www.rki.de/DE/Themen/Gesundheit-und-Gesellschaft/Gesundheitliche-Einflussfaktoren-A-Z/H/Hitze/Bericht_Hitzemortalitaet.html?nn=16965610>
. Data from government agencies and research institutions in Spain, France, the 
UK, Switzerland and the Netherlands put the region’s heat-related death toll at 
over 25,000.

Climate change is increasing the frequency and intensity of heat waves on the 
world’s fastest-warming continent, prompting national health agencies to issue 
repeated warnings. Western Europe has been seared by four heat waves this 
summer, shattering temperature records and denting economic output. The heat is 
set to build again next week, with London forecast to reach 34C by Wednesday 
and Paris as high as 38C.
