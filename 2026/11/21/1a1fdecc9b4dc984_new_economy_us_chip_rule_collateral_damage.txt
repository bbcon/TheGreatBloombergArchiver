When the Trump administration dropped its latest round of restrictions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/us-moves-to-limit-remote-access-to-advanced-ai-chips?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261121&utm_content=11554>
 on advanced artificial intelligence chips this week, it set off shockwaves not 
just in Beijing — their intended target — but also among regional neighbors 
that had made a lucrative business out of serving mainland China’s fast-growing 
demand.

In Jakarta, the chief executive of one of Indonesia’s biggest data centers 
stayed up all night refreshing the US Commerce Department’s website seeking 
details on the new rules. Malaysian and Thai officials urged local operators to 
comply, while airing angst about the economic fallout. At a Morgan Stanley 
conference in Singapore, the chip curbs became the key topic in panel 
discussions as clients sought insight on the impact on the region’sgrowth 
prospects 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-18/singapore-lawrence-wong-transshipment-impact-nvidia-us-chip-curbs-washing-megaspeed?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261121&utm_content=11554>
.

That all reflected the fact that Southeast Asia has emerged as one of the top 
locations in the world for AI data centers. It’s had some major advantages: 
plenty of available land, relatively low building costs, and — up to now — less 
of the kind ofpopular pushback 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/us-stock-futures-diverge-as-chip-selloff-extends-into-second-day?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261121&utm_content=11554>
 that featured in this month’s US elections.

What drew US attention, however, was the region’s role in a sort of arbitrage: 
its ability to provide remote access for Chinese companies that otherwise were 
prevented from getting their hands on high-powered, US-made chips like Nvidia’s 
Blackwell GPUs. With the US now closing an apparent regulatory loophole, that’s 
quickly reshaping the immediate Southeast Asian outlook.

The new Commerce Department rules apply to five nations 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h7k68n2UsSY>, four of which are in Southeast 
Asia — Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia. (The other is Brazil.) The 
newly stringent export licensing requirements are designed to clamp down on 
“higher-than-normal” risks of smuggling for advanced AI chips.

Like many of the trade-war missives 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-17/china-hawks-ascend-in-washington-to-push-new-tech-restrictions?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261121&utm_content=11554>
 since President Donald Trump returned to the White House, questions remain on 
whether — and how — the new rules will be enforced, along with thescope for 
negotiation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-19/trump-suggests-us-can-sell-blackwell-chips-to-china-in-future?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261121&utm_content=11554>
.

That will do little to soothe anxieties in Southeast Asia, however, where the 
rapid buildout of data centers has become an economic lifeline in a year marred 
by the global oil shock triggered by the US-Israel war with Iran.

In Malaysia, by far the regional leader, data-center investment is nearing 
$100 billion thanks to big names such as Amazon.com Inc.’s Amazon Web Services, 
Microsoft Corp., Alphabet Inc., Bridge Data Centres Pte. and DayOne Data 
Centers Ltd. KPMG earlier forecast that construction activity could create some 
$24 billion in economic output annually by 2030 for the country, while 
operations could add a further $10 billion and 31,000 jobs.

“It is still early days, but the rule could be consequential,” said Lavanya 
Venkateswaran <bbg://people/profile/17367241>, an economist at Oversea-Chinese 
Banking Corp. in Singapore. She estimates that China-linked operators account 
for more than 30% of the approved data-center capacity in Malaysia and 
Thailand, which will likely be the hardest hit by the new restrictions.

It wouldn’t be the first time Southeast Asia faced potential collateral damage 
from US measures targeting China’s growing trade dominance. The region’s 
geographic proximity to and integrated supply chains with Beijing means it has 
inevitably been caught in the crossfire between the world’s top two economies.

In April 2025, Trump targeted the region with some of his highest tariffs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-20/trump-s-global-tariffs-struck-down-by-us-supreme-court?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261121&utm_content=11554>
, and threatened an additional40% trans-shipment levy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-23/rerouted-us-imports-avoiding-trump-s-tariffs-top-300-billion?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261121&utm_content=11554>
 on those nations that divert China-made goods to the US.

In a speech at the APEC CEO Summit in Shenzhen this week, Malaysian Prime 
Minister Anwar Ibrahim appeared to take a swipe at the latest round of 
protectionist measures. “Relatively more successful” countries should be 
prepared to allow “opportunities for those in emerging economies or developing 
countries to then participate in that process” of development, he said.

Perhaps in an attempt to sugarcoat the pill, the US pledged to help develop 
Malaysia’s sovereign AI cloud and offered$50 million in support 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-19/us-malaysia-to-strengthen-sovereign-ai-cloud-development?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261121&utm_content=11554>
 following a meeting between Trump and Anwar.
Anwar IbrahimPhotographer: Qilai Shen/Bloomberg
Not all is dim for the outlook for Southeast Asia’s data centers. Countries 
are pushing to localize data for government, state firms and sensitive 
industries, and the region’s $300-billion digital economy requires sizable 
cloud computing. American hyperscalers turned away by NIMBYism in the US could 
still find a home in the region.

But AI training and inference, driven by Chinese demand, has been the 
fastest-growing segment by far and presented the most promising gains. The risk 
for the region is this may only be the start of more US scrutiny, with Congress 
deliberatingseveral proposals 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-12/openai-joins-groundswell-of-support-for-ai-chip-control-measures?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261121&utm_content=11554>
 to tighten security around chips and other sensitive technologies.

“The direction of travel is for more, not fewer restrictions,” says Ariana 
Salvatore <bbg://people/profile/21587084>, Morgan Stanley head of US public 
policy strategy.
