Deep Dive: Old Tankers and New Tactics
The Al RayyanSource: Marine Traffic
Six months into a war that sowed upheaval in the Persian Gulf, Qatar found 
itself stuck. By late August, even limited shipments from the gas heavyweight 
had all but stopped following a projectile attack on one of its tankers outside 
the Strait of Hormuz.Pressure was growing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/qatar-reinvents-lng-trade-to-restore-global-clout-even-as-iran-war-drags?cmpid=101426_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10068>
, not only from energy-starved clients but also from inside an economy built on 
regular deliveries.

 * Then senior executives at QatarEnergy saw a potential fix — one of the 
oldest vessels in the fleet. Al Rayyan had been idling for days when the order 
came through. With security assurances from the US Navy in hand, the captain 
was given the green light but was told to sail out quietly, without 
transponders or lights, testing a vital workaround.
 * It worked. The tanker slipped through and headed toward the port of Sohar 
in Oman on Sept. 10. A few days later, it was met by another Qatar-controlled 
vessel that would carry the cargo of liquefied natural gas to its final 
destination: China.
 * In interviews with more than two dozen traders and officials, Bloomberg 
pieced together Qatar’s efforts to steadily restore its position as a leading 
LNG exporter, even without a clear end in sight for the conflict between the US 
and Iran that has destabilized the region. Their accounts, provided on 
condition of anonymity given the sensitive nature of the issue, offer a glimpse 
into how a gas powerhouse has begun to adapt to a new and uncertain reality.
