China Real Estate Revamp

More than two decades after China’s central bank recommended banning <> 
property developers from selling residential units before they were completed, 
the nation’s regulators have finally takenexactly that step 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-28/china-pushes-forward-home-sale-overhaul-as-crisis-drags-on?cmpid=BBD090126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8366>
.

In the 21 years between those two events, China saw a mammoth property surge 
and adevastating crash 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/china-s-property-crisis-from-evergrande-collapse-to-spiraling-prices?cmpid=BBD090126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8366>
 that’s yet to hit bottom. The so-called pre-sales system was part of it.

Developers were able to start selling future homes within months of acquiring 
land, and eager buyers forked over most of their payments once construction had 
“topped out” — that is, finished the shell of the building up to its highest 
point. Real-estate firms then used a swath of that cash to go out and acquire 
more land.

When the leverage merry-go-round came to an end 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-10-08/what-china-s-three-red-lines-mean-for-property-firms-quicktake?cmpid=BBD090126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8366>
 with a regulatory crackdown on developer borrowing in the early 2020s, untold 
numbers of properties were yet to be completed. Many buyers were left both 
without a new home and without their payment, financially ruined.

Until the bottom fell out, nearly nine out of every 10 homes in China were 
“pre-sold,” according to Bloombergreporting 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2021-12-12/abandoned-projects-shatter-confidence-in-china-s-housing-market?cmpid=BBD090126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8366>
 back in 2021.

Under the new rules, published Friday, from China’s housing-development 
ministry and other agencies, homebuyers’ payments will be placed in escrow. If 
the developer fails to deliver the property on schedule, the buyer has the 
right to terminate the contract and the purchase money must be refunded. And 
pre-sales can go ahead only after topping out.

To some extent, this is shutting the barn door after the horse has fled. An 
official Xinhua news agencyQ&A 
<https://www.news.cn/20260828/b2228e17249b48e3b21a1459430dac87/c.html> 
highlighted that, at this point, new-home sales are smaller thanexisting-home 
transactions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/china-new-home-prices-fall-at-faster-pace-damping-rebound-hopes?cmpid=BBD090126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8366>
, so China’s market has matured. The most leveraged private-sector developers 
have already long since crashed, and upending their model now has much less of 
an impact it once would have.

Even so, “there is likely to be a sizable drop in new projects 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/china-new-home-prices-fall-at-faster-pace-damping-rebound-hopes?cmpid=BBD090126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8366>
 in 2027,” once the current pipeline of developments — grandfathered in under 
the previous rules — runs out, according to Xiaoxi Zhang at Gavekal 
Dragonomics. Pre-sales still “accounted for about 75% of new housing sales at 
the end of 2025,” she said.

That may also worsen the situation for local and municipal governments, which 
have already seen revenue from land sales plummet. But further constricting 
supply will also surely bring closer a stabilization of prices.

“For policymakers still vowing to reduce real-estate risks, another drop in 
property starts may be seen as a price worth paying,” Zhang wrote in a note 
Monday.

Need-to-Know Research

In a potentially worrying sign of how inflation expectations can become sticky,
new analysis 
<https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2026/08/consumer-vibe-check-gas-prices-and-inflation-expectations/>
 from the San Francisco Fed indicates that consumers appear to respond more 
strongly to a surge in gasoline prices than they do when those levels come back 
down.

“Our results suggest that gasoline price pressures may leave a more persistent 
imprint on household inflation outlooks when prices climb than when they 
subside,” Taerin Kim, a research associate, and Yeji Sung, an economist, wrote 
in a note published Monday. That’s important because “gasoline prices are among 
the most salient and volatile expenses consumers face,” they said. After all, 
they’re posted on large signs along most roads.

“When gas prices rise sharply, people may interpret the increase not just as a 
price change in this category but as a signal about inflation more generally,” 
the duo wrote. “This pattern is strongest among lower-income and less-educated 
households. Analysis also suggests that increases in expected gas price growth 
are associated with greater perceived inflation uncertainty.”
