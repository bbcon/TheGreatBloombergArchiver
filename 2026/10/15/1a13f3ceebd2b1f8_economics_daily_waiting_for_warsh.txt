Waiting for Warsh

September’s hot inflation data 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/us-consumer-prices-climb-by-most-since-april-on-fuel-costs?cmpid=BBD101526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10118>
 triggered a flurry of bets that the Federal Reserve will hike interest rates 
this month. If its chairman wants to express a view, he’ll have a global stage 
to do it from.

Kevin Warsh is due to speak on the last full day of the International Monetary 
Fund meetings in Bangkok, in a Friday morning fireside chat with fund chief 
Kristalina Georgieva.

Investors are “waiting for Warsh tomorrow to bless the Fed going forward on 
hikes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/fed-s-goolsbee-says-inflation-discouraging-warns-of-overheating?cmpid=BBD101526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10118>
,” said Meghan Swiber, a US rates strategist at Bank of America. The inflation 
data displays “broad-based strength and it will be harder for the Fed to look 
through.”
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-14/hot-inflation-lifts-fed-rate-hike-bets-video?cmpid=BBD101526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10118>
An October hike appeared unlikely in recent weeks after policymakers signaled 
there was no rush. Most traders still expect the Fed to wait till December. And 
Warsh has vowed not to steer markets with his own near-term outlook.

Still, the inflation surprise will likely heighten scrutiny of what he says 
Friday. So will the calendar. With the Fed due to enter its so-called media 
blackout period the following day, ahead of the Oct. 27-28 policy meeting, 
Warsh’s IMF appearance is essentially the last chanceto send a signal on the 
likely decision 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/fed-s-warsh-to-get-final-word-in-bangkok-as-inflation-roars-back?cmpid=BBD101526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10118>
, asEnda Curran 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASJnLeYEat4/enda-curran?cmpid=BBD101526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10118>
 andYe Xie 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AOaIfVMEEws/ye-xie?cmpid=BBD101526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10118>
 report.

Surging fuel costs were the chief driver of the US price acceleration. They’re 
hurting almost everywhere else too, a key theme at the IMF this week.

The fund’s own forecast is for the inflation spike to ease in 2027, with the 
global rate dropping back down 4.1% from 4.7%. With few signs of an end to the 
US war on Iran, which set off the energy shock, prices could stay higher for 
longer.

Adding those pressures to the economy’s resilient growth, IMF expectations are 
shifting toward tighter monetary policy.

 Georgieva’s own advice for central bankers in general is to maintain a 
“prudently hawkish bias.” And she praised Warsh, in aBloomberg TV interview 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/fed-s-warsh-earns-imf-praise-for-focusing-on-price-stability?cmpid=BBD101526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10118>
 last week, for his readiness to pursue price stability “not only with words 
but also with actions.”

Need-to-Know Research

China’s State Council — the equivalent of the government’s cabinet — last 
month issued the latest in a series of calls from Beijing for big companies to
stop delaying payments 
<https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/content/202609/content_7080627.htm> to small and 
medium sized enterprises. The trouble is, they’re addressing the symptom of a 
much bigger issue.

That’s according to Andrew Batson and Xiaoxi Zhang at the research group 
Gavekal. The “arrears problem” is worsening not because officials are failing 
to step up their efforts. It’s “because the finances of many companies are 
deteriorating in a persistently weak economy,” they wrote in a note Wednesday. 
They cited a survey that estimates how long it would take for firms to collect 
outstanding receivables at the current pace. The latest reading is a record 
high.

While not directly comparable, the 72-day average for August is longer than 
the 60-day arrears standard the government set for large companies. The thing 
is, some firms simply cannot afford to make payments on time, the Gavekal 
analysts said. “The years ofsluggish growth 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-09-26/china-s-economic-regime-shift-may-sow-global-risk-new-economy?cmpid=BBD101526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10118>
 that have followed the property bust and the end of the pandemic have resulted 
in steadily growing financial stress for many companies.”
