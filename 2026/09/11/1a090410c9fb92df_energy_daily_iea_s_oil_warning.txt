Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Grant Smith explores the outlook for the oil market after the IEA 
warned demand may have to shrink further as war in the Middle East persists.

When the Iran war started back in February, oil markets were consumed with the 
immediate shock 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-01/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-march-2?cmpid=BBBXT091126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8803>
.

The once-unthinkable closure of the Strait of Hormuz; Middle East giants such 
as Saudi Arabia forced to make vast production cuts; and a swift surge in crude 
prices above $120 a barrel.

After a while, that initial convulsion appeared to fade somewhat.

As Persian Gulf exporters turned to backup pipelines and improvised solutions 
likeclandestine shuttle runs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-27/hormuz-oil-flows-rising-as-gulf-giants-ramp-up-accelerates?cmpid=BBBXT091126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8803>
 — while major consumer China tempered its oil imports — it often seemed like 
the industry had learned to live with theunprecedented turmoil 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-12/iran-war-is-causing-biggest-ever-oil-market-disruption-iea-says?cmpid=BBBXT091126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8803>
. By July, futures were almost back in the $60s.

But as the conflict drags on far longer than US President Donald Trump 
initially predicted 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-09/trump-insists-i-have-a-plan-as-oil-prices-surge-on-iran-war?cmpid=BBBXT091126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8803>
, its cumulative toll on the energy world is becoming ever more visible.

Oil prices surged again above $100 a barrel this week as renewed fighting hit 
tankers in the Gulf. More crucially for consumers, the price ofworkhorse fuel 
diesel 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-11/us-diesel-prices-rise-past-6-a-gallon-for-first-time-ever?cmpid=BBBXT091126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8803>
 soared past $6 a gallon in the US today for the first time in history.
A driver fills a vehicle with diesel at a Shell gas station in Seattle. The 
fuel rose above $6 a gallon for the first time on Friday.Photographer: Bloomberg
The International Energy Agency 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-11/iea-warns-oil-demand-may-have-to-fall-further-as-iran-war-drags?cmpid=BBBXT091126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8803>
, which has led efforts to plug the supply gap by coordinating amassive release 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-11/iea-confirms-huge-release-of-emergency-oil-stockpiles?cmpid=BBBXT091126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8803>
 of emergency oil reserves, is warning that worse may be to come.

In its latest monthly report, the IEA once again slashed estimates for global 
oil consumption, saying the crisis is as grave as any of the biggest shocks of 
the past six decades.

The agency made even more swingeing cuts to its supply forecast, acknowledging 
that the war’s disruption will now persist to at least the end of the year.

That could be painful. Earlier this week, Saudi Arabia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-10/saudis-tell-opec-that-output-slumped-again-to-lowest-since-1990?cmpid=BBBXT091126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8803>
 informed its OPEC colleagues that it had been compelled to reduce production 
to the lowest level since the 1990 Gulf War.

The resulting hole in stockpiles will be larger and last longer than 
previously thought, the IEA predicts. And the burden to bring markets into 
balance may land squarely on households.

“Further demand reductions may be required in the coming months to close the 
gap,” the agency said.

—Grant Smith, Bloomberg News

Chart of the day

European natural gas prices headed for their biggest weekly gain since July 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-11/europe-gas-set-for-biggest-weekly-gain-since-july-as-war-expands?cmpid=BBBXT091126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8803>
 as the conflict in the Middle East intensified, stoking fears of deeper and 
longer disruptions to energy flows. The war’s continued impact on liquefied 
natural gas supplies is squeezing the market just as Europe works to refill 
depleted inventories before winter. Prices have more than doubled during the 
war.
