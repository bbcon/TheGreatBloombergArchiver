Hi, this is Allen Wan in Beijing.

I have been punched by a humanoid robot, kicked by another and had my emotions 
toyed with by a female automaton during tech conferences in the Chinese capital 
over the past year.

It is a reflection of not just the developing nature of high-tech gadgets but 
also how much money and attention embodied intelligence is getting inanother 
emerging industry 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-15/china-sends-robots-out-into-the-world-to-learn-how-to-be-human?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
 Beijing hopes to dominate.

That is why when the US said this week that it will tighten curbs on some 
foreign-made robots 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-29/us-risks-broadening-china-tech-clash-with-robot-inverter-curbs?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
 (without naming China even though it’s the dominant player), it was seen as 
another shot across the bow intended to rein in Beijing’s tech ambitions.

Already, the US has used plenty of sticks in an attempt to slow China’s 
progress in AI, ranging from export controls on advanced semiconductors, 
Pentagon bans, smuggling probes, andUS politicians warning Apple 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-29/senators-warn-apple-not-to-buy-memory-chips-from-chinese-firms?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
, the world’s most valuable company, not to use Chinese chips even if the 
products are destined exclusively for that market.

It has now become a common refrain — China makes a tech breakthrough and the 
US pushes back.

And Beijing had plenty to crow about this week. Chipmaker CXMT soared 466% in 
its trading debut 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-26/china-memory-champion-cxmt-set-to-debut-after-9-8-billion-ipo?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
 to become China’s largest onshore-listed company, getting a massive infusion 
of capital to challenge foreign suppliers in DRAM chips and making many people 
rich along the way.

Its founder’s wealth soared, employees may get to split a bonus pool of more 
than $5 billion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-27/cxmt-founder-zhu-yiming-pledges-5-6-billion-in-worker-bonuses-after-china-ipo?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
, the DeepSeek founder’s hedge fund investmentreaped $121 million in paper gains
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-28/deepseek-founder-s-hedge-funds-are-among-big-winners-of-cxmt-ipo?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
 and even a province got in on the act, postinga $192 billion windfall 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-28/cxmt-listing-creates-192-billion-windfall-for-chinese-province?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
 from its investment.

CXMT’s shares are holding up quite well after the first-day pop, in contrast 
to Elon Musk’s SpaceX, which hasretreated since its record-setting debut 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-28/spacex-falls-20-below-ipo-price-erasing-1-2-trillion-value?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
.

There’s more. Chinese startup Moonshot AI, which released a new model that 
drew global attention for its powerful capabilities and triggered a selloff 
earlier this month, made its Kimi K3 model available for public download,
expanding its reach and influence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-27/what-is-moonshot-ai-s-kimi-k3-model-and-why-is-it-making-waves?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
 in the global open software community.

To top it off, the Information reported that a Shanghai-based company has 
started making immersion deep ultraviolet lithography tools,sending ASML’s 
shares sliding 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-27/asml-slides-after-report-of-china-beginning-duv-tool-production?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
 on concern China is developing cutting-edge semiconductor technology on its 
own.

The US is clearly concerned after seeing China become a leader in electric 
vehicles, solar equipment and batteries — especially since AI has far-reaching 
implications beyond commerce to include military applications.

And the stakes are high for investors around the world. The global AI stock 
rally has been predicated partly on the notion that chipmakers such as Samsung 
Electronics, SK Hynix and Micron will continue to dominate the supply chain and 
not be challenged by CXMT.

Then there’s the need for massive investments by OpenAI, Anthropic and others 
to pay off and not be undercut by cheaper Chinese competitors such as Moonshot.
CXMT offices in Anhui province.Photographer: CFOTO/Future Publishing/Getty 
Images
Maybe the fight over AI ends in a whimper just like the trade war seems to 
have. But the tech arena does feel very, very different. This week, Beijing 
warned Washington against sanctioning Chinese AI companies, drawing a line 
before Chinese leader Xi Jinping travels to the US to meet Donald Trump,a trip 
set for September 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/china-shares-concern-over-restrictive-us-measures-with-bessent?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
.

This week has provided a reminder that China is all-in on AI — regardless of 
US pushback. It is key to its multi-trillion-dollar infrastructure push,known 
as the Six Networks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-26/how-china-plans-to-supercharge-its-ai-ambitions-with-six-networks-program?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
, to lay the foundations for long-term growth in the world’s second-biggest 
economy.

For that reason, we can expect yet more fireworks to erupt between Beijing and 
Washington down the road.

Journey West

Hi, this is Allen again.

I recently returned from a weeklong trip to the roof of the world, otherwise 
known as Tibet.

It was my first time back in more than 30 years, since I was a teen fascinated 
with adventure after reading James Hilton’s novel Lost Horizon about the 
Tibet-inspiredmythical kingdom of Shangri-La 
<https://www.britannica.com/topic/Lost-Horizon-novel-by-Hilton>.

I didn’t have to figure out complicated and dangerous ways to enter Lhasa like 
I did back in 1988 when there were soldiers everywhere due to unrest.

This time, the Chinese government invited several foreign journalists to a 
curated tour of the region, which is subject to security and religious 
restrictionslike its northern neighbor Xinjiang 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-03/how-china-is-using-tourism-to-rebrand-xinjiang?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
.

This included a stop in the city of Shannan, often referred to as the cradle 
of Tibetan civilization. We got to see palaces, museums, cultural shows, 
traditional medicine companies and a winery.

Along the way there were plenty of signs featuring slogans about ethnic unity 
and huge posters of Xi, whohas linked his legacy to the region 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-07-29/xi-s-167-billion-tibet-mega-dam-promises-to-spur-china-s-economy?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
. There were also references to past Dalai Lamas but none to the current one.
A memorial to Chinese leaders past and present in Lhasa, in western China.
Photographer: Allen Wan/Bloomberg via Bloomberg
What has changed? Pretty much everything.

The capital of Lhasa is as modern as any medium-sized Chinese city, complete 
with nice hotels, malls and fast-food chains like McDonald’s and KFC.

That’s unlike what I experienced back in 1988, when the only Western hotel to 
be seen was the Holiday Inn, where I got to try a Yak burger. Back then, I 
stayed at a hole-in-the-wall guest house where I watched reruns of the old US 
cop showHunter in Mandarin, while drinking Yak milk tea.

Now, there are tons of new low-rise buildings with Tibetan motifs. A boom in 
infrastructure building has yielded new highways and a railway that connects 
Lhasa to neighboring Qinghai province, cutting the travel time to a mere 13 
hours from what used to be several days overland by truck.

Beijing has poured hundreds of billions of dollars into Tibet over the past 
decade for railways, highways and energy projects. Construction ofthe world’s 
biggest hydroelectric dam 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-07-21/why-china-s-167-billion-mega-dam-project-in-tibet-is-so-controversial?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
, a project that’s set to be far larger than the Three Gorges Dam, is estimated 
to cost around 1.2 trillion yuan ($167 billion).

All the new investment is helping to alleviate poverty and increase incomes. 
One 70-year-old weaver named Danzang Luozhui at a collective said she was 
struggling in the countryside before being relocated by the authorities and 
given a new apartment and job.

The Pazhu winery in Shannan, which holds the Guinness World Record for the 
commercial vineyard at the highest altitude (3,600 meters), is boosting incomes 
and allowing Tibetans to work closer to home, said Suolang Bianba, an employee 
who used to be an itinerant construction worker.

At a community center, a 73-year-old former nurse named Kalsang Lhamo was 
learning to write Tibetan script after doing without for much of her life. The 
older Tibetans we met spoke little to no Mandarin. The younger generation, like 
our native guide, was more fluent, speaking Tibetan and Mandarin — the result 
of compulsory education.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/games/pointed?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
At Jokhang Temple, one of Tibet’s most revered shrines, we asked a senior monk 
named La Ba about the next Dalai Lama once the 91-year-old now in exile passes 
away.

He rejected the notion of having two Dalai Lamas, one approved by Beijing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-07-02/dalai-lama-how-does-succession-work-will-politics-china-affect-decision?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
 and another in permanent exile like the current 14th Dalai Lama, Tenzin 
Gyatso. He didn’t want to say much more than that.

There were exhibits at the Xizang Museum in Lhasa displaying the first several 
Dalai Lamas, including historical artifacts depicting the conferment of an 
imperial seal to the 7th Dalai Lama that purports to show longstanding central 
government authority over Tibet.

Other exhibits aimed at showcasing ethnic unity included murals of the 
marriage between Tang dynasty Princess Wencheng to Songtsen Gampo, king of an 
ancient kingdom. Toward the end of the museum tour, we saw exhibits on 20th 
century Western imperialism under the banner of “Opposing Separatism, 
Maintaining Unity.”

And that’s the rub of it. For all of Tibet’s economic progress and 
modernization in the past few decades, there is still a need for constant 
reminders from Beijing to show China’s historical links with Tibet as subtle 
justification for current rule.

There may no longer be soldiers patrolling with rifles as I saw back in 1988, 
but in their place were tech surveillance and slogans along roads and highways 
commemorating the anniversary of Tibet’s “liberation.” And there was the 
massive billboard at a public square right across from the Potala Palace 
showing a youngish-looking Xi smiling at tourists.

Behind the Great Firewall

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

This week China both celebrated and did a bit of soul-searching after two of 
its nationals won one of the world’s most prestigious prizes in mathematics.

Wang Hong and Deng Yu claimed the Fields Medal alongside two people from the 
US and Canada. Often described as the Nobel Prize of mathematics, the award is 
presented every four years to outstanding mathematicians under the age of 40.

Their accomplishment sparked an outpouring of pride in China, making headlines 
in state media and trending on social platforms like Weibo, where many users 
hailed it as a milestone.

“For the Chinese mathematical community, this is a dream we have longed to 
realize for decades,” Shing-Tung Yau, the first Chinese-American to win the 
Fields Medal, told state media.

Much of the celebration centered on Wang — just the third woman to ever 
receive the honor in its 90-year history.

“Such brilliant, vibrant female power,” one Weibo user wrote, attracting more 
than 5,000 likes.
Wang Hong in Philadelphia on July 23.Photographer: Li Rui/Xinhua/Getty Images 
via Xinhua News Agency
The mathematicians’ career paths also prompted some to wonder why the nation’s 
brightest minds have to head abroad to win these kinds of accolades. Both Wang 
and Deng studied at the prestigious Peking University and now work at American 
universities.

For some online commentators, their success reinforced the enduring pull of US 
academia.

“Top-tier talent continues to gravitate there, highlighting the significant 
gap that our domestic academic sector still needs to bridge,” a Weibo user 
wrote.

China has rapidly emerged as a science and tech powerhouse 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-07-17/xi-positions-china-to-lead-the-ai-rollout-and-profit-from-it?cmpid=BBD073126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7117>
. As tensions with the US have grown, a growing number of Chinese researchers 
have returned from Western institutions to take up positions at home.

Still, some in China argued that the academic system remains too bureaucratic 
to nurture top talent and breakthrough research.

“It’s a good thing that Wang Hong and Deng Yu went abroad,” said another Weibo 
user. “Otherwise, they’d still be bogged down worrying about academic 
promotions, blocked from applying because they aren’t ‘old enough,’ and left 
with no time for real work because they’re tied up with administrative chores 
and entertaining bosses at social dinners.”
