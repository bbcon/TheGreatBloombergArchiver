Cleaning the Ganges

By Shruti Srivastava 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASufBdA7hXc/shruti-srivastava?cmpid=BBD110626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11048>
,Krishna Karra 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWss3yq6qEg/krishna-karra?cmpid=BBD110626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11048>
 andLou Del Bello 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWe3VxBwoBc/lou-del-bello?cmpid=BBD110626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11048>

The boat pushes off from Kanpur’s riverfront promenade in the early hours, 
slipping onto the Ganges as the northern Indian city slowly gives way to a 
quieter, more industrial edge. The four-hour journey along the riverbank ends 
in Jajmau by early afternoon. The boatman, practiced and unhurried, steers 
through the current with ease. He has seen this before: The steady stream of 
officials, inspectors, and researchers who come to test the river, measure it, 
and leave.

As they move downstream, the river tells its own story. Small drains appear 
one after another, trickling straight into the Ganges, most carrying household 
sewage. Then, near the tannery belt of Gajju Purwa, the water darkens. A larger 
outflow carrying waste cuts into the river, turning the surface a murky 
black-blue streaked with acrid-smelling froth. The air thickens with a sharp, 
chemical stench. The water churns sluggishly, alive with insects.
People gather at Har Ki Pauri, Haridwar’s most prominent Ganges ghat.
Photographer: Atul Loke/Bloomberg
Conditions have improved over the years, the boatman says, but he insists some 
of the biggest drains are still opened after dark. At one beside a cremation 
ghat, the channel lay dry during the day. By dusk, black water was coursing 
into the river.

For more than a decade, cleaning the Ganges — known in India as Ganga — has 
been a crucial priority for Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s government, which 
has allocated more than $4.5 billion for programs including the construction of 
hundreds of sewage treatment plants and other infrastructure. Yet much of the 
river remains dangerously polluted, carrying levels of sewage and industrial 
waste that threaten the health and livelihoods of communities in a basin that 
supports hundreds of millions of people and generatesabout 40% 
<https://www.decadeonrestoration.org/press-release/un-recognizes-indian-government-initiative-restore-ganges-river-special-award>
 of the nation’s economic output.

The reason, reporting suggests, lies not in any single failure but in a 
combination of patchy enforcement, explosive population growth, sprawling 
urbanization, a reliance on state governments to turn national ambition into 
action, and a challenge far larger than policymakers appreciated when the 
program was launched.

To understand what the billions have delivered, Bloomberg News used satellite 
imagery to build an independent, decade-long record of the river’s health 
across nearly 2,000 kilometers. The analysis tracked turbidity, a measure of 
how muddy the water is; algae, which thrive on nutrients from sewage and 
fertilizer runoff; and shifts in surface temperature to look at where the water 
is heating or cooling.

Together, the data reveals that, across much of the Ganges, the water is no 
clearer than it was a decade ago. In the months after the monsoon, nearly 80% 
of the river is becoming muddier. The investigation also went beyond what 
satellites could see, testing water collected from the river’s industrial 
heartlands to its sacred cities and finding contaminants at levels far beyond 
permissible limits.

India’s government says it has made significant progress in cleaning the 
Ganges, though acknowledges much remains to be done. “We want results,” India’s 
Water Minister CR Patil, who oversees the project, said at his government 
bungalow in New Delhi. “I am hopeful that before the 2029 national election, 
the Ganga water would be fit for bathing for all,” he said, putting the goal 
squarely against the next general vote, when Modi could seek a fourth term.
Waste-laden water flows near the banks of a cremation ground in Kanpur.
Photographer: Atul Loke/ Bloomberg
The Ganges is India’s holiest river and one of its most vital economic 
arteries, sustainingmore than 650 million 
<https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2109078&reg=48&lang=2> 
people, irrigating one of the world’s most productive agricultural regions and 
binding together faith, commerce and daily life across northern and eastern 
India. A decade ago, Modi made restoring the river a defining promise of his 
first election campaign. Since then, roughly430.6 billion 
<https://nmcg.nic.in/writereaddata/fileupload/36_WebsiteMPRforMonthofSeptember2026.pdf>
 Indian rupees ($4.5 billion) had been allocated to the task by the end of 
September, though only a little over half has been spent, partly due to 
bureaucratic delays, a hybrid financing model and slow local execution.

The scale of Modi’s effort dwarfs what came before. His administration has 
authorizedmore than ten times 
<https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1554782&reg=48&lang=2> as 
much for cleaning the Ganges as successive governments deployed on earlier 
programs spanning nearly three decades from 1985 to 2014.

Cleaning the Ganges is not only an environmental or religious project: It is a 
test of whether India can protect public health and economic opportunity in a 
region that supports a population larger than most countries.

Some aspects of state capacity have strengthened under Modi, particularly 
digital programs and social safety nets, while efforts requiring sustained 
coordination across different levels of government have proven more difficult, 
said Arvind Subramanian, a senior fellow at the Washington-based Peterson 
Institute for International Economics who served as chief economic adviser to 
the Modi government from 2014 to 2018. Programs that receive concentrated 
political attention tend to produce better results than those requiring 
sustained bureaucratic follow-through, he said.
Residents collect water from a hand pump in Jana village.Photographer: Atul 
Loke/ Bloomberg
Improving the river is especially challenging because it requires political 
leaders, bureaucrats, and state and local governments to work in tandem despite 
differing incentives and priorities, Subramanian said. “That’s kind of India in 
a nutshell,” he said. “More and more, the prominent and visible stuff gets done 
somewhat well, but the ongoing stuff always is lagging behind in terms of 
implementation, governance and accountability.”

That may also leave the Ganges at a disadvantage against more conspicuous 
infrastructure projects, he said. “That’s why probably it’s inherently a more 
difficult task, and maybe less visible and less of a priority than building 
roads and airports.”

Pausing the data center rush

By Kyle Stock 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ARKz8x17rNs/kyle-stock?cmpid=BBD110626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11048>
 andMiquéla V Thornton 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXbWgIM0aSA/miquela-thornton?cmpid=BBD110626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11048>

Data center opposition had been building across the country, one moratorium or 
blocked project at a time. Then, virtually overnight, the midterm election 
tilted a broad swath of the regulatory map against the AI buildout.

Newly elected governors in Ohio, Georgia, Michigan, New Mexico and Texas 
campaigned on a promise to stop — or at least pause — data center construction 
statewide, while those in New York and Oregon pledged to prolong pauses already 
in place. Gubernatorial candidates calling for a moratorium on data centers 
also lead in Alaska and Kansas, though those races have not been called.

All told, about 40% of pending data center capacity in the US is planned to be 
built in states where newly elected governors have promised to stop or pause 
such construction, according to data from Cleanview, a market intelligence 
company. That’s on top of 46 local ballot measures approved or on track to pass 
that also stall development, according to Bloomberg Economics; at least 17 of 
those are outright bans on large-scale data centers.

“It was obviously not a very good night if you’re a data center developer,” 
said Justin Teresi, a Bloomberg Intelligence policy analyst.
