Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
senior reporter Kevin Crowley looks at the implications of a climate lawsuit 
against Big Oil companies before the US Supreme Court.

Big Oil is seeking to end more than a decade of US climate litigation as 
Suncor Energy Inc. and ExxonMobil Holdings Corp. ask the Supreme Court to tossa 
case alleging they misled the public 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-05/supreme-court-grapples-with-climate-suits-against-oil-companies?cmpid=BBBXT100626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9761>
 about fossil fuels’ role in global warming.

Years in the making, the suit originated with litigants representing Boulder, 
Colorado, and is a test of whether states can seek damages from oil companies 
for the effects of rising seas, drought and violent weather.

Opening the floodgates to such lawsuits would be a major blow to the industry, 
resembling the high-stakes litigation against Big Tobacco in the 1990s that 
culminated in a $200 billion settlement. The justices are due to rule by late 
June 2027.

Given the court’s conservative majority, it’s unlikely Boulder will secure a 
clear win. But lawyers for the city got a boost last week when Justice Samuel 
Alito, a conservative expected to be receptive to the oil industry’s arguments,
recused himself 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/alito-says-recusal-in-climate-case-is-prudent-but-not-required?cmpid=BBBXT100626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9761>
 from the suit.

That raised the possibility of a 4-4 split among the justices, which would 
leave the lower court’s ruling intact and allow Boulder’s case to proceed. But 
without a Supreme Court majority, the decision wouldn’t establish a rule for 
similar cases nationwide.
Justice Samuel AlitoPhotographer: Erin Schaff/The New York Times/Bloomberg
Even if the Supreme Court rules in Boulder’s favor, that would only allow the 
lawsuit to go back to state court, where the companies have consistently argued 
that climate change cannot be ascribed to individual firms because it results 
from human activity around the world.

In oral arguments Monday, Chief Justice John Roberts voiced concern about 
letting the case proceed.

“Presumably, if you prevail, the next day a municipality in every single state 
will file a lawsuit,” he told lawyers representing Boulder. But he also noted 
the court has long allowed similar suits over out-of-state activities that have 
an in-state effect.

With strong federal regulation of CO2 emissions proving difficult to enact 
under both Democratic and Republican administrations, environmentalists have 
long hoped the legal system would provide an outlet for climate action.

Now the moment is getting closer.

“The stakes are profound for both sides,” said Andrew Logan, a senior director 
at Ceres, a sustainability advocacy nonprofit. “For the fossil industry this 
case could be a death blow to most if not all climate litigation. For the 
climate community it could be the door to keeping those cases alive.”

—Kevin Crowley, Bloomberg News
