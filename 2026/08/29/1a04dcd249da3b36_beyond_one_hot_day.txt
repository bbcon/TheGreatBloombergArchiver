Heat’s lasting toll

by Zahra Hirji 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVypnhGtqak/zahra-hirji?cmpid=BBD082926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260829&utm_content=8306>

Earlier this summer, I wrote about how Arizona’s Maricopa County was making 
progress in preventing heat-related deaths. One of the reasons is that medical 
experts have rolled out a novel life-saving intervention called cold water 
immersion therapy. This involves quickly dunking someone struggling with heat 
stroke, the most extreme form of heat illness, in an icy slurry to bring down 
their core body temperature fast.

“Without exaggeration, what we’re hearing from partners in the healthcare 
setting is that patients who may never have left the hospital or would likely 
have been leaving with some type of permanent disability are now walking out 
under their own power,” David Hondula, Phoenix’s director of heat response and 
mitigation,told me at the time. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-11/arizona-s-maricopa-county-heat-death-prevention-faces-new-threat?cmpid=BBD082926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260829&utm_content=8306>

Up to that point, I’d reported extensively on how people can prevent heat 
illness – and how they die from it – but not on what happens to those who get 
really sick and survive. How quickly are they returning to school, to work, to 
daily life? What does recovery entail?

These questions led me and my colleague Emma Court on a search for heat stroke 
survivors willing to talk about their plight. We also interviewed the experts 
helping them recover. What we discovered is that no two recoveries from heat 
stroke are the same, and sometimes the impacts are long-lasting.

Take Rebecca Ochoa. When she was 13 years old, she experienced heat stroke 
while trying out for her high school volleyball team in the August heat in 
Texas. It wreaked havoc on her body: She developed a severe related condition 
called rhabdomyolysis, which involves muscle breakdown. Doctors were concerned 
about possible kidney damage. She was hospitalized for about two weeks.

After suffering a heat stroke as a teenager, Rebecca Ochoa was hospitalized 
and participated in testing at the Korey Stringer Institute as part of her 
recovery.Source: Courtesy of Ochoa via Source: Courtesy photos

That was 11 years ago. “I am still not the same as before this happened, 
physically or mentally,” Ochoa says. “I constantly battle dehydration, fatigue, 
nosebleeds, and even passing out during the warmer months.” She never returned 
to volleyball.

Research has also linked heat stroke to a later risk of heart and kidney 
problems. A2018 study 
<https://www.ejinme.com/article/S0953-6205(18)30387-X/abstract> that tracked 
survivors of classic heat stroke found that 12 years later they had 3.5 times 
the rate of ischemic heart disease, the most common form, compared with people 
without a history of heat illness. Anotherstudy 
<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6373898/> linked heat stroke to 
increased risks later on of heart attack, other heart problems and chronic 
kidney disease.

In the most severe cases, survivors can be permanently disabled.

“There are people that, unfortunately, barely survive heat stroke and then 
they’re paralyzed or disabled for the rest of their life,” says Rebecca 
Stearns, chief operating officer of the Korey Stringer Institute, a nonprofit 
named after a Minnesota Vikings player whodied 
<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28314926/> from exertional heat stroke in 2001.

As climate change continues to increase exposure to dangerous heat, more 
people may encounter the grim truth Ochoa already knows: The effects aren’t 
always temporary or reversible.

Luxury awnings and shutters

By Olivia Rudgard 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWBnLoYN_tg/olivia-rudgard?cmpid=BBD082926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260829&utm_content=8306>

After months of lobbying, Grosvenor Group Holdings Ltd. has won concessions 
that will make it easier to upgrade its portfolio of luxury listed properties 
and shield tenants from the effects of rising temperatures.

The UK is now set to relax planning requirements that Grosvenor, which is 
owned by billionaire Hugh Grosvenor, the 7th Duke of Westminster, has long 
criticized as antiquated.

Local council rules that hindered tenants in Mayfair and Belgravia from 
installing basic heat protections such as awnings or shutters are set to be 
streamlined, with the UK government acknowledging their “unnecessarily 
restrictive” nature.
Terraced residential properties in the Mayfair district in London.
Photographer: Chris Ratcliffe/Bloomberg
Tor Burrows, chief sustainability officer at Grosvenor, described the 
development as “fantastic.”

Existing rules have hampered efforts by homeowners of listed buildings to 
install cooling features or insulation if such upgrades alter facades deemed to 
be of historical or cultural significance. That’s become increasingly 
problematic as temperatures rise, with the UK’s Met Office warning that 40C 
(104F) summer days are growing more likely.

Grosvenor’s campaign has coincided with a relentless string of heat waves that 
has exposed the extent to which UK housing and infrastructure are unprepared 
for the continual rise in temperatures. But Burrows said heat isn’t the only 
issue, with energy efficiency in listed buildings a general concern.

“Energy bills are set to rise again this autumn,” she noted.
