Pivot!?

Was this the moment of a great pivot, and if so in which direction? For people 
of a certain age, the word “pivot” brings back a classic moment ofphysical 
comedy from “Friends,” <https://bloom.bg/4fpIe6s> as our heroes try to maneuver 
a large sofa up a twisting staircase. The critical point is when to pivot, and 
they can’t get it right. Sometimes they hear instructions from two people at 
once; at others they need to pivot in different directions in quick succession; 
and the result is that the sofa stays stuck between floors. Economic policy is 
in a similar place.

The Federal Open Market Committee <https://bloom.bg/4fAlcbw> meets this week 
in what once looked likely to be a non-event. June showed lower job gains and 
slowing inflation, removing the urgency to hike. But last week, there was 
another pivot. The chance of a hike is back close to 40%:

Much was due to the epic deterioration in the Gulf, with the US pivoting back 
toward escalation and Iran threatening to close the Bab el-Mandeb (and with it 
access to the Suez Canal) as well as the Strait of Hormuz.

A much calmer weekend, with journalists now being briefed 
<https://bloom.bg/3TFyVXj> that a major escalation is unlikely because the US 
is running out of the necessary munitions, brought a sharp fall in oil at 
Monday’s open of Asian trading. But that doesn’t take the chance of a hike all 
the way back to zero. Marc Chandler of Bannockburn Global Forex commented:

Sure, it’s mostly the war and oil, but several Fed officials had set a low bar 
to hike (something like unless inflation falls) but also note that weekly 
jobless claims fell last week to the lowest since 1969. It could be a fluke, 
and we will have a better idea next week.

Chandler is right that companies seem exceptionally reluctant to lay people 
off, and that makes it much harder to keep rates low when inflation remains 
above target:

Rates expectations really moved, however, when energy traders lost patience 
with the returning hostilities in the Gulf and took Brent above $100 per 
barrel. This directly raises headline inflation, and puts higher costs into the 
pipeline for companies that will likely be reflected eventually in inflation 
for consumers. The question this week is whether the market’s pivot has forced 
a durable shift in US military policy, away from escalation:

Another pivot is underway, in the stock market’s attitude to the capital 
expenditures building out artificial intelligence. The hyperscalers — 
Amazon.com Inc., Alphabet Inc., Meta Platforms Inc., Microsoft Corp. and Oracle 
Corp. — that are trying to build a dominant position in the new technology saw 
their shares rally for years after the launch of ChatGPT. That is over. Their 
share prices have gone nowhere for the last 12 months as the excitement moved 
to chip manufacturers, the suppliers that stand to benefit most.

Last week, there appeared to be a further pivot, when Alphabet announced 
<https://bloom.bg/4ht5SAc> revenues and earnings ahead of expectations but also 
said that it would be further stepping up capex.Both the hyperscalers and their 
suppliers are falling <https://bloom.bg/4fDutjk>. All the constituents are 
still sitting on huge gains for the ChatGPT era, but at least implicitly the 
market is now asking whether the hyperscalers will really go through with their 
capex plans, and are cutting back on the chipmakers accordingly:

It’s way too early to say that the AI trade has peaked, and this week’s 
results from Apple, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft should shed further light. But 
note that the nerves over AI are happening even asoverall earnings remain 
remarkably strong <https://bloom.bg/3Rj8ztE>.

This makes it very hard to argue that current rates are in any way 
restrictive. But the assumptions that the AI trade is transformative, and that 
it’s inflationary in the short term and deflationary in the longer term, all 
now appear to be in question — even though the effect on earnings has been 
undeniable.

None of this helps the Trump administration’s targets of a cheaper dollar, 
lower bond yields <https://bloom.bg/4pHI4uB>, cheaper oil, and a stronger stock 
market. Largely as a result of the recent pivot toward escalation once more, 
progress on all of those fronts has been sent into reverse:

Where this will go next depends on whose voice is loudest and who decides to 
change course. The likelihood remains that the Fed will stay its hand while it 
tries to work out where AI and oil, the greatest economic shocks of this year, 
will take them next. The sofa likely stays on the landing until then.

No Stimulus in China

China’s stock market rescue last week provided the latest evidence that the 
2024 rally sparked by the Communist Party’s“whatever-it-takes” 
<https://bloom.bg/4fxaK6d> pivot had run out of steam. It needs another 
artificial jolt. Big boosts from Beijing have in the past stimulated the entire 
global economy — most notably in the wake of the Global Financial Crisis in 
2008. But it would be unwise to count on help from that quarter this year.

Buying by the  so-called national team <https://bloom.bg/4hxJqFZ> of 
state-controlled investors instantly put a floor under a dismal run for Chinese 
equities, particularly technology stocks. Policymakers’ intention is clear — 
boosting technology’s competitiveness so that it can rival the US and drive 
growth that shores up living standards.

Yet, as in 2024, the durability of this intervention remains uncertain. 
Weakness in consumer demand is undermining growth, even as exports — which 
stepped in after the collapse of the property market — continue tosurge 
<https://bloom.bg/4hwMMZU>. Does another Beijing policy shift lie ahead? If the 
chief concern is exports, then probably not:

But exports alone can’t drag the economy along. The recent slowdown in fiscal 
spending points to an economy that’s barely staying afloat. Data published last 
week by the Finance Ministry shows fiscal spending declining by 12% 
year-on-year across the general and funds budgets:

This doesn’t necessarily mean that another big stimulus would help. Leah Fahy 
of Capital Economics suggests that the shortfall may be due to local 
governments failing to use all their allocated funds; ministry data shows they 
used up 47% of their special bond quota in the first half of this year, 
compared to 49% in the first half of 2025. Fahy argues that fiscal spending was 
front-loaded last year, so base effects will turn favorable. But if local 
governments’ borrowing continues to be absorbed by restructuring old debts, the 
fiscal impulse may prove to be even smaller than the figures in this year’s 
budget implied.

A slowdown in growth <https://bloom.bg/4pLojCu> adds to the case for a fiscal 
stimulus. The official position blames “short-term factors and external 
influences,” particularly in the energy sector. The bottom line is that 
second-quarter 4.3% real growth in gross domestic product was the slowest pace 
since late 2022, when Covid-Zero restrictions were still in effect:

BCA Research’s Jing Sima projects that fiscal policy will turn somewhat more 
supportive as the year progresses, but on a limited scale. Beijing will 
accelerate existing measures to shore up domestic demand, with support tilted 
toward strategic sectors:

Although policy support will increase in the second half, the primary driver 
will be faster implementation of already-authorized fiscal measures rather than 
broad-based new stimulus.

Consumption remains fairly weak, but Gavekal Research’s Andrew Batson points 
out that it’s far from the extremes of two years ago — and that the authorities 
might now be prepared to accept lower growth:

Real growth in domestic demand — consumption plus investment — is still in the 
same 3-4% range it has been in since 2024. Arguably, that is not ideal, and the 
government should do more, but the authorities have tolerated subpar domestic 
demand for some time already.

Volatility in oil and gold prices complicates inflation, but Batson notes that 
the underlying trend is for very low inflation around 0.5%, not outright 
deflation:

Overall, local governments’ financial problems remain unresolved. The 2024 
debt-swap program stabilized the situation, and it’s possible that the central 
government has quietly expanded the scale of the bailout as needed, Batson 
argues. A meaningful fiscal stimulus package remains an option. But it appears 
far less urgent than many investors had hoped.

—Richard Abbey



 Survival Tips 



Readers’ consensus is that I was too negative about the Red Sox’ 
<https://bloom.bg/4wjKL80>s 15-game winning streak <https://bloom.bg/3TdsxGP>, 
which tied an 80-year record. My argument is that streaks commemorate 
distribution of performance, not overall quality, and are driven by randomness. 
There was a lot of luck in Boston’s streak, although they’d also lost a lot of 
one-run games beforehand, so it all averages out.

More meaningful streaks to be nominated include the six consecutive sixes 
<https://bloom.bg/4gZZWyr> in an over by the late Sir Garfield Sobers, who 
sadly died last week <https://bloom.bg/4pIM6CU> (the commentator was wrong to 
say that it had been done before); the Dutch wheelchair tennis playerEsther 
Vergeer <https://bloom.bg/3RcqQsA> went unbeaten for 10 years (460 games) which 
must be more than randomness (evenRoger Federer was awestruck 
<https://bloom.bg/4fGkSIt>); Jimmy Connors <https://bloom.bg/4pQwzRx> had a 
16-game winning streak over Vitas Gerulaitis, prompting the latter to say, 
“Nobody beats Gerulaitis 17 times in a row” when he finally broke it — before 
Bjorn Borg went on to do exactly that in a run that included one ofthe greatest 
Wimbledon matches of all time <https://bloom.bg/4pJxZNR>; Australian squash 
playerHeather Pamela McKay <https://bloom.bg/4foPcsl> (née Blundell) won 16 
British Opens in a row, going undefeated throughout; Brentford won all their 
home games in the 1929-30 season, the only time that’s been done, and still 
didn’t earn enough points for promotion; the New York Knicks won13 straight 
playoff games <https://bloom.bg/4fwPwUv> on the way to this year’s title (and 
yes, it was fantastic to be here for it, even if I prefer the Celtics); and the 
Joe Torre Yankees won 14 consecutive World Series games, starting fromtwo down 
to a great Braves team in 1996 <https://bloom.bg/3RgapeR>. And streaks do have 
their uses when they test the bounds of plausibility. We should all have noted 
Bernard Madoff’s 198-month streak of positive returns, one reader suggests. If 
it looks too good to be true, that’s because it’s a lie. More streaks 
gratefully accepted. Have a great week everyone.
