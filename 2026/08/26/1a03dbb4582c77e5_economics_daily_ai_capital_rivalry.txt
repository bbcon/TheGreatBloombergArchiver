AI as Capital Rival

It’s probably fair to say that “Kauri bonds” aren’t a well known corner of the 
global financial system. But that’s exactly why South Korea’s trade credit 
agency turned to to this quiet patch to raise money this week.

In fact, there had been no new sale of Kauri bonds — which are securities 
issued by overseas borrowers in New Zealand —in nine years 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-25/ai-debt-crowding-spurs-biggest-korean-bond-sale-in-kauri-market?cmpid=BBD082626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8182>
 before the Export-Import Bank of Korea sold NZ$625 million ($372 million) of 
five-year notes on Tuesday.

Why head down to the South Pacific? It was essentially to escape competition 
from the companies scrambling to amass funding for the artificial intelligence 
investment boom. Sungho Park, a director at the state-owned bank’s treasury 
department, explained that debt issuance costs are rising thanks tocompeting 
sales 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/ai-is-driving-up-treasury-yields-it-just-touches-everything?cmpid=BBD082626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8182>
 from “AI-related hyperscalers.”

“This was an attempt to overcome this challenge,” by “tapping into new markets 
where the impact is minimal,” Park said.

Park isn’t alone in pointing out the rivalry that hyperscalers are now 
providing to official borrowers. Bessent last week said that amid his effort to 
keep the US government debt market inequilibrium 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/bessent-says-buybacks-could-be-more-than-4-billion-per-issue?cmpid=BBD082626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8182>
, “we are also seeing big corporate issuance and a lot of that corporate 
issuance, I would say, is almost yield agnostic.”

In other words, the sellers don’t much care what the rate is on the securities 
they issue. Among the big sellers lately has been Google parent Alphabet Inc., 
whichsold $25 billion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/alphabet-returns-to-bond-market-amid-ai-spending-worries?cmpid=BBD082626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8182>
 of securities with maturities ranging from 2 to 40 years.

Bessent even weighed in on their tactics, hinting that the 5-year area would 
be a better place to sell. The more issuance there is at the longer end, the 
more risk of higher Treasury yields — and mortgage rates, afocus 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-19/us-housing-is-a-buyer-s-market-trouble-is-there-aren-t-many-buyers?cmpid=BBD082626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8182>
 of this newsletter last week. Hyperscalers are also triggering arise in risk 
metrics 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/big-tech-drives-up-credit-risk-for-safe-firms-with-no-ai-links?cmpid=BBD082626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8182>
 for some of the world’s safest firms, Bloomberg reported earlier this month.

But if anyone else wants a refuge, there’s always New Zealand.

Need-to-Know Research

China is likely to continue accumulating gold as a key part of its 
international reserves, according to S&P Global Ratings — in part because it’s 
behind the curve in shifting toward the precious metal, from traditional 
holdings such as US Treasuries.

Despite being the world’s top gold consumer and producer, China’s gold holdings
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-07/china-central-bank-adds-gold-in-hong-kong-to-support-trading-hub?cmpid=BBD082626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8182>
 have grown more slowly and remain smaller than most countries as a share of 
official reserves, S&P wrote in a report. “The perceived need to catch up or 
keep up will likely support sentiment for further build-up” via purchases or 
production, said Charles Chang, S&P’s greater China corporates lead.

“A gold chase across the developing world” has ensued since Russia’s 2022 
invasion of Ukraine, S&P said this week. The US and its allies froze Russian 
reserves after that move, spurring some nations to view gold as a potentially 
more flexible alternative reserve asset. “While coverage of central bank 
gold-buying often focuses on China, this phenomenon is global in nature, 
particularly among emerging markets,” said Chang said.
