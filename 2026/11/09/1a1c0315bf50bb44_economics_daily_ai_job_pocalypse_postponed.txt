No AI Job-pocalypse, Yet

Coming up on four years since the release of OpenAI’s ChatGPT 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-12-07/openai-chatbot-so-good-it-can-fool-humans-even-when-it-s-wrong?cmpid=BBD110926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11160>
, there’s abundant evidence that, so far at least, the massivehemorrhaging 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-03-05/the-dark-side-of-a-productivity-boom-is-coming-to-light-as-ai-threatens-jobs?cmpid=BBD110926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11160>
 of white-collar jobs feared by some has yet to unfold. The latest batch of 
data even suggest AI may be offering a net positive to the job market — again, 
for now.

Friday’s US employment report showed that management, scientific and technical 
consulting services firms added 17,300 workers to payrolls in October, the most 
in almost two years. Another 17,500 new jobs came from building construction 
(excluding housing), specialty trade contractors and computer and electronic 
product manufacturing industries.

It all suggested that white-collar consulting jobs tied to AI implementation 
may be joining blue-collar construction and manufacturing jobs tied to the data 
center buildout asa driver of current job growth 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-06/us-job-gains-may-be-spreading-from-ai-buildout-to-implementation?cmpid=BBD110926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11160>
.

The professional, scientific, and technical services categories are ones that 
include job descriptions “at the epicenter of concerns about AI-related job 
loss,” Michael Feroli, JPMorgan Chase’s chief US economist, wrote of the gains 
recorded in this area in October.

“As always, one shouldn’t get carried away with a one-month reading of 
anything” in the payrolls figures, “but this provides a glimmer of hope that 
the feared job-pocalypse is still far off,” Feroli wrote.

One example of a positive impact may be seen in the global consulting giant 
Accenture, which said last month it had boosted headcount by 5% amid growing 
demand from clients seeking toimplement AI technologies 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/accenture-targets-mid-single-digit-growth-backed-by-deals?cmpid=BBD110926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11160>
 — even as AI was making its own work more efficient.

Still, some of the October job gains came against a backdrop of earlier 
losses. Financial activities firms added 7,000 positions last month, but 
headcount remained 99,000 below a year earlier. “Information,” meanwhile has 
shed 119,000 jobs over the past year, to the lowest level since 2020.

“It’s striking that sectors accounting for both healthy job gains — 
professional and business services — and significant job losses — information 
and finance — are among the major early adopters of AI,” Nancy Vanden Houten, 
lead US economist at Oxford Economics, wrote in a note. “For the economy 
overall, it still appears that AI is augmenting labor demand more than it’s 
replacing it.”

Need-to-Know Research

President Donald Trump’s move to tap Stephen Miller as his new White House 
chief of staff — the main architect of hardline immigration policies slammed by 
Democrats (and many voters 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/trump-says-tough-deporation-policy-likely-hurt-gop-in-midterms?cmpid=BBD110926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11160>
) — argues for less deal-making and more brinksmanship on US fiscal policy in 
2027.

That’s according to Tobin Marcus at Wolfe Research, writing in a note Friday. 
“Miller is simultaneously a highly polarizing partisan figure, and an ardent 
advocate for novel and expansive uses of executive power, neither of which 
suggests that the White House is focused on compromise with Congressional 
Democrats,” he wrote.
Stephen MillerPhotographer: Aaron Schwartz/Sipa/Bloomberg
On paper, the grand deal to be made is the boost to health-care spending 
Democrats are seeking combined with the GOP’s desire for a bump in defense. “We 
think investors are underestimating how hard it would be,” Marcus said. “Deals 
are still possible, but we think they’re more likely to be catalyzed by a 
debt-ceiling standoff in late 2027″ than from early-2027 dealmaking, he said.
