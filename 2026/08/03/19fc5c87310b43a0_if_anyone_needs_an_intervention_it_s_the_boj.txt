All Hands on Yen 

Over the past four years, Japan’s Ministry of Finance has made a habit of 
intervening to support the yen. It hasn’t helped, yet last week’s episode felt 
different. It produced a big turnaround, and this time the ministry had help 
from a significant quarter — US Treasury SecretaryScott Bessent 
<https://bloom.bg/3SdH98N>. But it needs the Bank of Japan’s assistance as well:

With the currency near its weakest in four decades, the intervention led to 
the strongest level since May. Since buying yen in September 2022 — the first 
such operation since 1998 — the ministry has spent at least $255 billion 
without stemming the tide. That conservative figure doesn't include last week, 
pending the official amount.

This matters beyond Japanese shores because the carry trade — the strategy of 
borrowing in yen and parking in a higher-yielding currency like the Mexican 
peso — has been an ultra-reliable source of profits. Over five years, it has 
strongly beaten even the S&P 500’s total return. A big yen appreciation might 
prompt the carry trade to unwind, as it did to chaotic effect two years ago. 
Last week’s move has already jolted the carry trade out of what had been a 
startlingly steady upward trend:

These actions are growing more frequent precisely because they are ever less 
impactful. Those betting against the yen know that the ministry’s resources are 
finite, and that Japan is in a poor fiscal position. Four years ago, the market 
reportedly viewed around 145 yen per dollar as Tokyo’s line in the sand. The 
latest intervention suggests that threshold has shifted closer to 160, 
underscoring investors’ growing willingness to test authorities’ resolve.

Washington’s uncharacteristic support — which seems to have included rate 
checks <https://bloom.bg/4vZvI2j> and selling euros to buy yen — is potentially 
a big deal and sends a powerful signal. Mizuho Bank’s Masayuki Nakajima argues 
that this reflects Washington’s growing concern over the risks of further 
depreciation, or that rising volatility in Japanese bonds could spill over into 
US Treasuries:

But US involvement remains problematic. Robin Brooks of the Brookings 
Institution, a long-term skeptic of Japanese efforts to prop up the yen, points 
out:

US participation raises more questions than answers, especially the very odd 
news that the US sold euros to buy yen. This kind of twist in my opinion 
undercuts the efficacy of US participation, because it invariably will have 
markets wondering why the US didn’t just fund yen buying out of dollars. FX 
intervention is a confidence game. The last thing you want is to give markets 
any kind of reason to ask questions.

What of the Bank of Japan? In June, it raised its policy rate to 1% — the 
highest since 1995. But it stayed on hold last week, and only one more hike is 
expected this year. Expectations have stayed relatively constant even as 
markets have priced in increases for other central banks following the oil 
supply shock. The yen weakening has been almost exactly in line with the 
widening gap between the BOJ and the Fed:

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, who received a massive electoral mandate six 
months ago, has anadverse stance <https://bloom.bg/3U9AbSI> on policy 
tightening as she goes for growth.

The timing and magnitude of the ministry’s action — with the estimated $52.8 
billion <https://bloom.bg/4pT0E2I> spent on July 31 marking the largest 
single-day intervention on record — suggest a new level of resolve. What the 
BOJ does next is critical. Not long ago, the oil shock (Japan is an energy 
importer) combined with the weak yen to suggest renewed inflationary pressure. 
But the numbers remain ambiguous, with last year’s big spike possibly 
attributable to the bad rice harvest. The case for hawkishness isn’t clear from 
the latest data:

As Bloomberg Opinion colleague Gearoid Reidy points out 
<https://bloom.bg/45GtTfS>, Governor Kazuo Ueda failed to provide meaningful 
forward guidance when the market was desperate for it. Jesper Koll, the 
long-term Tokyo-based investment banker who publishes theJapan Optimist 
<https://bloom.bg/3JSLdXy> newsletter, was scathing:

The fact that, thank you, Governor Ueda tells us with great confidence he sees 
Japan inflation re-accelerating to above 2% in the second half of Japan’s 
fiscal year got undermined immediately by his lack of action. So why exactly 
are you not hiking if you’re so confident? Could it be that Japan’s financial 
system is so weak that accelerated rate hike would trigger a possible banking 
crisis? 

Koll, an optimist, and Brooks, a skeptic, agree that even this level of 
intervention won’t work on its own. For Brooks, the yen is falling “because 
Japan’s high public debtprevents <https://bloom.bg/45HLCUd> the country from 
allowing yields to rise freely.” With bonds kept capped, the yen becomes the 
main symptom of “a debt crisis that’s getting papered over.”

Koll thinks Takaichi’s aggressive pursuit of growth will probably work, but 
agrees that the likely path of the yen is weaker. He suspects that the BOJ is 
holding off because of worries about the secondary banking system, but that the 
new fiscal policy is almost guaranteed, ultimately, to be inflationary. Thus, 
risks are still asymmetrically tilted toward an even weaker yen.

AIncredible Volatility

After ChatGPT’s 2022 debut, investors first rewarded companies building AI 
models, and then those supplying them, led by chipmakers. Then they got nervous 
about the spending involved, leading to extraordinary goings-on last week.

It’s hard to know where to start, but Microsoft Corp. might be a good place. 
Having been out of favor, its latest earnings induced quite a turnaround; 
market cap rose more than $600 billion in two days as it rejoined the $3 
trillion club. This time last year, though, it was in the $4 trillion club:

After driving earnings growth, this FactSet chart (which doesn’t include the 
latest wave of results) showed Big Tech ceding its leadership:

The Nasdaq 100 was down 11% from its peak before Microsoft’s results generated 
a rally powerful enough to reverse the S&P 500 Equal Weight Index’s 
outperformance. After two months of “broadening,” the market narrowed again:

One strong quarter for Microsoft cannot settle broader questions about 
hyperscalers’ capital spending. The brutal reception for Meta Platforms Inc. 
and Alphabet Inc. showed that anyone overspending will be punished, even if 
they produced strong earnings. Matt Rowe, a portfolio manager at Man Group, 
sums up anxiety:

Investors’ attitude right now is they’re overexposed to the equity category 
generally. They know it… So there’s been a lot of discussion around portfolio 
hedging and how to remain long, but put some kind of a net under this risk.

That accords with what Viktor Shvets of Macquarie Group calls a series of 
“rolling bubbles” that will inflate and deflate across AI derivatives. Shocks 
from China, in the form of DeepSeek and Moonshot AI’s Kimi 3 models, achieved 
on tighter budgets, ignited doubts about the wisdom of hyperscalers’ spending:

China’s AI breakthroughs <https://bloom.bg/4yYrvyQ> are hard to ignore, as 
they’ve shown the ability to innovate at a fraction of the cost. Shvets sees 
Beijing playing a key role across the entire AI space, preventing monopolies 
and creating new opportunities by turning everything into AI plays.

The reshuffling within the sector isn’t over. Shares in semiconductor and 
memory chipmakers pulled back last month, followed by a remarkable rebound in 
the last few days. Most dramatically, South Korea’s Kospi gained 17% on Friday 
alone in almost unfathomable volatility:

Amid overall confusion both in the US and in emerging markets, chips have 
still doubled in value this year — but it’s not clear that the latest 
convulsions mean the start of a durable rebound:

Jason Pride of Glenmede notes that the semiconductor industry is still 
inherently cyclical. “When times are good, profits are really thick. When times 
are just a little bit thinner, profits can actually be surprisingly weak.”

Ordinarily, investors would try to time a rotation from cyclicals to 
defensives, but Shvets argues against that. Conventional style rotations, he 
says, now offer neither returns nor a hedge. It’s better to look for where the 
bubble will roll next:

Backing the revolution, searching for new breakouts, minimizing deflating past 
winners while diversifying across a range of themes is the only rational 
response. Billions will rapidly turn into trillions and vice versa. This is not 
Buffett’s or Burry’s world.

Picking the next winners and losers won’t get any easier. Sadly, for now, even 
the best large language models can’t tell us that.

—Richard Abbey


We Need to Talk About Kevin

Kevin Warsh raised more questions than he answered at his press conference 
<https://bloom.bg/4fJzfx7> last Wednesday. Non-farm payrolls this week, and 
interventions from other Fed officials, might clarify things. For now, two 
points.

First, Warsh confused people, but the point of not offering guidance is that 
the market forms its own view from the data, and the projected course of 
interest rates at the end of the week was barely changed:

Second, something is afoot in long-term yields, which suggests possible damage 
to the Fed’s credibility. The 30-year TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected 
Security) yield has only ever been higher for a few days at the worst of the

 Survival Tips 



Last week’s volatility received a push from the hedge fund Situational 
Awareness, <https://bloom.bg/4qcMpX3> which got into trouble, unwound some big 
AI positions <https://bloom.bg/4z00viu>, and submitted to a rescue from Citadel 
<https://bloom.bg/4yLrCNU> for pennies on the dollar. It’s unclear whether it 
was more a symptom or cause of the volatility and (full disclosure) I haven’t 
had the time to do a full analysis. We’ll return during the week ahead.

BUT: One tip for survival is clear. If you don’t want your financial vehicle 
to be a famous blowout, don’t tempt fate with its name. We’ve hadLong-Term 
Capital Management <https://bloom.bg/3RETDWR> (seized up because it was so 
short-term); Archegos (“leader from the front” in Greek); and Enron entities 
with Star Wars-themed names like Jedi LP, Kenobe Inc. and Obe-1 Holdings that 
proved incapable of using The Force. Situational Awareness wasn’t aware of its 
situation. Thanks to Academy’s Peter Tchir for these suggestions. A name like 
Trying to Get Right Slightly More Than I Get Wrong LLP might work better. Any 
other nominations?
