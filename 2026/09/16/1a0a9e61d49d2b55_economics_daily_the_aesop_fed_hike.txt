Aesop’s Rate Hike

Following his first policy meeting as Fed chief, Kevin Warsh said in June, 
“We’ve gotsome work to do 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-06-18/warsh-establishes-inflation-credibility-now-sets-up-fed-regime-change-later?cmpid=BBD091626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8929>
 on the price-stability front” after inflation had run way over the 2% target 
for years.

After his second, in July, he referenced “impatience” among the public in 
getting inflation down, and declared, “where necessary and appropriate, we will 
not hesitate to act.”

In August, at the Jackson Hole conference, he said “we have work to do” unless 
there’s confidence prices are moving to 2% “clearly and at sufficient speed.”

There’s little doubt in Fed watchers’ minds that Warsh has been talking the 
talk on fighting inflation. His longtime colleague, Treasury Secretary Scott 
Bessent, was among those saying he had come out “tough 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-24/bessent-signals-confidence-in-warsh-sees-inflation-coming-down?cmpid=BBD091626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8929>
” on that score. But as this newsletter noted in July, the question was whether 
he’d reallywalk the talk 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-07-15/kevin-warsh-talks-the-talk-on-inflation-while-leaving-the-walk-in-question?cmpid=BBD091626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8929>
.

On Wednesday, Warsh faces the millennia-old “boy who cried wolf” issue from 
Aesop’s fables. If he doesn’t vote to hike interest rates, the Fed-watching 
community is unlikely to believe his inflation rhetoric going forward. His 
credibility risks being shot.

That’s after Friday’s consumer price index 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-11/us-core-cpi-rises-more-than-forecast-bolstering-case-for-hike?cmpid=BBD091626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8929>
 report, the last before this week’s meeting, was difficult to be interpreted 
as showing inflation was “clearly” and speedily moving to 2%.

Ironically, had Warsh been less tough-talking before that report, it might not 
otherwise have been viewed as a decisive blow for a rate increase. Some of 
those who switched their call after the release appeared todo so reluctantly 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-16/fed-decision-inflation-war-and-ai-feed-into-bond-market-s-toxic-stew?cmpid=BBD091626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8929>
.

Goldman Sachs was among those changing to a quarter-point rate-rise forecast 
on Friday. The CPI report “has not changed our fundamental inflation view,” 
economist David Mericle wrote in explanation. “We do not see a strongeconomic 
case 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-10/us-cpi-report-to-guide-fed-decision-on-rates-impact-on-economy?cmpid=BBD091626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8929>
 for raising.”
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-09-16/fed-rate-hike-to-be-one-and-done-citi-s-hollenhorst-video?cmpid=BBD091626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8929>
The Goldman team is among those flagging that inflation currently is being 
kept high by one-time factors. Energy costs have surged due to the Iran war, 
for example. But policymakers likely will move nonetheless because they will 
want to avoid the market reaction that would likely follow from remaining on 
hold when the market is pricing a nearly90% chance of a hike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-11/treasuries-fall-as-higher-than-expected-cpi-boosts-fed-hike-odds?cmpid=BBD091626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8929>
.”

In other words, a failure to deliver on Wednesday would risk investors 
concluding that Warsh’s price-stability cries are hollow. The risk is they’d 
then price in faster inflation for longer, sending yields — and mortgage rates

Need-to-Know Research

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s 3% US economic growth target, which 
underpins his goal to stabilize thefederal debt trajectory 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/us-public-debt-hits-40-trillion-high-raising-doom-loop-risk?cmpid=BBD091626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8929>
, could come close to doing just that, according to Wells Fargo analysis. But 
there are some provisos, economists Tom Porcelli and Michael Pugliese wrote in 
a note Tuesday.

Stabilizing debt-to-GDP over the next decade would require both 3% to 3.5% in 
gross domestic product increases per year and “no major changes” to taxation 
and spending policies, the duo wrote. The US hasn’t sustained that sort of 
trend growth rate since before the financial crisis that began in 2007. But, 
“on the face of it, let’s simply call this possible.”

What makes things more challenging is the aging US population means labor 
force increases won’t be helping as much as they used to. That means “it would 
take the fastest labor productivity on record to achieve this outcome” for 
growth. As for Washington forgoing major tax and spending policy changes for a 
decade? The duo didn’t immediately weigh in. US budget experts have been 
skeptical, as Bloombergreported 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/bessent-s-growth-solution-for-us-debt-is-seen-failing-the-credibility-test?cmpid=BBD091626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8929>
 Tuesday.
