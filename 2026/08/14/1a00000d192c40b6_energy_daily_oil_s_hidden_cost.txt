Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Charles Gorrivan looks at the ecological costs of months of war in the 
Middle East.

In the past five months of war in Iran, Middle Eastern oil producers have gone 
to great lengths to meet the world’s insatiable demand for crude. Now, barrels 
are washing ashore across the region, showing the environmental cost of some of 
those efforts.

Even in some of the fiercest periods of the conflict, a smattering of Gulf 
countries, led by the United Arab Emirates, have continued to use a route 
through the Strait of Hormuz near Oman. Those ships — often sailing with their 
satellite transponders switched off — have been key to keeping inflation in 
check and supporting the economies of regional producers.

With sporadic attacks continuing in recent days, the risks facing oil tankers 
and other commodity carriers making such transits have been laid bare.

On the Iranian side of Hormuz near Qeshm Island, satellite imagery shows a 
dark, elongated slick where oil appears to have washed ashore along the nearby 
coastline. There, the human toll of months of bombing is now being compounded 
by an environmental one, with authoritiesfighting 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-12/iran-battles-oil-spill-near-key-persian-gulf-island-of-qeshm?cmpid=BBBXT081426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7732>
 a spill next to a major conservation area.
Satellite imagery shows an elongated trail in waters off Shib Deraz on Iran’s 
Qeshm Island.Source: Copernicus Sentinel/ESA via Bloomberg
Another apparent slick stretches near Fujairah, the oil and bunkering hub by 
the strait’s southwestern entrance. It has in recent days appeared to spread 
across at least 60 square kilometers (23 square miles), according to John Amos, 
a pollution expert who reviewed satellite images at Bloomberg’s request.

“We’re starting to see really major oil spills,” said Amos, chief executive 
officer of SkyTruth, a nonprofit organization that specializes in using 
satellite tools to track environmental risks.

An oil slick nearby offers a hint of a conflict more than a thousand miles 
away, too.

A sanctioned tanker that was hauling Russian oil ran aground off the southern 
coast of Oman, and has beenpouring barrels into the sea 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/experts-race-to-salvage-leaking-oil-tanker-grounded-off-oman?cmpid=BBBXT081426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7732>
 near the country’s Hallaniyat Islands in recent weeks. The danger from that 
disaster is being heightened by a seasonal monsoon.

Together, the spills point to the more hidden repercussions of conflict. While 
shipments are still exiting the Gulf, and the inflationary threat has been 
somewhat tamed, battles for the world’s energy chokepoints are raging on, 
bringing the risk of ecological catastrophe.

—Charles Gorrivan, Bloomberg News
