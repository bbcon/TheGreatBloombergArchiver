Safety in IPO Numbers?

Once earnings are over, Anthropic PBC’s IPO promises the next great moment of 
truth, completing arguably the greatest year for stock market debuts on record. 
The massive sums involved evoke memories of the last great IPO wave, which 
crested in 1999 just as the internet bubble was about to burst.

In general, an IPO is not a great time to buy, as it’s the one point at which 
the interests of existing owners and potential investors are not aligned. 
Indeed, the IPOX SchusterIPOX Composite index 
<https://www.ipox.com/indexes/ipox-us-composite-index>, including all issues to 
go public within the previous four years, has lagged the public markets since 
2021:

This may be due to the non-appearance of huge AI heroes. Had they gone public 
by now, the IPO index might be outperforming.

How comparable are the two waves? In 1999, Nicholas Colas of DataTrek Research 
points out, 476 IPOs raised $65 billion between them ($133 billion in today’s 
prices).SpaceX 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-06-02/spacex-to-boldly-raise-money-where-no-ipo-has-gone-before?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 on its own raised $86 billion this year.Anthropic 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-10-27/anthropic-ipo-amodei-s-hellish-difficulty-is-its-unknowable-demand?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 hopes to top $100 billion. These aren’t comparable periods.

In 1999, many went to market too early, before establishing profitability or 
even, in some cases, making any sales. Now, with private credit and equity 
enabling founders to raise more money without going public, companies may be 
leaving it too late. The differences in scale are profound. As Colas puts it, 
“These are the proverbial apples and bowling balls.”

That leads to a key point of distinction with the last IPO wave. Back then, 
the notion was that there was safety inportfolio effects 
<https://www.ubs.com/global/en/our-firm/what-we-do/our-brand/nobel-perspectives/laureates/financial-economics/what-is-modern-portfolio-theory.html>
. Buy everything, and you’d catch the winners. It wouldn’t matter if the rest 
went bust. For an extreme example of this math, Nvidia was one of the 476 IPOs 
in 1999. If all had gone to zero except for Nvidia, a portfolio of all those 
companies would still have beaten the S&P 500:

Or look at Amazon.com, which went public in 1997 and hit a bubble-era top in 
December 1999 before losing more than 90%. Backon the 10th anniversary of its 
IPO, I wrote an amazed piece 
<https://www.ft.com/content/1f7a89a4-f4e3-11db-b748-000b5df10621> detailing 
that it would, with hindsight, have been a good idea to buy at its 1999 peak. 
Indeed, you could have bought it at IPO, along with 10 other IPOs that went to 
zero, and beaten the S&P 500. I thought that was impressive. As of now, with 
the company reaping the rewards of cloud computing, you could have bought 
Amazon and 365 duds and come out ahead:

That logic worked three decades ago. It won’t this time. As Colas puts it, 
this wave “puts a lot of eggs in a limited number of baskets,” even if it does 
concentrate capital with managements who know what they’re doing. In the 
unlikely event that OpenAI or Anthropic go the way ofPets.com’s sock puppet 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tMYvqRJiOd8>, there will be economic wreckage, 
no matter how other IPOs do. In this wave, there’s no safety in numbers.

Divide and Conquer

Moments of truth are at hand. We will hear from a swath of central banks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-28/new-zealand-unexpectedly-holds-interest-rates-steady-kiwi-falls?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 this week and get results from the artificial intelligence hyperscalers. As 
that approaches, the action remains below the market surface, but intense.

Earlier this year, investors bet that AI would destroy software groups’ 
business models. The SOX semiconductor index beat the S&P 1500 software sector 
by 150% in six months. Since then, the reversal has been almost as dramatic:

(In some arithmetic that will need to be revisited with Hindsight Capital LLC 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-07-01/2008-template-in-hindsight-will-a-coming-crash-seem-obvious?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 at the end of the year, a long SOX/short software trade until June 22, then 
reversed to go long software and short semiconductors, would have turned $100 
on Jan. 1 into $463.)

Moves like this have an impact. The stock market has suffered a series of 
narrowings 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-09-23/stocks-never-has-so-much-been-owed-to-so-few?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
, leaving leadership to an ever smaller group, followed by chaotic returns to 
breadth when everyone else inches forward. This shows in the relative 
performance of the S&P 500’s equal-weighted version, in which each company 
accounts for 0.2% of the index, and in the cumulative progress of advancers 
compared to decliners. October’s turbulence has involved baby steps forward for 
many:

Rising bond yields, with the 10-year Treasury currently at almost exactly 5%, 
have catalyzed a broad derating for stocks that extraordinary growth forecasts 
have hidden. With the exception of a few days after last year’sLiberation Day 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2025-04-03/trump-tariffs-you-can-not-be-serious?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 tariff announcement, stocks are at their lowest multiple of forward earnings 
in three years. They have given up almost all the expansion since ChatGPT 
launched in November 2022:

Colas of DataTrek argues:
The combination of recently elevated long-term Treasury yields, high oil 
prices, and AI capex uncertainty is taking more of a toll on investor sentiment 
than the absolute level of the S&P 500 suggests.
The distribution of returns is remarkable. Many companies are in zero-sum 
competition with each other, but positive correlations are the norm — everyone 
benefits from a stronger economy, and market tides tend to carry all with them. 
But not now.

There are 124,750 possible pairs of companies within the S&P, and at present 
40% are negatively correlated. That’s a lot, as shown in the black lines in the 
chart below from Societe Generale SA, while the red lines show the norm for the 
last 30 years. That can ultimately be attributed to the zero-sum games created 
by AI and the oil price shock, and they make life difficult for investors:

These conditions enable hedge funds to make a lot of money if they take the 
right positions. They also set up a potential collapse — as seen in the
quantpocalypse of the summer of 2007 
<https://web.mit.edu/Alo/www/Papers/august07.pdf> — if multiple fund managers 
have to exit the same stocks at once. With luck, these moves will remain safely 
below the surface, but a few surprises from earnings season could change that.

Getting Aggressive

The Bank of Japan already sent its benchmark rate to its highest level in more 
than three decades when it hiked in September, so it’s not surprising that it’s
expected to hold 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/most-boj-watchers-expect-next-interest-rate-hike-in-december?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 this Friday. Tightening can resume in December. If the market has this right, 
the BOJ will move in alignment with theFederal Reserve’s expected hawkish hold 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-16/warsh-boosts-market-expectations-of-fed-hold-in-october?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 this week and wait until the year’s last meeting.

Pressure on Governor Kazuo Ueda has abated a bit. July’s currency 
intervention, coordinated with the US Treasury, stopped the yen’s fall. After 
Friday, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’ssoftening opposition to rate hikes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/one-year-on-japan-s-takaichi-faces-reality-check-on-tax-cut-defense-promises?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’spreference for higher Japanese rates 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-31/bessent-tells-japan-officials-rate-hikes-are-needed-nhk-says-mthudgie?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 will allow the BOJ more room to tighten, according to Bloomberg’s World 
Interest Rate Probability function:

The case for tighter policy hinges on underlying inflation. Friday’s press 
conference should show whether the bank believes inflation has hit its 2% 
target on an enduring basis. At present, the headline rate, at 2.1%, and the 
core excluding food and energy of 2.2%, both suggest Japan’s long deflationary 
slump could be over. But there have been false alarms before, particularly 
after consumption tax hikes drove transitory increases, so the bankers need 
good evidence before declaring victory:

The swap market’s near-certainty of a December hike suggests investors believe 
price-stability is within reach. Capital Economics’ Marcel Thieliant expects a 
hawkish hold this week:
With the pick-up in inflation excluding fresh food and energy in September set 
to run much further, the Bank will probably continue to signal that inflation 
risks are tilted to the upside.
Crucially, early indications 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-22/japan-s-top-union-group-calls-for-6-wage-increases-in-2027?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 are that pay rises in Japan’s big round of wage talks (known as theshunto) 
could accelerate next year. Inflationary pressure would increase, while 
reducing the need to keep rates low for the sake of living standards. Japan’s 
largest labor federation is pursuing raises of around 6%:

This would mark a departure from the roughly 5% pay gains targeted since 2023 
— when the Russia-Ukraine war ended years of wage stagnation by driving up 
household costs — and get workers back to wage gains they last enjoyed in 1990, 
the year the great Japanese bubble burst.

Geopolitical factors might justify wage gains. Bloomberg Economics’ Taro 
Kimura points out that energy price pressures and the weak yen are straining 
household budgets. That strengthens the case for a raise, and also makes it 
easier for the central bank to respond with tighter money, as there’s a risk of 
an inflation overshoot. Ueda has argued that a wage gain of about 3% should be 
consistent with his inflation target — so if workers negotiate as successfully 
as expected, that would open the door for additional tightening beyond December.

Bloomberg Economics expects the BOJ to stop at 2%, after three more 
25-basis-point hikes in December, March and July. That should cushion theyen’s 
gains 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-09-09/rising-yen-finally-gets-some-respect?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 achieved through various intervention episodes:


Japan isn’t accustomed to rates this high; could this derail the economy 
again? Oxford Economics’ Shigeto Nagai argues that most firms and households 
have healthy balance sheets:
On aggregate, the Japanese are net savers and rate hikes will have a net 
positive impact by raising deposit rates. People in their 30s and 40s tend to 
be net debtors due to large, overwhelmingly floating-rate mortgages, making 
them vulnerable. Although higher rates will weaken already stagnant residential 
investment, their impact on overall economic growth should be limited.
After the carry trade unwind two years ago, the prospect of tightening — and 
presumably a stronger yen — is unnerving. So far,Japan’s stock market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-18/japanese-equities-set-to-fall-on-tech-share-selloff-higher-oil?cmpid=BBD102826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10639>
 is little scathed, with rate hikes stalling progress but not forcing a reverse:

For now, the only thing that could stop market hikes, Kimura says, is renewed 
market turmoil. That’s largely out of Ueda’s control. It may well fall to the 
market to determine when and how hard the BOJ applies the brakes.

—Richard Abbey
