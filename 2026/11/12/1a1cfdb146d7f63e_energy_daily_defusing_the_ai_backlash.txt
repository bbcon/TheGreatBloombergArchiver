Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Mark Chediak looks at US utilities’ efforts to tackle the popular 
backlash against the energy-hungry AI boom.

Last week’s midterm elections didn’t go well for US power utilities. Popular 
anger over data centers and rising electricity bills helped drive theblue wave 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-04/democrats-win-control-of-the-house-ddhq-projects?cmpid=BBBXT111226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11288>
 that saw the Democrats take control of Congress.

In one of the biggest shocks of the night, Texas — home to some of the largest 
planned investments in AI and related power infrastructure — elected a
Democratic governor 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-04/democrat-hinojosa-wins-texas-governor-race-cnn-projects?cmpid=BBBXT111226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11288>
 who pledged a moratorium on new data center projects.

More broadly, the election results have increased the risk of tougher 
regulation just as utilities plan massive grid investments to serve 
proliferating data hubs.

The firms are taking stock, but not backing down.

Power companies say they’re working with state regulators to ensure data 
centers pay their fair share to connect to the grid, according to arecent study 
<https://www.eei.org/en/news/news/all/2026-cra-report-october> flagged by 
utility lobby theEdison Electric Institute <https://www.eei.org/>. That will 
end up lowering electricity costs for other customers, including households, 
the group argues.

An analysis released by Texas utility Oncor Electric Delivery Co. this week 
said essentially the same thing.
A CloudHQ data center next to a Dominion Energy power substation in Ashburn, 
Virginia.Photographer: Bloomberg
The tech giants also insist they’re stepping up 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-11/microsoft-vows-to-pay-its-own-way-to-power-data-centers?cmpid=BBBXT111226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11288>
. Brandon Oyer, Amazon Web Services’ head of energy and water for the Americas, 
told a BloombergNEF event in Houston yesterday that his company’s extensive 
data center plans in Jackson, Mississippi, have helped fund capital spending by 
the local utility.

Yet skepticism remains over whether the tech firms will really stump up all 
the extra costs incurred by their huge new demand on the power system, 
especially since a hyperscale AI facility can require a host of new 
transmission lines, substations and other grid upgrades.

Utilities say they’re structuring power agreements to make sure data centers 
pay their way and household customers are protected.

But with public apprehension over the AI boom widespread — and last week’s 
post-electionmarket wobble 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-04/wall-street-sees-risks-to-ai-boom-after-the-democratic-sweep?cmpid=BBBXT111226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11288>
 highlighting doubts over its durability — their argument will still be a tough 
sell.

—Mark Chediak, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Governments in Europe, the US, India, Australia and Japan have awarded at 
least $3 billion to 42 electrolyzer makers to expand, yet the subsidies have 
had limited impact on either manufacturing capacity or the firms’ financial 
health, according toBloombergNEF <https://about.bnef.com/>. Technological 
competitiveness, supply-chain management and demand in key markets will be more 
important than public funding in determining which companies remain in 
business, BNEF says.
