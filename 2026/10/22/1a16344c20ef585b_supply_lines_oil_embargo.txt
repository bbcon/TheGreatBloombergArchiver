A third of all imported olive oil could be banned from the US market under a 
proposal from a relatively unknown federal agency with a big remit to regulate 
maritime trade.

It all stems from an investigation that began under the Biden administration 
with reports that at least three Maersk Line US-flagged vessels — which carry 
commercial cargo as well asgoods for the US military 
<https://www.maritime.dot.gov/national-security/strategic-sealift/maritime-security-program-msp>
 — were denied entry to Spanish ports on the grounds that they were carrying 
cargo bound for or coming from Israel.

“Spain has publicly denied docking privileges to multiple vessels and publicly 
stated it will deny docking privileges to certain vessels in the future. Those 
vessels had to reroute to non-Spanish ports and omit Spanish ports from future 
voyages,” the Federal Maritime Commission said in anotice 
<https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-21067.pdf> last week. 
“Spain’s policy of port denials is ongoing.”

The FMC is a bipartisan, independent agency with broad authority to 
investigate and respond to unfair actions taken by other countries that create 
unfavorable shipping conditions in foreign trade. That includes imposing fees 
on vessels and denying entry of maritime cargo to the US.

“We will not accept discrimination against US-flagged ships — period, full 
stop,” said FMC Chairman Laura DiBella in a statement to Bloomberg. “In other 
words, we have teeth and we aren’t afraid to use them.”

If finalized, a ban on Spanish olive oil would be pretty toothy indeed. About 
a third of olive oil imported to the US market comes from Spain, and the rest 
is largely from Italy and Tunisia. According to the American Olive Oil 
Producers Association,just 5% of olive oil <https://aoopa.org/olive-oil-101> 
consumed in the US each year is produced domestically.

The FMC’s also proposal includes a $200,000 fee for Spanish-flagged vessels 
each time they call at US ports, alongside limits or bans of imported goods 
from Spain, including perfume and cosmetics, oil and fats, ceramics, articles 
of stone, and confectionaries.

Relations between the US and Spain have grown testier since the FMC’s initial 
notice in December 2024. At the time, the Spanish government argued it was 
required by Spanish, European Union and international law to prevent the 
transit of defense and dual-use goods through its ports.

Earlier this year, President Donald Trump threatened to “cut off all trade 
with Spain” after the country denied access to its military bases for his 
bombing campaign against Iran.

Unlike tariffs and other trade restrictions imposed by the Trump 
administration, the vessel fees and limits on olive oil and other imports are 
meant specifically to remedy the damage caused by Spain’s restrictions on US 
vessels, according to the FMC.

Public comments are due Dec.14 and the agency may reverse any final actions 
once a diplomatic solution has been reached.

China’s surge in clean-tech exports is proving more durable than a one-month 
spike, supported by higher fossil fuel prices. Exports of solar modules, 
lithium-ion batteries and passenger electric vehicles reached a record $21 
billion in July 2026 and stayed above $20 billion in August, according to a 
report from BloombergNEF, available to Bloomberg Terminal clientshere 
<bbg://news/stories/TNARGCR24U83>. The value of plug-in hybrid electric vehicle 
exports rose 116% year on year to $20.5 billion over March-August.
