A green AI advantage?

By Dan Murtaugh 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQxh6IYYLWU/dan-murtaugh?cmpid=BBD100926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9913>
 andJin Wu 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVFlcsZvVJo/jin-wu-news?cmpid=BBD100926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9913>

To grasp the gargantuan scale of China’s green-energy revolution, consider 
Gansu. In this arid strip on the edge of the country’s western expanses, it is 
possible to drive an hour or more through barren landscape without a single 
break in the neatly regimented lines of wind turbines, stretching out to the 
horizon.
Wind turbines in Jiuquan, Gansu Province, China.Photographer: Qilai 
Shen/Bloomberg
Jiuquan, in the north of the province, is home to the world’s largest wind 
farm – more than 20 gigawatts built on the edge of the Gobi desert by dozens of 
companies over the past two decades. That’s enough to power as many as 20 
million Chinese homes.

The catch is that neither these households nor energy-starved industries are 
close enough to easily use all of that capacity. Much is sent to cities 
hundreds of kilometers away. Every year, more is produced and more is going to 
waste.

China is now poised to spend trillions of dollars on fixing this leakage <> 
with history’s most ambitious power grid buildout. Spanning power lines, 
mega-batteries and other longer-term energy storage, the effort has the 
potential to harness the world’s biggest trove of renewable energy and tip the 
scales in Beijing’s competition with the US to develop artificial intelligence. 
It may also provide a template for other countries wrestling with squandered 
capacity and even blackouts, as parts of southern Europe sawlast year 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-04-29/the-five-seconds-that-plunged-spain-into-darkness?cmpid=BBD100926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9913>
.

“Absorbing renewable energy into the grid is of paramount importance,” said 
Zhao Li, planning director at the Guangdong unit of China Southern Power Grid 
Co., one of the country’s two dominant utilities.

“New energy is defined by its intermittency and volatility. That is what makes 
grid integration the central challenge,” he added, speaking at the sleek 
Guangzhou office from which he oversees one of China’s most power-hungry 
provinces — and one of the first to face outages during the energy crunch of 
2021.

The first challenge in China’s clean-power transition was to build — at speed. 
The world’s top polluter embarked on a race to add more generation capacity 
than any other country, and won.

But its second test will be even more consequential: figuring out how to use 
that abundance.

Official curtailment figures — effectively, the share of renewable energy that 
could have been generated but was never sent out into the world, because of 
insufficient grid capacity or demand — suggest about 9% of wind and solar 
energy was wasted in the first half of this year. That almost certainly 
underestimates the problem, given the scale of green production.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-08/why-china-generates-too-much-power-video?cmpid=BBD100926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9913>
Watch: Why China Generates Too Much Power
China could have generated an extra 401 terawatt-hours of power in the first 
six months alone, according to the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air, 
a nonprofit supporting efforts to curb pollution — nearly as much as Spain and 
the Philippines’ combined annual generation.

Granted, there are encouraging signs: incremental gains in spot power trading, 
increased utilization of battery storage and a more flexible use of power 
lines. China is also tapping its excess energy to accelerate an affordable 
artificial intelligence data center buildout — the US, by contrast hasstruggled 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-27/biggest-us-grid-to-launch-emergency-power-auction-for-ai-boom?cmpid=BBD100926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9913>
 with soaring power bills.

But there’s a long way to go. More power lines, batteries and long-term energy 
storage are needed. BloombergNEF estimates the country will need to pour $3.9 
trillion into the grid through 2049, well over a quarter more than what is 
expected from either Europe or the US.

Brazil’s potential climate pivot

By Fabiano Maisonnave 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXmFWA_HFBY/fabiano-maisonnave?cmpid=BBD100926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9913>

Brazil’s election runoff between conservative Flávio Bolsonaro and leftist 
President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva poses a stark choice on the future of the 
Amazon and the climate. The Oct. 25 vote will follow a first round on Oct. 4 in 
which Bolsonaro edged ahead of Lula,making him the favorite 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-04/bolsonaro-lula-set-to-face-off-in-brazil-runoff-datafolha-says?cmpid=BBD100926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9913>
 to win.
Brazilian presidential candidate Flávio Bolsonaro gestures during a motorcade 
with supporters in Goiania, Brazil, on Sept. 27.Photographer: Sergio 
Lima/AFP/Getty Images
Here are some of key issues on which Bolsonaro and Lula differ, and a few they 
agree on.

Climate change

Flávio Bolsonaro is a senator and a son of Brazil’s former President Jair 
Bolsonaro. Like his family’s ally, US President Donald Trump, Flávio has 
repeatedly questioned human-caused climate change, and the issue isn’t part of 
his election platform. Asked in a recent television interview about his claim 
that the world was cooling rather than warming, he described extreme weather as 
cyclical, then pivoted to the need to expand basic sanitation in Brazil.

Flávio’s brother Eduardo Bolsonaro, a former lawmaker who is expected to have 
a foreign-policy role in the government if his brother is elected, said during 
the campaign that international climate negotiations are useless.
A deforested area in the Amazon rainforest, in the municipality of Ipixuna do 
Para, Para state, Brazil.Photographer: Mauro Pimentel/AFP/Getty Images
By contrast, Lula’s reelection platform ties environmental policy to Brazil’s 
nationally determined contribution, or NDC, under the Paris Agreement. That 
calls for cutting net greenhouse-gas emissions by 59% to 67% by 2035, from 2005 
levels, and renews a pledge for net-zero deforestation by 2030.

Deforestation

Land-use change, driven largely by deforestation, remains Brazil’s largest 
source of greenhouse gases, accounting for about 40% of gross emissions.

Last year, Brazil hosted COP30 in the Amazonian city of Belem 
<bbg://news/stories/T4ZNODGPQQ92>. Lula has sought to position the country as a 
global leader on forest protection and climate finance, with effortsto mobilize 
more funding from rich countries <bbg://news/stories/T5Q3L6GPQQ8A> for 
mitigation and adaptation. His predecessor, Jair Bolsonaro, never attended 
United Nations climate talks. After Jair Bolsonaro was elected in 2018, Brazil
withdrew its offer to host COP25 
<https://www.theguardian.com/world/2018/nov/28/brazil-reneges-on-hosting-un-climate-talks-under-bolsonaro-presidency>
.

Flávio Bolsonaro has pledged to reach zero illegal deforestation by 2029. But 
the definition of “illegal” isn’t permanently fixed; laws determine how much 
land clearing is permitted, and Bolsonaro’s allies in Congress have pushed to 
make deforestation legal in more cases.

If he wins, his favored policies will be easier to get through Congress, 
thanks to election gains by his Liberal Party in both houses. Many of the 
party’s candidates ran and won on pledges to roll back environmental 
protections.

Energy expansion and development in the Amazon 

There are a few points where Flávio Bolsonaro and Lula’s platforms converge. 

Bolsonaro backs the expansion of oil production into the mouth of the Amazon 
River — one of Lula’smore controversial initiatives 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-08-05/brazil-to-drill-for-oil-off-amazon-coast-ahead-of-cop30?cmpid=BBD100926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261009&utm_content=9913>
 in the Amazon. Both support paving a federal highway that cuts through 
hundreds of miles of preserved rainforest. Lula has worked to grow the carbon 
market, and Bolsonaro has defended carbon credits and the creation of economic 
value without deforestation.
Contractors work on photovoltaic modules at a solar farm in Guaimbe, Sao Paulo 
state, Brazil.Photographer: Jonne Roriz/Bloomberg
On the energy transition, both candidates propose expanding renewables, 
including solar power and biofuels.

Still, there are differences. Lula calls for the gradual phaseout of fossil 
fuels and participation in a global roadmap away from them, even as he supports 
continued oil and gas exploration. Bolsonaro wants more oil and gas development 
and he explicitly backs fracking, although there are currently no commercial 
fracking operations in Brazil and it’s subject to a legal review.
