Hi, this is Dan Murtaugh in Ulanqab, in the northern Chinese region of Inner 
Mongolia.

Surrounded by rolling grasslands and herds of grazing sheep and cattle, 
Ulanqab is an unlikely hot spot inthe tech rivalry with the US 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-09/us-says-alibaba-deepseek-have-systematically-siphoned-ai-models?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
.

Yet it’s here, about 215 miles northwest of Beijing, that China is making a 
massive bet on the future 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/china-races-to-build-ai-data-centers-far-away-from-its-biggest-cities?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
 of the infrastructure that will underpin its artificial intelligence economy. 
It’s part of a strategy known asdongshu xisuan, or “data in the east, computing 
in the west.”

The plan would see some AI requests from big coastal cities processed far away 
in less populated areas with plentiful land and renewable energy.

Ulanqab, a city of fewer than 2 million in Inner Mongolia (small by Chinese 
standards), is embracing the opportunity. “BIG DATA” is spelled out in 
five-foot-high letters at the entrance of Chahar High-Tech Development Park, 
where city leaders named the streets after tech giants like Apple and Huawei.

By around 2028, Ulanqab is set to become one of a handful of cities in China’s 
remote north and northwest that will overtake regions around megacities like 
Shanghai and Beijing in terms of computing equipment in active use, according 
to research from BloombergNEF.

That will help China more than double its data center capacity in the next 
five years, the researchers found.
An Alibaba data center in Ulanqab, Inner Mongolia.Photographer: Dan 
Murtaugh/Bloomberg
It’s a big contrast to the US, where the backlash against data centers has 
become a major political issuein this year’s midterm elections 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-09/why-data-center-backlash-is-shaping-the-2026-us-midterm-elections?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
, and several states have effectively frozen new developments.

The difference isn’t going unnoticed in Washington 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/latest/data-center-backlash?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
. This week, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned that the US faces dire 
consequences if it loses out in the AI race. President Donald Trump said 
earlier that growing public anger about data centers risks giving China the 
upper hand in AI.

It’s part of a broader divide in how people in the two countries view AI 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-14/why-ai-optimism-is-so-much-higher-in-china-than-the-us?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
. Stanford University’s AI Index shows 84% of Chinese say they’re excited by 
the tech, the highest share for any country surveyed. In the US, the number is 
just 38%.

One reason for that disparity is a pocketbook issue. In the US, soaring 
electricity costs have fueled people’s anger against energy-hungry data centers 
sucking up scarce resources.

That’s not a problem in China, where the country has in just the past four 
years built more power capacity than the US has in total.

Let that sink in: China has built more power capacity in the past four years 
than the US has in total.

The kicker is that government-regulated rates in China for residential 
electricity haven’t budged in years.

In fact, China’s push for western data centers is partly a response to the 
power sector’s growing problem of abundance. When the sun shines and the wind 
blows, western regions with plenty of inexpensive renewable generation are 
producing more than they consume, leaving leftovers for energy-hungry servers 
and other hardware.

China’s strategy has its risks. Too many cities out west building too many 
data-center projects could result in the kind of capacity issues already
plaguing other industries in the country 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-11/china-halts-new-battery-factories-on-capacity-concerns-caixin?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
. Beijing is trying to get ahead of that by forcing large projects to get 
national approval before work can begin.

And of course, the AI race will come down to more than just data centers. 
Chips, brains and government rules will all play big roles.

But for now, people in Ulanqab are taking advantage of the frenzy. At an 
intersection near several data centers under construction, a woman surnamed Yin 
joked with builders while her husband scooped portions of chicken and egg, tofu 
skin and vegetables onto a plate for the equivalent of $1.50 each.

“Take a look around,” she said. “How can this not be good?”

Patriotic Backlash

For many years, China was the main sales driver for luxury goods as growing 
wealth and disposable incomes drove consumers to Louis Vuitton handbags, Hermès 
scarves and Gucci shoes.

That demand helped drive LVMH sales and shares to record highs in 2023. 
Underscoring the good times, last year Louis Vuittonopened a huge cruise 
ship-shaped store 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-11/lvmh-nears-china-store-openings-as-luxury-demand-begins-recovery?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
 in the swanky Jing’an neighborhood of Shanghai.

Then the headwinds mounted. A slowing economy and a government crackdown on 
conspicuous consumption hit the sector. Luxury brands also suffered from 
efforts byauthorities to reclaim tax revenues 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-25/xi-s-unpredecented-tax-clawback-hammers-chinese-listed-companies?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
.

And now, a trademark spat with a local tea maker 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-10/louis-vuitton-loses-china-sales-after-legal-fight-with-tea-chain?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
 has sparked a backlash that’s exacerbated a demand slump for LVMH’s most 
profitable label.
The Louis Vuitton store shaped like a cruise ship in Shanghai last year.
Photographer: Raul Ariano/Bloomberg
Social media fury over the case against Molly Tea, whose four-petal floral 
design was found to be too similar to the French brand’s famous monogram, drove 
customers away. Molly Tea plans to appeal the court ruling that ordered it to 
pay the French brand the equivalent of $1.5 million, the state-run Global Times 
newspaper reported.

The public backlash began showing up in the brand’s sales, according to a 
research firm. 

“We have continued to observe double-digit sales declines in August, albeit 
the decline narrowed from approximately 30% in July to an estimated 20%-25%,” 
said Junheng Li, chief executive officer and head of research at JL Warren.

LVMH declined to comment. 

The case hit a social media nerve in China, with some online posts saying that 
it was “cheap” of a luxury giant like Louis Vuitton to sue a small, local tea 
chain that it doesn’t compete against.

Some said the tea company’s logo was similar to a flower pattern dating back 
to the Tang Dynasty, which ruled more than 1,000 years ago.

At the peak of the controversy, Louis Vuitton’s social media accounts on 
Chinese platforms like Douyin, Weibo — where it has more than 11 million 
followers — and the Instagram-like Xiaohongshu fell silent. No official 
announcement was issued for an event in Shanghai in late July.

“You may win in a court, and you may have the government’s seal of approval, 
but the court of public opinion is also strong and may deviate from the 
official line,” said Robert Wu, CEO of Shanghai-based market data and research 
firm Baiguan.
A Molly Tea store in Shenzhen with the brand’s logo.Photographer: Cheng 
Xin/Getty Images AsiaPac
Chinese patriotic fervor has hit other Western retailers in the past 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2022-china-nationalistic-online-shoppers/?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
. Nike, Adidas and H&M have run afoul of consumers in the Asian nation for one 
reason or another. Back in 2018, another luxury brand, Dolce & Gabbana, was hit 
hard by a boycott that hurt sales for years because an ad campaign was 
considered insensitive and racist.

The uproar against Louis Vuitton seems to have cooled a bit. Taiwanese singer 
Ouyang Nana and Chinese Olympic snowboard champion Su Yiming attended a store 
opening in the northeastern city of Changchun this month.

JL Warren sees China sales for Louis Vuitton showing further improvement this 
month, estimating a 13% drop.

Behind the Great Firewall

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

Apple unveiled its iPhone Duo foldable this week 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-09/apple-s-foldable-iphone-launch-event-gets-underway?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
 and the reaction in China — the company’s second-biggest market — was, well, 
divided.

Several hashtags appeared on Weibo, with one attracting some 28.9 million 
views. On the one hand, some praised the device’s graphics, integration with 
other Apple products and the divide down the middle of the screen as appearing 
less obvious than with other foldables.

“The crease performance is significantly better than I expected,” wrote a 
Weibo user with 1.3 million followers. “The crease is virtually unnoticeable, 
putting its aesthetic appeal firmly in the top tier.”

Many apparently ardent Apple fans indicated they planned to buy the Duo — the 
largest design change 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-10/iphone-duo-hands-on-apple-s-first-foldable-phone-makes-strong-first-impression?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
 to the iPhone in its nearly two-decade history — when it hits store shelves 
next month.

Apple could grab a quarter of the global foldable market this year and claim 
the No. 1 spot with a 41% market share in 2027, according to projections by 
Smart Analytics Global, a research and advisory firm.
Apple CEO John Ternus with the iPhone Duo.Photographer: David Paul 
Morris/Bloomberg
However, some in China suggested the price was a major issue for them.

The Duo starts at 15,999 yuan ($2,385) with 256 gigabytes of storage and 
climbs to 26,499 yuan.

“Now, smartphones have become more expensive than computers,” Weibo user 
Student Chenzhen told his 10 million followers.

Others poked fun at the word “Duo,” which means “more” in Chinese. The pun 
worked out to something like the “iPhone costs more.”

Apple representatives didn’t immediately respond to a request for comment on 
this story.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/games/pointed?cmpid=BBD091126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8739>
Pricing could be an edge for Chinese phone makers like Huawei and Xiaomi, 
which launched new or updated foldables just before Apple. The Xiaomi 18 Fold 
starts at 10,999 yuan. The Huawei Mate XT 2, a trifold device, starts at 19,999 
yuan.

In China, foldables have been a resilient bright spot in an otherwise 
declining market, yet some questioned whether the Duo orany double-screen phone 
would finally push the devices into the mainstream.

“I haven’t built trust in foldable phones yet, so I’m not considering one as 
my primary work device,” one person wrote on Weibo. “However, using an iPhone 
Duo as a secondary phone seems too extravagant.”
