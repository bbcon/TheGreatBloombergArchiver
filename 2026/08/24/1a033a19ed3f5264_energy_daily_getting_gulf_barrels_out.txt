Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Alex Longley takes a look at the success of several Persian Gulf 
nations in shipping their oil to the global market even as the US and Iran 
continue to fight over the region’s key energy chokepoint.

The clandestine shuttle trade through the Strait of Hormuz is getting 
increasingly hard to keep under wraps.

For months, the United Arab Emirates has led the way 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/uae-s-adnoc-defies-hormuz-risks-to-keep-crude-flowing-to-global-market?cmpid=BBBXT082426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260824&utm_content=8108>
 in getting its barrels out of the waterway, dodging Iranian attacks to bring 
oil onto a market thrown into turmoil by the Iran war.

The UAE’s Abu Dhabi National Oil Co. said in recent weeks it’s doing all it can
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-16/covert-mideast-oil-flows-are-keeping-global-prices-in-check?cmpid=BBBXT082426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260824&utm_content=8108>
 to deliver supplies, while adding that the situation is far from normal for 
its vessels and crews.

But the emirate isn’t alone in shipping barrels out of Hormuz. Other countries 
have been quietly doing so, too, fromIraq 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-12/uae-shuttles-oil-exports-from-iraq-through-the-strait-of-hormuz?cmpid=BBBXT082426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260824&utm_content=8108>
 to Qatar and Kuwait. Last week, they appeared to be joined bySaudi Arabia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-21/saudis-shuttle-oil-north-on-sinokor-tankers-to-evade-houthis?cmpid=BBBXT082426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260824&utm_content=8108>
, whose alternative export route via the Red Sea isunder threat 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-23/saudis-oil-logistics-roiled-again-by-houthis-red-sea-threat?cmpid=BBBXT082426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260824&utm_content=8108>
 from Houthi attack.

Together, those flows are starting to add up. 

Axios reported over the weekend that around 40 ships transited the strait 
using a southern route hugging the coast of Oman on Friday night. That totaled 
about 16 million barrels of oil, the news service said, or about four-fifths of 
regular prewar flows.
Vessels in the Strait of Hormuz off the Omani port city of Khasab in May 2026.
Photographer: AFP via Getty Images
Shipments through Hormuz are generally moving in convoys 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-01/ships-cross-hormuz-in-convoy-formation-as-gulf-oil-keeps-flowing?cmpid=BBBXT082426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260824&utm_content=8108>
 with protection from the US military. That means volumes can be lumpy, so the 
key to knowing how much crosses the waterway isn’t large single-day numbers, 
but weekly averages.

The flows remain a sensitive political topic, with the US keen to paint 
volumes being asclose to normal as possible 
<https://x.com/SecretaryWright/status/2087208693855146014> as President Donald 
Trump tries to jawbone prices lower, and Iran insistent that it controls the 
chokepoint.

Still, as ships busily ferry cargoes between one another 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-11/a-dozen-ships-switch-oil-outside-hormuz-as-barrels-keep-flowing?cmpid=BBBXT082426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260824&utm_content=8108>
 off the coast of Oman, there are signs that Hormuz flows have indeed gathered 
pace in recent weeks and are continuing to grow.

And with the oil market having eaten up supplies previously stuck at sea to 
meet high summer demand, that increase may have come just in time.

—Alex Longley, Bloomberg News

Chart of the day

Despite the continued flow of oil out of Hormuz, Iran’s shipments to Asia have 
all but dried up 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-24/iran-oil-cargoes-dry-up-even-before-us-acts-on-tehran-threats?cmpid=BBBXT082426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260824&utm_content=8108>
, driving the cost of those cargoes to the highest levels in years. Much of 
that scarcity is down to the success of a US blockade of Iranian ports, which 
has left loaded vessels trapped inside the Persian Gulf and a fleet of empty 
tankers stuck outside.

BNEF today

China is building a new wave of ultra-high-voltage direct current, or UHVDC, 
power lines, coordinating construction with renewable energy plants. UHVDC 
capacity is expected to rise about 50% by 2030 from 2025 levels, according to
BloombergNEF <https://about.bnef.com/>. Renewables will account for a larger 
share of the power flowing on the new routes, with wind and solar flows 
forecast to triple by the end of the decade.
