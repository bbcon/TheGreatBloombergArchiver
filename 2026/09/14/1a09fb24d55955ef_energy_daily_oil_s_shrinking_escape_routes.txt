Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Alex Longley looks at the oil market repercussions from Saudi Arabia’s 
closure of a key pipeline.

Last week’s hit to Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-11/saudi-oil-pipeline-that-bypasses-hormuz-shut-after-attacks?cmpid=BBBXT091426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8877>
 offers the clearest reminder yet, if it was needed, that the oil market is far 
from out of the woods when it comes to conflict in the Middle East.

The pipeline, which stretches from the Persian Gulf to the Red Sea and allows 
Saudi Arabia to bypass the Strait of Hormuz, has helped keep the kingdom’soil 
flowing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-06-03/oil-s-hormuz-alternatives-offer-only-a-partial-fix?cmpid=BBBXT091426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8877>
 during the Iran war. It was struck by militants who launched drones from Iraq 
last Thursday.

On that day, oil prices jumped more than $8 at one point as satellite images 
and word of mouth among traders spread news that the kingdom announced publicly 
the following day.

Even in the days before the attack, oil prices rallied above $100 a barrel. 
Chinese buying picked up after a hiatus, tightening physical markets after 
global inventories have run down since the war began.

Fuel markets also remain exceptionally tight, thanks to both the 
Russia-Ukraine and Iran wars, with diesel futures trading around $200 a barrel. 
That’s set to keep demand for crude elevated in the weeks ahead.

Adding to the pressure, threats from Yemen’s Houthi militants to Saudi flows 
through the Red Sea have complicated the kingdom’s efforts to keep oil moving, 
delaying some shipments as cargoes are forced to sail around Africa.

It’s against that backdrop that any restart to the pipeline will be assessed. 

If repairs are needed, Aramco has an exceptional track record. In 2019, when 
attacks claimed by Yemen’s Houthi militants knocked out 5% of global 
production, Saudi Arabiarestored 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2019-10-14/one-month-after-worst-oil-supply-halt-aramco-says-all-clear?cmpid=BBBXT091426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8877>
 output to where it was before the strikes within a month.

It also managed to bounce back quickly from an earlier attack 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-08/saudi-oil-pipeline-damage-said-to-be-limited-after-drone-strike?cmpid=BBBXT091426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8877>
 on the pipeline this year, though that one only produced only limited damage 
and didn’t require the link to be shut down, people familiar with the matter 
said at the time.

In the short term, traders will be looking to see how much of its exports the 
kingdom will divert through the Strait of Hormuz.

So far it has been relatively cautious in its approach to sailing through the 
waterway since the war began, but even before the latest attack there were 
signs of a ramp-up in Saudi Persian Gulf loadings.

Whatever happens, the oil market will need a quick fix. It’s currently 
screaming for barrels.

—Alex Longley, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

An emerging super El Niño could weaken China’s liquefied natural gas demand 
through two channels, according to BloombergNEF. Milder winter temperatures 
would trim heating needs, while stronger hydropower output next summer could 
displace gas-fired power generation. Lower consumption by the world’s largest 
LNG importer could help ease pressure on an increasingly tight global market.
