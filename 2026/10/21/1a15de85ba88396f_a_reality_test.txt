Sanae Takaichi, Japan’s first female prime minister, marks one year in office 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/one-year-on-japan-s-takaichi-faces-reality-check-on-tax-cut-defense-promises?cmpid=BBD102126_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10357>
 today, still more popular than previous premiers and well positioned to win 
another leadership race within her ruling party next year.

That’s already a feat in a country known for its revolving door of prime 
ministers. Takaichi is Japan’s fifth leader in a decade, and her predecessor 
lasted a little over a year.

The public seems to like her direct, assertive communication style, as well as 
the fact that she doesn’t come from a political dynasty and reached the top on 
her own.

Takaichi has laid out an ambitious vision 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/japan-to-map-out-first-five-years-of-takaichi-s-investment-plan?cmpid=BBD102126_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10357>
 to reinvigorate Japan’s economy with aggressive spending while strengthening 
the nation’s defenses.

Her confidence is giving hope to people who have long lived with economic 
malaise and growing anxiety over threats from neighbors such as China. While 
her controversial remarks over Taiwan angered Beijing, they haven’t cost her 
much political support at home.

Now comes her biggest challenge: turning that vision into reality.

Takaichi is trying to ease the pain of rising living costs with a sales-tax 
cut on food starting in April. Households will welcome the relief, but 
financial markets want to knowhow she’ll pay 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/japan-s-kiuchi-says-record-budget-requests-need-broad-comparison?cmpid=BBD102126_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10357>
 for the measure, estimated to cost roughly ¥5 trillion ($32 billion) a year, 
without adding to Japan’s already huge debt burden.

If she fails to ease those concerns, bond yields could rise further. That 
would mean higher borrowing costs for the government as it tries to fund her 
economic agenda — and higher mortgage payments for households.

Voters aren’t happy with everything about her administration. Takaichi has 
just suffered the first resignation of a cabinet minister over avote-buying 
scandal <bbg://screens/vote-buying%20scandal>.

That carries risks for a leader who relies on her public popularity to 
maintain her political clout.

One year in, Takaichi faces a reality check at home. — Yoshiaki Nohara
