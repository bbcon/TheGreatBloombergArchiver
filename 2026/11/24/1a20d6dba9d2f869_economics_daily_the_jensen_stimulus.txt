The Jensen Stimulus

For the past few weeks, the biggest engine by far that’s been driving US 
economic growth appeared to be sputtering. But a single move Monday —Huang 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-23/why-did-nvidia-reinstate-jensen-huang-as-ceo?cmpid=BBD112426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11752>
’s return to lead Nvidia — now reduces the risk of a stalling out.

At least, that’s the narrative that appeared to be taking hold in the stock 
market on a day when the S&P 500 climbed almost 5%, handily recouping Friday’s
big losses 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-20/us-stock-futures-dip-as-anthropic-premiere-disappoints-investors?cmpid=BBD112426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11752>
, and then some.

The ousting of Huang earlier this month had roiled the outlook for Nvidia, the 
world’s dominant player in the AI build-out. It also added to concerns about 
the momentum for the technology that had been sown by the midtermcongressional 
election results 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-04/ai-data-center-boom-is-set-for-upheaval-after-us-election-rout?cmpid=BBD112426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11752>
.

Viewed through an economic lens, rather than a national security one, the 
worry was that an Nvidia without its key leader would not only weaken 
capital-spending plans in the AI industry, but also weigh on consumer spending.

The stock boom it has driven has generated a major boost to consumption, 
especially among higher income households, during a period of persistent 
inflation and a surge in energy costs. As for the job market, data have 
suggested AI might if anything have led — so far — to a netaddition to payrolls 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-09/how-ai-may-be-aiding-not-hurting-us-employment-growth?cmpid=BBD112426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11752>
.
Huang and Trump during a meeting on AI in the White House on Sept. 29.
Photographer: Tierney L. Cross/The Washington Post/Bloomberg
As much as a third of US GDP growth this year will be due to the AI boom, 
including both investment and consumer spending, according to Mark Zandi, chief 
economist of Moody’s Analytics.

“AI is driving the economic train 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/us-economy-grows-a-faster-than-expected-3-4-most-in-a-year?cmpid=BBD112426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11752>
, and if AI is derailed it will take the economy with it,” Zandi said.

With Huang back at Nvidia’s helm, there’s potentially less uncertainty about 
the AI engine. And if all the angst over the past few weeks has made investors 
and creditors a little more sober in their views on the industry, that could, 
as this newsletter hassuggested 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-06/data-center-pullback-may-shield-real-economy-from-ai-bubble?cmpid=BBD112426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11752>
, lay the ground for a more sustainable expansion.

“Provided AI investment slows, rather than outright contracts, the economy 
should be fine,” said Stephanie Roth, chief economist at Wolfe Research. She’s 
keeping particular watch on thestock market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/asia-braces-for-more-volatility-after-us-selloff-markets-wrap?cmpid=BBD112426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11752>
. “A key issue is the equity channel, which could make the economy impact more 
severe if it slows consumption.”

Need-to-Know Research

The big selloff in the Philadelphia Stock Exchange’s Semiconductor Index in 
recent months bodes for some China macroeconomic stimulus in early 2027. That 
may not be the most obvious connection. But logic bears it out, according to 
Larry Hu and Yuxiao Zhang at Macquarie.

Even after Monday’s bounce, the SOX Index is down about 33% for the second 
half of 2026. Macquarie analysis shows this gauge of chipmaker stocks “tends to 
lead Chinese export performance by around two quarters.” And this suggests
China’s export surge 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/chinese-trade-surplus-surpasses-1-trillion-for-second-time-ever?cmpid=BBD112426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11752>
 will start to slow in the next quarter, Hu and Zhang wrote.

That in turn means Beijing wouldn’t be able to rely on trade to secure its 
growth objectives, as it did this year. In 2026, the export surge meant 
policymakers were able to get by with limited stimulus to a subdued domestic 
economy. But that wouldn’t hold for 2027 if export gains have now peaked.

“How much policy easing is required in 2027 will largely depend on whether the 
tech rout proves to be a mid-cycle pullback or something much more severe,” 
they said.
