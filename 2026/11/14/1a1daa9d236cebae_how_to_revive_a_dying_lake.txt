Planting a lake

By Eric Roston 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQdgGkZhizQ/eric-roston?cmpid=BBD111426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11430>

Uru Uru Lake in Bolivia’s mountainous west sustained people for generations 
with its pristine water and abundant fish and birds.

Then, starting in the 1990s, mining operations and plastic and trash from the 
nearby city of Oruro filled Uru Uru with toxic contamination in the time it 
takes a young woman to reach her 20s.

What is that young woman to do?
Dayana Blanco Quiroga places a plant, in a still from the film.Source: How to 
Plant a Lake
If it’s Dayana Blanco Quiroga, there’s a simple plan and a lot of work ahead. 
An Aymara Indigenous woman, she co-founded a movement — the Uru Uru Team — in 
2019 to clean up the lake by hand.

The documentary, How to Plant a Lake, packs Blanco and the community’s epic 
challenge into a brisk arc. The film directed by Satomi Blair and CG Foisy is 
the winner of the 2026 Bloomberg Green Docs competition, earning its creators a 
$25,000 grand prize, announced Friday at the DOC NYC film festival.

Over ten minutes, the documentary takes on a sweeping scale — the nearly 
drained, toxic 83-square-mile lake 12,000 feet above sea level; the blackened 
water and fields of garbage; the centuries-old history of the people living 
nearby, and the Uru Uru Team’s infinite task.

The independent filmmaking duo behind it drew on their eclectic background — 
visual arts, experimental theater and moral philosophy.
A contaminated area with garbage, plastic, bottles and toxic waste of the 
Tagarete River which flows into Uru Uru Lake in 2021.Photographer: Gaston Brito 
Miserocchi/Getty Images via Getty Images South America
Blair and Foisy operate 4Hawk Productions, the company they created to find 
and tell these stories. They traveled to Bolivia last year and met Blanco and 
her team, in collaboration with The World Around, a nonprofit that promotes 
sustainable architecture and design.

The film opens with a 35-second, low-flying drone shot above a vast, spoiled 
and desolate basin. Overdubbed to these images is the sound of Blanco, short of 
breath, driving a shovel into earth to dig up dried-up marsh plants for 
transplanting to polluted areas where the basin still has water. There, they’re 
mounted atop handmade frames, removing heavy metals and fetid poisons from the 
water. The plants themselves retain the contamination — and must be cordoned 
off so animals don’t eat them.

In addition to pollution, the lake has faced other pressures, including 
drought linked to El Niño and climate change, which almost dried it out a 
decade ago.

“If tomorrow we have to tell them what happened to the water,” says Blanco 
Quiroga, referring to future generations, “at least we will be able to say that 
we fought for our lake.”

Price carbon-or-die mode

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD111426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11430>

Electoral gains by US Democrats create an opening to advance carbon tariffs 
and constrain some of President Donald Trump’s climate policy rollbacks, 
according to Senator Sheldon Whitehouse.

“We’re looking at significant leverage with a Democratic-controlled Congress 
to abate some of the worst fossil fuel misbehavior,” Whitehouse said in an 
interview on the sidelines of the COP31 summit in Turkey.

For instance, congressional appropriators could tie Environmental Protection 
Agency funding to requirements that the EPA’s cost-benefit analysis includes 
assessments of the health harms caused by pollution.

Whitehouse, a Democrat from Rhode Island, was one of more than two dozen US 
mayors, governors and lawmakers that swept into the climate summit after the 
midterm elections ensuring that Democrats will take control of the House and 
Senate next January.
Senator Sheldon Whitehouse.Photographer: Al Drago/Bloomberg
He predicts there’s a window to move on a carbon border adjustment mechanism 
too — by capitalizing on Trump’s support for tariffs as a tool for combatting 
unfair trade policies in other nations. The mechanisms are designed to tax 
cheaper goods produced under weaker environmental rules.

Trump’s trade team has acknowledged that “when one country has high 
environmental standards and another country has lower environmental standards, 
tariffs are an appropriate remedy” to counter unfair competitive advantage, 
Whitehouse said Friday. “Trump likes tariffs,” he said. “On the principle of 
the thing, the administration is very aligned.”

Whitehouse is a longtime champion of the kind of carbon border adjustment 
policies that have been adopted in the European Union and UK, and debated in 
locations including Australia, Canada and Turkey.

There’s a growing inevitability around the approach of putting a price on 
planet-warming pollution, he said. “There’s a significant global move in the 
direction of carbon pricing,” Whitehouse said, and “we’re kind of in 
price-or-die mode.”

Representative Jared Huffman, a Democrat from California and in line to chair 
the House Natural Resources Committee next year, also said he sees 
opportunities for his party to “flex” its new power and thwart Trump 
administration efforts to sell off public lands or open them for data centers.

Trump “only understands political hardball” and is a dealmaker, he said. “I 
just think we need to be far more assertive than we’ve been in recent years,” 
Huffman said. “We’re just going to have to fight and win, and if we don’t, the 
message that we send to this president is that he can bully us.”

On the ground at COP31

By Lili Pike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXt16hTqABg/lili-pike?cmpid=BBD111426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11430>
 andJennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD111426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11430>

COP31 host Antalya saw its first major protest to take place outside the 
summit venue, with aPeople’s Climate March 
<https://350.org/press-release/peoples-climate-march-to-bring-global-call-for-climate-justice-to-the-streets-of-antalya/>
 gathering advocates, community organizations, Indigenous Peoples and others in 
the city’s historic center.

Demonstrators waving flags and banners paraded through streets to demand an 
end to new fossil fuel projects and better funding to allow nations to quickly 
move to 100% renewable energy.

The rally follows a series of smaller protests inside the event’s blue zone — 
the area reserved for accredited delegates, and where negotiations take place.
Climate change activists protest at the COP31 climate conference in Antalya, 
Turkey, on Friday, Nov. 13, 2026.Photographer: Chris Ratcliffe/Bloomberg
Meanwhile, businesses say they are relishing the warmer welcome they’ve gotten 
at COP31, saying it’s provided an opportunity to collaborate on climate 
solutions.

In the most significant shift, the Turkish presidency designated the country’s 
largest business group, the Union of Chambers and Commodity Exchanges of 
Turkey, known as TOBB, as a private-sector envoy, giving it a formal role in 
the conference. The group mounted a two-day business summit inside the blue 
zone.

It follows similar efforts by Brazil last year to improve engagement with 
business.

The growing involvement of the private sector directly inside the conferences 
has drawn some criticism, including from civil-society groups that say they’ve 
been denied similar access.

“Business is delivering solutions, and we need to be part of this,” said Marty 
Durbin, a senior vice president of policy with the US Chamber of Commerce. “Now 
we can have a more realistic conversation about what it takes.”

Among businesses inside the blue zone Saturday was tech giant Alibaba, which 
sponsored events at the China Pavilion on AI — both using the tools for climate 
action and on cutting data center emissions. Alibaba Cloud executive Roger Wang 
touted the company’s use of renewables to power data centers — reaching a 74% 
clean electricity share this year, up from 22% in 2022.
A Buddhist civil society observer paints scenes at COP31Photographer: Lilli 
Pike/Bloomberg
Other delegates found time Saturday for quieter reflection. A Buddhist civil 
society observer used a sketchbook to paint a scene looking out over the 
Turkcell Pond.

China’s new energy leap

By Bloomberg News 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/?cmpid=BBD111426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11430>

China is expected to meet all growth in power demand with new clean energy 
generation by 2030, China Central Televisionreported 
<https://content-static.cctvnews.cctv.com/snow-book/index.html?item_id=13696053328519925248&t=1794616723343&toc_style_id=feeds_default&track_id=710F974F-1DC9-4F26-B117-87B6C5F1A06A_816318352287&share_to=copy_url>
.
Solar panels at the Tengger Desert New Energy Base in Zhongwei, Ningxia, China.
Source: Bloomberg/Bloomberg
Renewable energy generation exceeded 3 trillion kilowatt-hours in the first 
nine months of the year, accounting for more than 40% of total power output, 
CCTV said, citing a report from the Academy of Macroeconomic Research. The
institute <https://www.amr.org.cn/> is under the National Development and 
Reform Commission, China’s top economic planning agency.

Wind and solar are already among the main sources of new generating capacity 
and are gradually becoming major sources of power generation, the report said. 
They are expected to become the main source of additional electricity supply by 
2030.
