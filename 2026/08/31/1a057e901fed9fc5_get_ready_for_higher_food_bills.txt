The extreme heat impact you can’t avoid

By Akshat Rathi 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUdOobfNzkQ/akshat-rathi?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>

You can count yourself lucky if you’ve not directly experienced an extreme 
weather event this year. What you can’t escape is the indirect effect: a higher 
price for your weekly grocery shop.

The heat waves that make it harder to sleep at night and the droughts that 
ruin your running track are becoming more frequent and severe due to climate 
change. During a record-breaking summer for both in parts of the northern 
hemisphere, they’ve also been reducing crop yields.

Food prices have moved higher this year as a result of trade disruptions due 
to war and extreme weather. And farmers anticipate more pain to come, with 
wonky weather tied to El Niño, a natural climate phenomenon, expected to wreak 
havoc on harvests in some places.

The Bloomberg Agriculture Spot Index, which tracks 10 major crops, is heading 
for the steepest gain since July 2012.

Read More: War, Weather Push Crop Prices to Biggest Monthly Gain Since 2012 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-31/war-weather-push-crop-prices-to-biggest-monthly-gain-since-2012?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>

“Climate change is now contributing to increasing prices around the world,” 
said Paul Behrens, British Academy Global Professor at the Oxford Martin 
School, on theZero podcast. “If you layer on top of that Strait of Hormuz, if 
you layer on top of that Ukraine grain interruptions, if you layer on that El 
Niño, you’re looking at a situation where you’ve got a big slug of inflation 
coming through.”

Why Your Food Bills Are Likely to Go Up
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/audio/2026-08-26/zero-why-your-food-bills-are-likely-to-go-up-podcast?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>
While event-driven spikes in food prices can fade over time, climate-driven 
inflation is more persistent. Extreme heat on its own may increase global food 
prices by 0.9% to 3.2% a year starting in 2035, according to a study.

As the world keeps burning fossil fuels and climate change worsens, we should 
expect to see constant upward pressure on food prices, according to Maximilian 
Kotz, a researcher at the Barcelona Supercomputing Center who co-authored the 
study with European Central Bank staff. And increases in food prices will hurt 
poor countries much harder. Grocery bills make up 35% of an Indonesian home’s 
basket of goods, while it is 40% for the Philippines, according to insurance 
firm Allianz SE.

Read More: What Climate Change Costs You at the Checkout 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-06-22/climate-change-is-making-your-grocery-bill-more-expensive?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>

On top of climate change, this year El Niño is likely to sow even more chaos 
in the food system. The cyclical natural phenomenon pushes out trapped heat in 
the Pacific Ocean, raising global temperatures and changing rainfall patterns. 
It can raise yields in some parts and cause devastation in others.

The damages to the global economy from a bad El Niño are in the trillions of 
dollars. This year’s El Niño is now expected to be stronger than any in 
recorded history, and food prices are where most countries are likely to feel 
its effects first.

Droughts in India, Australia and southern Africa lower harvests for grains and 
oilseeds, while dryness curbs palm oil production in Southeast Asia. Floods in 
Latin America hurt crop yields or lower quality of the production. Tighter 
supplies in wholesale markets then show up in higher prices in local markets.

Read More: Record El Niño Threatens Fragile Economies Squeezed by War 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-el-nino-forecast-impacts-emerging-markets-data/?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>

“We have systemically underestimated the impacts of climate change” on the 
food system, said Behrens. While the world has started to take moving away from 
fossil fuels seriously, Behrens says there’s still a long way to go for 
governments and people to change the harmful food system that feeds us.

Fortunately, many of the solutions to improve food security are also those 
that can cut prices and lower emissions from food production. One such key 
solution is to redirect farming subsidies toward agricultural practices, such 
as carbon farming, which can increase yield while cutting planet-warming gases. 
Another solution is moving to a plant-rich diet, said Behrens. That’s not a 
vegan diet, but it’s consuming a beef burger only once in two weeks, chicken 
once a week and some dairy daily.

Livestock, including for meat production, require three-quarters of the global 
agricultural land. Replacing the protein from meat with that from plants 
requires only a tiny fraction of that space, which frees up huge swathes for 
other kinds of agriculture — increasing the resilience of the food system and 
lowering the emissions that contribute to climate change.

“This is why I work in the area because I’m simultaneously utterly terrified 
about all of the impacts,” said Behrens. “But on the same side, I’m so excited 
by the opportunities.”

England’s water network buckles

By Jess Shankleman 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASoVYP9ojQ0/jess-shankleman?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>

The severe “flash drought” that has gripped England is pushing an already 
struggling water network toward breaking point — and it’s showing no sign of 
easing.
The partially revealed bed of Woodhead Reservoir following a falling water 
level, near Glossop, northern England on August 22.Photographer: Oli 
Scarff/AFP/Getty Images
The situation is particularly dire in the southeast, where one of the wettest 
<https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/about-us/news-and-media/media-centre/weather-and-climate-news/2026/an-early-look-at-winter-2026-just-how-wet-has-it-been>
 ever winters had replenished reservoirs and boreholesso much 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-24/england-sees-drought-risk-dwindle-after-months-of-heavy-rain?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>
 that they should have had enough water to get through summer. But now, water 
companies are trucking supplies around, seeking emergency restrictions and 
asking to keep drawing from environmentally protected rivers to keep the taps 
on.

Some 30 million people face hosepipe bans and supplies have been disrupted by 
heat waves and drought, which drained rivers, sparked wildfires, hurtcrops 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-26/extreme-heat-adds-pressure-to-food-supplies-already-hit-by-war?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-23/londoners-find-horrendous-cracks-in-homes-after-heat-waves?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>
 subsidence. Utilities have scrambled to avoid a repeat of1976 crisis 
<https://www.ceh.ac.uk/our-science/projects/standpipe-drought-1975-1976>, when 
water was rationed in some areas and people filled buckets from standpipes in 
the street.

“The summer of 2026 has produced the most intense flash drought we’ve seen,” 
saidJamie Hannaford <https://www.ceh.ac.uk/staff/jamie-hannaford>, head of 
water resources and drought at the UK Centre for Ecology & Hydrology.

Water companies are now preparing for the possibility that conditions 
deteriorate even further.

A hearing was held Tuesday as Southern Water <https://www.southernwater.co.uk/>
 seeks to impose tougher restrictions on customers while continuing to take 
water from the River Test even as its flows fall below a set threshold. The 
Test is a protected chalk stream, and taking more when flows are already low 
can further damage its habitats, including those used by endangered salmon.

Newsom snubbed on wildfires

By Michelle Ma 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWTYlfwjEUk/michelle-ma?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>
 andAndrew Oxford 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWXeCN-2j-Y/andrew-oxford?cmpid=BBD083126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260831&utm_content=8340>

California legislators introduced a bill 
<https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB492>
 Saturday that would update the state’s wildfire response without shifting 
liability away from publicly traded utilities.
Homes destroyed in the Eaton Fire on East Alta Loma Drive in Altadena, 
California, on Feb. 10, 2025.Photographer: Kyle Grillot/Bloomberg
The move comes after extended negotiations with Governor Gavin Newsom’s 
administration, which had been advocating for measures that would bar insurers 
from suing utilities to recover payouts for property policyholders.

The legislation is the result of a compromise with Newsom and is intended to 
bolster the state’s wildfire recovery response amid a rapidly warming climate. 
Newsom said the legislation will also speed up payouts to survivors, put new 
limits on hedge funds pursuing wildfire-related lawsuits against utilities and 
tighten safety accountability for utility executives.

“Nonetheless, this system needs full structural reform — not a partial one,” 
Newsom said in a statement, calling on lawmakers to revisit the issue next 
year, after he leaves office due to term limits.
