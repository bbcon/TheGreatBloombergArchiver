Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Tokyo-based reporter Yusuke Maekawa looks at why international interest in 
Japan’s electricity market is starting to pick up.

The global energy upheaval triggered by the war in the Middle East is creating 
opportunities for hedge funds looking to capitalize on volatile oil, coal and
gas prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-20/asian-lng-prices-jump-to-highest-since-2022-on-tanker-strikes?cmpid=BBBXT102226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10406>
.

Their next target: Japanese power.

Japan liberalized its electricity market a decade ago, but futures trading 
only started to take off in the past few years. That’s giving foreign funds a 
new way to profit from price swings, especially because Japan’s dependence on 
imported liquefied natural gasleaves it exposed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-19/japan-power-price-at-four-year-high-with-rising-coal-lng-costs?cmpid=BBBXT102226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10406>
 to global market turmoil.

Surging fuel costs are also forcing the country’s utilities to hedge their 
exposure 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-14/japan-s-power-futures-get-boost-as-utilities-hedge-price-risk?cmpid=BBBXT102226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10406>
 to wholesale electricity prices more aggressively, helping to deepen the 
futures market and draw in more financial players. That was far less common 
just a few years ago.
Central Tokyo lit up at night.Photographer: Toru Hanai/Bloomberg
The sector has already attracted foreign energy firms such as BP Plc and Engie 
SA, which launched Japanese power trading desks in the country in 2023. The 
following year,Ken Griffin’s Citadel 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-06-27/citadel-buys-goldman-alum-s-company-to-trade-power-in-japan?cmpid=BBBXT102226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10406>
 bought Tokyo-based Energy Grid Corp. and Mercuria Energy Group Ltd. started 
trading there.

Japan’s electricity market remains immature 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-23/japan-s-plan-to-secure-power-supply-may-curb-market-liquidity?cmpid=BBBXT102226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10406>
 by global standards. Monthly futures trading surged to a record earlier this 
year, but volumes were still less than a 40th of those in European powerhouse 
Germany.

Yet interest seems to be on the rise. Representatives from at least six hedge 
funds attended a Japan power symposium in the capital this week, alongside a 
contingent of commodity traders.

Among them were high-frequency trading firms including XTX Markets and Jump 
Trading — companies that cut their teeth on equities and currencies and are now 
looking for opportunities in electricity outside Europe and the US.

The biggest prize is the more liquid — and volatile — physical market. Getting 
access is harder, since firms need a local presence and people who understand 
the quirks of Japan’s complicated power system, making talent from local 
utilities highly sought after.

Millennium Management, for example, recently hired a trader 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-05/millennium-to-hire-jera-power-trader-in-japan-energy-push?cmpid=BBBXT102226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10406>
 from the country’s biggest generator.

Wall Street traders are increasingly testing Japan’s fast-growing market, but 
relatively thin volumes and regional price dislocations could bring losses 
before a position pays off. Whether they can make lasting gains will ultimately 
determine if they stick around.

—Yusuke Maekawa, Bloomberg News
