Yen History Lesson

When I moved to Japan from the US as a kid in 1985, my dad gave me a tip on 
how to think about prices in our new home country: ¥1,000 was $4. Good, I 
thought. Easy to internalize. But a few months later, he flagged that things 
had changed: ¥1,000 had become worth $5. Huh, OK then, I thought... A few more 
months passed, and he told me, ¥1,000 was now $6.

I figured I’d better start reading the newspaper to figure out for myself 
whatever was going on. It was the impact, of course, of the1985 Plaza Accord 
<https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/plaza-accord-30-years-later-0>, when 
the US and four allied powers decided to engineer a depreciation of a 
too-strong dollar. Since the era of floating exchange rates began in the 1970s, 
that agreement has stood as themost consequential 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2025-09-17/why-the-plaza-accord-is-still-the-gold-standard?cmpid=BBD081826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7813>
.

But by the 1990s — as financial deregulation took hold around the world and 
the scale of daily foreign-exchange trading volumes soared — it became 
conventional wisdom that policymakersweren’t able to fundamentally shift 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-07-17/japan-faces-diminishing-returns-from-intervention-to-support-yen?cmpid=BBD081826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7813>
 a currency’s direction. The market was thought to be just too big to be 
susceptible to intervention.

Similar sentiment 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-12/jpy-usd-why-yen-intervention-by-japan-and-us-has-limited-impact?cmpid=BBD081826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7813>
 has been rife with regard to the July 31 joint US-Japan intervention to prop 
up a yen that most economists agree is well undervalued around the current 
exchange rate. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s wager doesn’t change thewide 
gap 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-12-25/yen-bearish-voices-build-for-2026-on-cautious-boj-policy-path?cmpid=BBD081826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7813>
 in US-Japan interest rates or alter Japanese investors’ desire toplow money 
overseas 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-01-22/japan-s-love-of-foreign-stocks-risks-pushing-the-yen-even-lower?cmpid=BBD081826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7813>
, the arguments go.

The funny thing is, though, that even much more modest efforts than the Plaza 
Accord have tended to coincide with turning points.

After the dollar fell to a post-WWII low versus the yen, then-Treasury 
Secretary Robert Rubindeclared <> in March 1995 that a “strong dollar” was in 
the US national interest and ordered intervention to aid the currency. Much as 
today, some traders initially played down the move’s importance.

“Nobody’s getting bullish <> on the dollar, believe me,” one trader said at 
the time. And yet, little more than a month later, the dollar troughed, and 
began climbing. It took more than 15 years for dollar-yen to revisit March 1995 
levels.

Last month’s yen buying was the first by the US since 1998. In June that year, 
Rubin moved to support Japan’s economic revival efforts of the time,unleashing 
<> the intervention tool. About two months later, dollar-yen peaked. It didn’t 
get back to 1998 levels until 2022.

On the other side of the Pacific, it’s worth noting a June 2015 declaration by 
then-Bank of Japan Governor Haruhiko Kuroda. Appearing in Japan’s Diet, he 
signaled the yenhad fallen enough 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2015-06-10/kuroda-s-yen-warning-signals-shock-and-awe-phase-may-be-over?cmpid=BBD081826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7813>
 after a years-long campaign by Tokyo to address excessive strength. While he 
laterplayed down 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2015-06-16/kuroda-says-he-wasn-t-trying-to-influence-yen-s-exchange-rate?cmpid=BBD081826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7813>
 the remarks, history shows that the former head of currency policy at Japan’s 
Finance Ministry had “top-ticked” dollar-yen, as the saying goes. Again, for a 
matter of years.

Then take euro-dollar. US participation in intervention <> to aid the newly 
established single currency in September 2000 essentially marked its low 
against the dollar.

“Their joint interventions have been quite successful over the years,” says 
Chris Turner, head of foreign exchange strategy at ING. Nevertheless, “to be 
successful with this high-profile intervention, Bessent will need the 
fundamentals to change,” he wrote in anote 
<https://think.ing.com/opinions/yen-why-bessent-backs-himself-for-successful-intervention/?M_BT=2537034784>
 Monday — with part of that being higher BOJ interest rates. And which now are
increasingly in view 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/japan-s-bond-yields-climb-on-boj-hike-bets-fiscal-concerns?cmpid=BBD081826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7813>
.

Responding to a query to the Treasury, an administration official said it’s 
not in the business of calling market turning points or targeting a specific 
exchange rate. History shows durable moves are driven by economic fundamentals, 
not intervention alone, the official said. Coordinated action is most effective 
when it reinforces sound fundamentals and supports orderly market functioning, 
the official said.

Need-to-Know Research

Germany’s businesses these days aren’t just contending with amped-up Chinese 
competition and elevated energy costs. They’ve also got a major demographic 
challenge on their hands, according to figures compiled byZEW Institute 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-18/more-german-companies-are-closing-down-due-to-economic-headwinds?cmpid=BBD081826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7813>
 and Creditreform, a creditors’ protection association.

Almost 190,000 firms ceased operations in 2025, up about 10% from the previous 
year and the second significant annual increase in a row, the findings show. 
Only every eighth case was down to insolvency, with more and more firms 
shuttering due to reasons includingskilled-worker shortages 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-12-11/germany-s-workforce-is-set-to-shrink-even-more-than-feared?cmpid=BBD081826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7813>
, high costs and a lack of people willing to take over.

“The renewed rise in the number of business closures shows that the pressure 
on the German economy to adapt has continued to increase,” ZEW researcher 
Sandra Gottschalk said. “Demographic trends are becoming increasingly 
significant. More and more companies are closing because their owners are 
retiring and can’t find successors, even though these companies would otherwise 
be viable.”
