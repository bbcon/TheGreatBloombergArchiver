**Strait of Hormuz and Energy Markets**
The Hormuz situation remains the dominant global macro risk and the primary driver of oil price volatility. Iran confirmed a deal framework with Oman during the week, and Trump described negotiations as progressing — but a draft Iranian parliamentary bill would bar American and Israeli vessels, demand compensation from hostile nations, and impose fee structures for transit, conditions that would substantially complicate any reopening. Houthi attacks on Saudi-backed forces reaching the Najran region on Thursday introduced fresh escalation risk. The futures curve implies Brent will remain elevated at six and twelve months — an implicit market judgment that full closure is temporary — but that assumption continues to be tested. Saudi Aramco's unexpected cut to Asian official selling prices by $0.50/bbl for September delivery may reflect both diplomatic signaling and a softer demand read.

**Currency Coordination and Monetary Divergence**
The US-Japan yen intervention's structural logic rested on preventing a disorderly yen collapse that could force Japan to liquidate its $1.1 trillion-plus Treasury holdings — a scenario that would push US yields higher when the 30-year is already at its highest since 2007. By selling euros rather than dollars, the Treasury sought to avoid contributing to dollar weakness; the ECB's exclusion from pre-coordination on a transaction that directly deployed euros represents a structurally significant precedent in G7 monetary governance. More broadly, the combination of Warsh's ambiguous Fed communication and Bessent's unconventional interventions has reignited "Sell America" dynamics in FX and bond markets even as equities hold near records — a tension that remains genuinely unresolved.

**El Niño and Structural Climate Risk**
A strengthening El Niño — on track to be the most powerful in 76 years — is converging with a global economy already stressed by conflict-related supply disruptions. Emerging markets face the most direct exposure, with food security, hydropower, and inflation all vulnerable. Europe's simultaneous record-low river levels, nuclear power curtailments, and 25,000 heat-related deaths illustrate that these risks are not confined to the developing world. The convergence of climate stress, energy market disruption, and AI-driven structural shifts in trade and capital flows is broadening the range of economies exposed to compounding shocks — a dynamic that warrants sustained monitoring rather than discrete event treatment.
