**Oil Markets and Strait of Hormuz**
The scale of the oil price decline reflects the degree to which geopolitical risk premium had been built into energy markets. Iran's de facto seizure of the Strait of Hormuz had severely disrupted tanker traffic, with Iran clinging to enriched uranium stocks and Gulf Arab states absorbing strikes. The pause in hostilities — and talks brokered via Oman on restarting Hormuz shipping — has released some of that premium. However, ECB researchers caution that conditions remain "highly volatile," and that the pre-crisis market had an unusually favorable starting point: a supply surplus of approximately 2.5 million barrels per day, large inventories, and aggressive strategic reserve releases. A prolonged disruption scenario has not been eliminated.

**Tariff Fatigue and Structural Trade Costs**
The non-reaction to the latest US tariff round is analytically significant. The Supreme Court's February ruling constrained presidential trade discretion, companies have adapted pricing strategies — including "trickle-up" incremental price increases under long-term contracts — and bond markets are less reflexively hostile given fiscal revenue needs. Yet the long-term structural damage to investment planning, captured in what analysts are calling the "Great Hesitation," may be the more consequential effect: capital expenditure decisions with 10-year horizons are increasingly difficult to authorize under persistent policy uncertainty, creating a slow-moving drag on global growth that does not manifest in short-term volatility.
