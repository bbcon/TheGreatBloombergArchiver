Yet behind the serene take, China has cause for concern.

One big reason is that while Democrats will likely stymie Trump on domestic 
issues, he’ll have room to maneuver on foreign policy. Speculation is mounting 
thathe’ll seek what he sees as wins 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-04/trump-set-to-push-overseas-agenda-after-midterms-wipeout-at-home?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
 by attacking Iran again or challenging Europe even more on Ukraine.

It is also entirely possible that Trump once again takes aim at China as he 
did in his first term when a full-blown trade war erupted — a move that would 
shatter the current, fragile ceasefire.

His administration remains frustrated with China on issues like rare earth

Trump could also look to ramp up levies on imports as a way to cement his 
Tariff Man legacy, though to what extent that would apply to Beijing is an open 
question given the leverage it has wielded.

Then there is the slightly longer-term picture.

Political thinkers in Zhongnanhai will no doubt be wondering what the blue 
tsunami means for the presidential election in two years. They’ll note that the 
midterm blue wave in 2018, during Trump’s first term, led to Joe Biden winning 
the White House.

And it is safe to say that Biden is not fondly remembered in Beijing. He 
trampledChina’s red line on Taiwan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2025-11-21/china-s-fury-at-japan-reminds-region-of-its-ultimate-red-line?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
 repeatedly by saying the US would come to the democratic island’s defense if 
it was attacked — though each time officials in Washington walked back the 
remarks.

Biden also called Xi a dictator 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2023-11-17/biden-reiterates-xi-jinping-is-a-dictator-video?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
. He hit China with chip export controls, US investment curbs and more 
restrictions on its telecoms. He also stepped up security cooperation with the 
Philippines and elevated the importance ofthe Quad, which also includes Japan, 
Australia and India 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-09-20/what-s-the-quad-alliance-and-why-doesn-t-china-like-it?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
 — all of it ultimately focused on countering China. The list goes on.

Could a potential next Democratic president have a very different take on 
China than Biden did? Sure. But there’s very little sign in the US that aside 
from Trump, bipartisan sentiment on Beijing has changed much lately.

And, while China does have that rare earths cudgel to hit back, it would much 
prefer the kind of cooperation that Trump has been affording.

For Xi, a confrontational US president who rallies American allies in the 
region — where China not only wants the US out, but greater sway — would be a 
major step in the wrong direction.

The Eternal Problem

Beijing is once again throwing its weight behind the stock market, a 
development that is raising questions over whether investors will stick around 
once the official buying stops.

What started as a year of hope has turned into one of disillusionment 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/china-tech-frenzy-turns-into-year-of-dismay-for-retail-traders?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
 for China’s 250 million retail investors. Many of them plowed money into 
stocks on the bet that the drive for self-sufficiency in AI, semiconductors and 
robotics would produce a new generation of winners.

Optimism turned feverish as the country joined a global tech rally, with 
spectacular stock debuts and overnight fortunes.
The Lujiazui financial district in Shanghai.Photographer: Raul Ariano/Bloomberg
Then came October, when the benchmark CSI 300 plunged the most in more than a 
decade. The tech-heavy Star 50 lost a stunning 24%. More than $3 trillion of 
value was wiped out as major indexes erased a year of gains.

State-backed funds known as the “national team” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-02/how-china-uses-its-national-team-to-influence-the-stock-market?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
 increasingly becamethe buyer of last resort 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-04/china-boosts-market-defense-with-national-team-buying-buybacks?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
. On Monday, Beijing stepped in more forcefully,enhancing tools first deployed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-02/china-to-boost-use-of-stock-support-tools-to-stabilize-market?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
 during the 2024 market rescue.

The initial enthusiasm quickly faded. The Star 50 slid the next day.

The gloom has spawned much dark humor. Some smaller investors likened the 
market to scam compounds in Myanmar.

Over Halloween, some younger investors wore spooky stocks costumes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-02/china-s-gen-z-dons-spooky-stock-market-costumes-for-halloween?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
. In the eastern hub of Hangzhou, one woman dressed as a chart of the 
plummeting Shanghai Composite Index. Her getup included a handful of leeks — a 
reference to internet slang for naive retail investors who are repeatedly 
“harvested” by larger forces in the market.

One Weibo user joked that worrying about trading during a recent session was 
“10,000 times scarier than ghosts during Halloween.”

Behind the jokes is a longstanding problem. The Chinese stock market has 
historically functioned more effectively as a fundraising vehicle for companies 
than as a place for households to compound their wealth.

Companies, including capital-hungry tech firms, have for years gotten more out 
of the market through listings and secondary share sales than they have 
returned to investors through dividends and buybacks.

Persistent questions about the quality of listed companies, corporate 
governance and shareholder returns haven’t helped.

Beijing has shown it wields considerable firepower when it comes to stemming a 
selloff. By midweek, Beijing’s defense of the market was having a visible 
impact. The Star 50 had its best session since July. (Worriesabout the AI 
rollout 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-06/china-s-ai-propelled-growth-may-take-a-blue-wave-hit-be-says?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
 were hitting stocks at week’s end.)

But state buying and share buybacks can only go so far. The prolonged property 
slumphas hit household wealth and consumer confidence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/china-s-economy-in-six-charts-growth-exports-and-the-property-slump?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
. As in other parts of the world, investors wonder whether AI-related earnings 
can justify lofty valuations.

The national team can buy equities and the central bank can provide cheaper 
liquidity. Those measures have put a floor under the market, for now at least.

Getting households to believe stocks are a reliable place for their savings is 
a much harder job — one officials don’t seem to know how to do just yet.

Behind the Great Firewall

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

Some in China have been captivated of late by the story of an impoverished 
young man and his humble wedding that turned into something much bigger than 
anyone expected.

It all started when a 24-year-old man named Xu Chenghong from Yunfeng village, 
in a remote area of the southwestern province of Yunnan, took to social media 
to invite people to his nuptials.

The man, who goes by Xiao Xiao Xu, said his parents died when he was young and 
he’d quit school to earn a living. He said he didn’t have friends or family to 
fill more than three tables at a banquet on Saturday, so the more the merrier.

He told people not to bring gifts or red envelopes with cash because he had 
saved enough for the big day with his various odd jobs.

And come they did, by the thousands, some of them apparently from far away.

Videos of ordinary people arriving in Xu’s village went viral. Xu said he 
thought about 3,000 wedding crashers showed up. People brought gifts like 
household appliances, food and even some live hogs transported by tractor. Cars 
had to jockey for room on the village’s narrow dirt roads.

Dozens of people volunteered to work as chefs. The party grew so large that 
local authorities moved it to a public square for safety reasons.

“Xiao Xiao Xu’s wedding could easily be called the wedding of the century,” 
said blogger Huadaojun, who has 765,000 followers on Weibo.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/games/pointed?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
China has seen this kind of flash mob thing happening in the past 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-11-11/china-u-turn-on-night-biking-craze-shows-obsession-with-control?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
. Back in 2023, Zibo, a small factory town in the coastal province of Shandong,
became a hotspot 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-04-28/chinese-gen-z-tourists-flock-to-shandong-s-zibo-in-post-covid-travel-boom?cmpid=BBD110626_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11038>
 because word spread among mostly young tourists about cheap barbecue meals.

In tough economic times, a fairly cheap train ticket and hotel room are just 
the thing people need for a little break. And local officials love the arrivals 
because they put their town on the map and bring in a bit of tourism spending.

Some people on the internet did raise questions over whether Xu’s wedding was 
scripted, saying that in the past he’d been linked to an influencer known for 
staging big stunts. They also wanted to know what the couple would do with the 
gifts. Local officials said they would look into the matter but so far no 
evidence of wrongdoing has surfaced.

In a social post on Douyin, Xu apologized, but only for causing such a big 
stir in his village. He thanked people for coming, saying: “I am grateful to 
all the netizens who traveled such a long way.”
