Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
energy reporter Dan Murtaugh explores a downturn in China’s fuel supplies ahead 
of the peak demand season.

China’s coal and gas production is faltering at the worst possible time, just 
as winter heating demand kicks in.

While consumers elsewhere have struggled with high energy prices since the 
Iran war, Beijing has been cushioned by years of preparation: ample crude 
stockpiles, electrified transport, vastrenewable capacity 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-23/china-aims-to-boost-renewable-energy-consumption-53-by-2030?cmpid=BBBXT101926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10262>
 and stable domestic production of coal, oil and gas.

But that final pillar is wobbling.

Output of coal — still the feedstock for about half China’s power generation — 
has plummeted since adeadly explosion <> in May that triggered inspections and 
kept many mines shuttered in Shanxi province, the country’s biggest production 
hub.

Officials have called for the restart <> of these mines in time for peak 
winter demand, but data released Monday showedoutput fell 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-19/china-s-coal-output-drops-as-concerns-build-over-winter-supply?cmpid=BBBXT101926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10262>
 year-on-year for a sixth month in September.

The reduced production threatens to chip away at the country’s hard-won buffer 
against the global energy shock.
Smoke billows from stacks at a coal-fired power plant in Shanxi province, 
China.Photographer: Kevin Frayer/Getty Images AsiaPac
Natural gas output also declined narrowly in September. Production has been 
flat since the spring, ending years of prodigious growth. The main reason 
appears to be offshore wells, with drilling giant Cnooc Ltd. citing maintenance 
at major fields as it reported a 7% drop in second-quarter gas volumes.

It’s too early for China to panic. Stockpiles, after all, remain at 
historically healthy levels, meaning there’s no pressing need to bring in 
additional supplies while seaborne coal and liquefied natural gas prices are 
near multiyear highs.

Coal inventories at six major power generators tracked by consultant China 
Coal Resources are above the five-year average, while undergroundgas storage 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-16/china-builds-gas-buffer-for-winter-as-iran-war-heightens-risk?cmpid=BBBXT101926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10262>
 was 80% full at the end of September — more than enough for winter.

Wind power is recovering from an early-year lull and nuclear reactors are 
coming online. Added to that, winter forecasts so far point to milder weather 
that should keep heating-fuel demand in check.

Still, forecasts can change. For Beijing to feel truly confident that its 
energy-shock buffer will remain intact, it needs its fossil fuel producers to 
get to work.

— Dan Murtaugh, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Corporations announced 26 clean energy power purchase agreements totaling 
3,740 megawatts in September, up from August, according to BloombergNEF. 
Year-to-date volumes reached 36.4 gigawatts, ahead of 33 gigawatts over the 
same period in 2025. Technology firms accounted for 81% of September capacity.
