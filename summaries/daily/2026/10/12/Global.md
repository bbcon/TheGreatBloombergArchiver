**Energy Markets: Supply Risk and Diplomatic Uncertainty**
The Houthi strikes on Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport — the second attack in as many days — represent a significant escalation, demonstrating the militia's capability to strike critical infrastructure at the heart of Saudi Arabia. The attacks have grounded hundreds of flights and prompted BP and Shell executives to withdraw from a major energy conference in the capital. Despite the security deterioration, oil markets are not yet pricing a supply disruption premium commensurate with the risk: Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman has sought to reassure markets that the kingdom's supply capacity remains intact, and the country is actively exploring pipeline routes that bypass Persian Gulf chokepoints. Trump's proposed Russia-Ukraine energy ceasefire adds a separate variable: even if implemented, Russian diesel exports cannot return to pre-conflict levels of 800,000–900,000 barrels per day before December at the earliest, given the extent of refinery damage.

**Global Macro: Debt and Monetary Policy Divergence**
The IMF-World Bank annual meetings in Bangkok have placed advanced-economy fiscal sustainability at the center of the policy agenda. IMF Managing Director Georgieva has identified developed-nation fiscal positions as among the primary risks to global financial stability, alongside AI disruption and the energy shock. US 10-year yields at 5.24%, French yields at multi-decade highs, and Japan's long-term rates at elevated levels collectively represent a tightening of global financial conditions that — if sustained — may constrain central banks' ability to ease even where domestic inflation data would otherwise support it. The IMF's assessment that global central banks are likely to enter 2027 with tighter-than-expected monetary settings underscores the extent to which the energy price shock is complicating an already difficult disinflation path.
