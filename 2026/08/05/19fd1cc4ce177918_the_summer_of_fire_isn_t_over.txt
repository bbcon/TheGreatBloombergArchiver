Everywhere has fire weather now

By Brian K Sullivan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AF4NRCMRVn8/brian-k-sullivan?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
,Leslie Kaufman 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUZ8izNmriE/leslie-kaufman?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
, andTodd Woody 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVl1IPrK6vs/todd-woody?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>

The US is enduring one of its worst wildfire seasons in years, with blazes 
scorching millions of acres. It’s part of a global pattern of hot, dry 
conditions that favor fires becoming more common with climate change.

Fires that erupted over the weekend in and around Spokane, Washington, have 
destroyed hundreds of structures and forced more than 60,000 from their homes — 
about 10% of the population. Three major fires have burned more than 8,000 
acres and have not been contained, according to InciWeb, a federal 
fire-tracking website. There are 17 total fires currently active across 
Washington, Public Lands Commissioner Dave Upthegrove said on Monday.

Meanwhile, ongoing fires in Greece have consumed tens of thousands of acres 
and triggered evacuations. These conflagrations follow thehistoric fires that 
raged in France and Spain 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-31/why-wildfires-in-france-and-spain-are-so-hard-to-put-out?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
 last month, displacing hundreds of thousands of residents.

All these fires broke out in different regions and ecosystems. But they’re 
examples of the same underlying shift: Rising temperatures are worsening 
wildfire risk. It’s happening in two ways at once. First, traditional fire 
country is becoming hotter and drier in all regions. Second, the range of 
landscapes that can sustain destructive blazes is growing broader.

“We’re seeing both, in the same moment, in different places, in different 
ecosystems,” said Daniel Swain, a University of California climate scientist 
who studies wildfire. “The commonality between all of them is that there has 
been a substantial period of record-breaking warmth and/or dryness.”

Swain describes the phenomenon as “lifting the flammability veil.” 

Some parts of the world have always been susceptible to burning but rarely 
became hot and dry enough, for long enough, to support large fires. Now global 
warming is “putting a thumb on the scale, producing severe and unprecedented 
heat waves,” said Noah Diffenbaugh, a professor of Earth system science at 
Stanford University.
A vehicle and structure burned on N. Skyline Dr. following the wildfire in 
Spokane, Washington, on Aug. 2, 2026.Photographer: Young Kwak/AP Photo via 
FR159675 AP
Trees lose moisture over extended periods of exceptional heat and dryness, 
becoming progressively more combustible. That has a stronger impact in 
ecosystems like Canada’s boreal forests, California’s Sierra Nevada mountains 
and parts of temperate Europe that evolved under relatively cool, moist 
conditions. Unlike many forests in the American Southwest, where trees are 
adapted to seasonal drought and frequent fire, these forests can become 
tinderboxes when subjected to weeks of unusual heat.

Last winter, the Pacific Northwest, Rocky Mountains and large parts of 
California experienced a snow drought. This robbed the landscape of its natural 
replenishment that happens every spring and early summer.

But extreme precipitation, not just the lack of it, plays a role in worsening 
fire conditions too. Deluges promote the growth of vegetation that then becomes 
fuel. Swain noted that heavy rains battered Spain in February prior to this 
summer’s heat waves.

“That is the same sequence of events that led up to the Los Angeles fires in 
2025,” he said.

With fires now happening everywhere all at once, nations will be constrained 
in their ability to come to each other’s aid by dispatching firefighters and 
equipment. Communities that didn’t think they neededevacuation plans 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-07/wildfire-evacuation-grows-more-urgent-as-blazes-move-faster?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
 or fire mitigation measures will need to reevaluate.

If the planet’s warming is lifting the flammability veil, as Swain says, the 
question is whether we’re prepared for what lies beneath.

El Niño’s economic shockwaves

By Lauren Rosenthal 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXCV-bQx5Dk/lauren-rosenthal?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
,Mary Hui 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXDwzJNHmiM/mary-hui?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
,Ben Westcott 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVmRUBH3adI/ben-westcott?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
, andLaura Curtis 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQx5ShZM0Yw/laura-curtis?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>

The El Niño on track to become the most powerful of the past 76 years is 
strengthening rapidly, posing a major threat to fragile economies already 
roiled by conflict in the Middle East.
A farmer walks past drought-damaged corn plants on a farm in Tacuba, El 
Salvador.Photographer: Camilo Freedman/Bloomberg
Since forming in June, the event, which some scientists are calling a “Super 
El Niño,” has boosted the odds of drought, heavy rainfall and extreme heat 
across parts of the Global South. Such hazards pose a direct threat to human 
lives and livelihoods, risking crops and food security for millions.

El Niño isn’t a new phenomenon by any means. It occurs on a cycle, as 
sustained warming in the Pacific Ocean interacts with the atmosphere to reshape 
weather patterns for up to a year or more. For traders, governments and 
households, El Niño’s arrival can offer useful if imperfect signals about what 
the next several seasons may hold. But it’s a mistake to think of this event as 
yet another one-off episode.

Global temperatures have grown significantly hotter over the past decade, 
raising concerns that some of the upcoming impacts may be particularly severe. 
A potentially record-breaking event may also offer a glimpse of what’s to come 
as climate change pushes temperatures higher, extreme weather intensifies and 
geopolitical tensions simmer.

For emerging markets, and especially for nations in Latin America, Africa and 
Asia, El Niño’s intense heat and erratic rains will likely compound months of 
higher oil import costs and strained supply chains warped by the war in Iran.
The Port of Callao in Callao, Peru, where the fishing sector has been 
disrupted due to the warming of the Pacific waters off the country’s coast.
Photographer: Marco Garro/Bloomberg
In countries including India and Colombia, El Niño events have repeatedly 
amped up pressure on energy systems and food commodities that help feed 
billions globally. Economic growth is alreadyslowing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-15/peru-economy-misses-expectations-on-weak-fishing-and-manufacture?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
 in Peru as the nation’s lucrative anchovy fishery — which supplies exports
valued 
<https://apps.fas.usda.gov/newgainapi/api/Report/DownloadReportByFileName?fileName=Oilseeds+and+Products+Annual_Lima_Peru_PE2024-0003.pdf>
 up to $1.8 billion — remains closed due to warming ocean waters. El Niño may 
be contributing to volatile monsoon conditions in India. Cumulative rainfall 
through August 3 was 12% below normal nationally, raising risks for rain-fed 
crops andhydropower 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-07/el-nino-dry-spell-hits-india-s-hydropower-output-straining-grid?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
 in the regions with the largest deficits.

In the Philippines and Indonesia, policymakers have watched the price of 
pantry staples swell afterworking 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-17/indonesia-philippines-set-to-hike-rates-as-caution-prevails?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
 for months to contain inflation exacerbated by the Iran war. El Niño risks 
sending those numbers even higher. Drought conditions from a typical El Niño 
may cause consumer prices to jump by nearly two percentage points in both 
countries, according to research from Bloomberg Economics based on past 
episodes.

“El Niño acts as an intermediate between economics and the climate, which is 
already warming and changing,” said Hazem Krichene, a senior climate economist 
at Allianz. “It might be an issue in those countries already suffering from a 
lot of geopolitical tensions and a lot of local conditions that are really, 
really hard.”

Japan’s heat relief

By Yui Hasebe 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWye-460yPw/yui-hasebe?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>

The glass door closes, and a curtain of cool air spills over your head, neck 
and shoulders, delivering instant relief from the punishing summer heat.
Trusco Nakayama’s Do Hiemon Box.Source: Trusco Nakayama Corp.
This is what it’s like to chill in the Do Hiemon Box 
<https://www.trusco.co.jp/en/lp/dohiemon/>, an oversized stainless-steel 
refrigerator that looks big enough to fit a human being, because that’s exactly 
what it’s designed to do.Trusco Nakayama Corp. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/9830:JT?cmpid=BBD080526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260805&utm_content=7363>
 has sold about 200 of the ¥1.5 million ($9,500) booths to steel mills, 
construction sites, golf courses, universities and leisure facilities since it 
went on sale earlier this year.

As global temperatures climb, Japan’s summers have been getting hotter and 
more humid, with readings regularly exceeding 35C (95F). The country’s weather 
agency has had to come up with new terminology for sizzling-hot days, while 
national broadcaster NHK routinely warns viewers of heatstroke risk. During the 
season, pharmacies and household-goods stores fill their shelves with 
blue-toned products promising cooling relief.
