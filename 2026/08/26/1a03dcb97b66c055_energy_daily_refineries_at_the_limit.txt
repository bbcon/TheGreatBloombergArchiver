Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Will Kubzansky looks at why the world’s growing reliance on US 
refineries is creating new risks.

Fuel markets are the tightest they’ve been in years thanks to the ongoing 
disruption in the Strait of Hormuz and Ukrainian attacks on Russian energy 
infrastructure.

Oil refineries in other regions have stepped up to fill the gap in supply, and 
nowhere is that more true than the US, where plants are running flat-out to
produce 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/fuelmakers-run-us-plants-at-fastest-pace-since-iran-war-erupted?cmpid=BBBXT082626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8195>
 the most fuel since prepandemic times.

But as the global market increasingly relies on the output of US refiners, it 
also grows more vulnerable to issues that, in less troubled times, might 
otherwise seem routine.

Refineries are remarkably complex and potentially dangerous pieces of 
infrastructure. Equipment frequently breaks down or catches fire, which can 
take production offline for weeks at a time.

Since 2015, the US has lost an average of around 3% of its annual refining 
capacity to unplanned outages, according to consultants Rystad Energy.

Now, as many refineries run at or close to maximum production, some are also 
deferring <bbg://news/stories/TJOCELT96OSH> planned maintenance 
<bbg://news/stories/TIZMSMKJH6V4> in an effort to capture historic profits 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-18/diesel-margins-top-100-a-barrel-to-reach-record-high-as-supply-crunch-grows?cmpid=BBBXT082626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8195>
. That raises the likelihood of unforeseenbreakdowns 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-19/us-oil-refiners-are-running-so-hard-they-are-risking-breakdowns?cmpid=BBBXT082626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8195>
.
The Motiva Port Arthur Refinery in Port Arthur, Texas.Photographer: Mark 
Felix/Bloomberg
There are also the quotidian dangers posed by the elements, from hurricanes to 
wildfires (and even wildlife — a brief outage at a Valero Energy Corp. plant in 
Memphis, Tennessee, wasblamed on a raccoon <bbg://news/stories/TK2Y7ZKK3NY8>).

All this leaves little margin for error for the global fuel market, as a major 
refinery outage now could squeeze fuel prices even higher.

Average gasoline prices remain stubbornly above $4 a gallon in the US. Diesel 
has been climbing back above $5.50 in recent weeks toward the records last seen 
in 2022, and looks particularly vulnerable with US supplies at their lowest for 
this time of year since 1996.

Meanwhile, the US set a fresh weekly record for diesel exports in the first 
week of August, with Morocco, Brazil and Turkey the leading recipients, 
according to Vortexa Ltd. And shipments to Brazil — now cut off from the 
Russian supplies it previously relied on — are also likely to tick up in the 
weeks to come as the country’s planting season approaches.

Any new supply disruption in the US, then, would likely be felt far beyond its 
borders.

—Will Kubzansky, Bloomberg News

Chart of the day

Ukraine’s two-pronged attacks on Russia’s oil refineries and Black Sea ports 
arehitting the country’s fuel supplies 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-25/ukraine-s-double-barreled-drone-strikes-snarl-russian-oil-flows?cmpid=BBBXT082626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8195>
 and preventing Moscow from diverting crude into exports. In the four weeks 
through Aug. 23, overseas crude flows fell to 3.46 million barrels a day, 
tanker-movements data compiled by Bloomberg show. Shipments had previously 
risen as the strikes on Russia’s refineries left more crude available for 
export, but that increase is now unwinding after drone hits on oil tankers cut 
shipments from the Black Sea.

Germany 42% and the UK 42%.
