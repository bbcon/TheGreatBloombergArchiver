The Only Way Is Up (for Bond Yields)

A lot goes into bond valuations, as survivors of corporate finance classes 
will be all too aware. Even in the current era of algorithms, quants and 
electronic trading, they’re driven by human nature and subject to the same 
tendency to overshoot that bedevils the stock market.

But last week was something different. As one investment banker put it, the 
10-year Treasury, the most liquid long-term security on the planet, is “trading 
like a meme stock.” This is its course over the five days with marks for the 
two most important economic releases: the ISM manufacturing data on Thursday 
and Friday’s non-farm payrolls:

Friday’s rebound after the payroll data is a particular head-scratcher. 
Employment looked considerably weaker than had been forecast - which should be 
a deterrent to future rate rises by the Federal Reserve. In particular, average 
hourly earnings growth is now in a marked decline, and is likely to come in 
below the headline rate of inflation (not due until Oct. 14) for the sixth 
successive month. That’s awful and argues at the margin a) that affordability 
is a growing problem and b) that the economy might not be in the best shape to 
withstand another four rate hikes after all:

In addition to lower-rate-friendly data, Fed officials spent the week 
amplifying a message that they haven’t necessarily decided on another 25 
basis-point hike this year. After the Chair Kevin Warsh, the central bank’s two 
most important officials are arguably his deputy Philip Jefferson and the 
governor of the New York Fed John Williams. Bothoffered very similar messages 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-02/warsh-s-top-fed-deputies-step-in-to-give-markets-a-clear-message?cmpid=BBD100526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9673>
 in an action that certainly looked like a coordinated form of forward guidance.

The market took heed. By the end of the week, futures were discounting a 
clearly slower pace of tightening; another hike this year, as Williams and 
Jefferson told traders, is no longer a done deal. But the market’s opinion on 
the ultimate destination is not changed and futures still price in a 4.75% Fed 
funds rate a year from now:

Dovish talk by senior Fed officials plus a disappointing employment report 
should have been the perfect setup for yields to correct lower - but evidently 
traders didn’t feel like going home for the weekend long in bonds. Friday saw a 
renewed sell-off, and the 10-year yield ended the week 6 basis points higher.

The revival in volatility is a further problem, as it effectively tightens 
monetary conditions by making it harder to use bonds as collateral. The 
Treasury under Scott Bessent made a determined effort to keep a lid on 
volatility after last April’s Liberation Day tarrif announcement, which worked 
well until the attack on Iran produced another geopolitical shock. In August, 
Treasury attempted to peg back the rise by increasing bond repurchases. Now, 
the lid appears to have come off:

“I can’t control the bond market,” Bessent admitted on Friday 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-03/bessent-downplays-worries-on-rising-yields-ai-bubble-concerns?cmpid=BBD100526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9673>
. “What I can do is get people to slow down and think.” His efforts to do that 
since August, when he appeared genuinely to believe that he could control the 
market, haven’t been remotely successful. That might well have been the 
catalyst for the surge in yields. If so, his new, lesshubristic 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-08-07/situational-hubris-catches-a-hedge-fund-unawares?cmpid=BBD100526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9673>
 approach could help - but there is a lot of momentum behind driving yields 
upward.

For now, earnings growth in the tech sector 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-04/wall-street-s-ai-party-is-on-edge-as-soaring-yields-raise-risks?cmpid=BBD100526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9673>
 has stopped the bond rout from having an obvious impact on the stock market, 
which is far more closely watched by consumers. There’s no major economic data 
due this week. So if there’s any opportunity for a respite, it ought tobe here 
now <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4dx6_p2tLJg>.

Trump’s EV Boost

Pain at the pump may just have given the US electric vehicle industry a second 
chance. Tesla and Rivian have increased sales recently, despite President 
Donald Trump’selimination of tax credits 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-07-03/what-killing-tax-credits-means-for-the-electric-vehicle-market?cmpid=BBD100526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9673>
 intended to encourage EV adoption:

So far, the lack of an apparent off-ramp and the president’s talk of 
escalation in Iran have keptBrent crude futures trading at more than $100 a 
barrel 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-04/why-is-oil-still-almost-100-a-barrel-war-ship-squeeze-shortages?cmpid=BBD100526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9673>
, far above the 200-day moving average of $88. That has combined with refining 
problems to push gasoline up by 54% and diesel by 84% at the pump since the war 
started. Diesel is on a 229-day streak above its 200-day moving average, its 
second longest since the pandemic:

Higher gas prices have coincided with a shift toward hybrid vehicles, and away 
from full EVs. Hybrids’ share of sales, as shown in this Oxford Economics 
chart, peaked at 17.1% in May, up from 13.9% in February before the Iran 
conflict:

Hybrid sales slipped after June, in line with falling gas prices. But with 
gasoline jumping nearly 10% in September, Oxford Economics’ Grace Zwemmer 
expects demand to remain solid, with monthly sales staying close to 200,000 
units. Hybrids are still preferred because of EVs’ problems withspotty charging 
infrastructure 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-03-07/ev-charging-demand-is-up-in-the-us-even-as-auto-sales-dip?cmpid=BBD100526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9673>
, high sticker prices, limited model choices, and concerns about running out of 
power, or “range anxiety.”

EV makers’ share prices rallied after the 2024 election, at which point 
Tesla’s Elon Musk was about to become the president’s closest adviser. Since 
Musk’s controversial pay package was cleared in court last December, its price 
has dragged:

Higher oil prices have helped China, which has led the US in EV exports 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-11/china-sets-70-ev-goal-for-new-car-sales-in-five-year-plan?cmpid=BBD100526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9673>
 since 2021. Beyond the billions of yuan the country has poured into R&D, tax 
rebates and credits, geopolitical forces continue to shape the market. The 
green transition, oil price volatility since Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in 
2022, the US repeal of the EV tax credits and, now, war in Iran have all buoyed 
demand.

For a Chinese economy still struggling to recover from the post-pandemic 
housing market collapse, any catalyst for exports is welcome. As Gavekal 
Research’s A.J. Cortese points out, the current energy shock played into the 
Communist Party’s goal to prioritize energy security. Beyond being the world’s 
leading EV exporter, Chinese companies are moving at breakneck pace to dominate 
the broader supply chain behind these vehicles. This could further erode the 
competitive advantages the US still holds. Cortese says:
We need to understand that the Chinese companies are no longer content with 
simply improving cost efficiencies for these components, but they’re trying to 
develop more integrated solutions that they can both deploy at home and 
increase their margins elsewhere.
US EV makers need a meaningful structural shift. BloombergNEF cut its outlook 
for the second consecutive year after the withdrawal of federal policy support. 
EVs are now expected to account for only 17% of US passenger vehicle sales by 
2030, down from estimates of 27% last year and 48% in 2024. Gavekal Research’s 
Arthur Kroeber argues the US shot itself in the foot:
There’s probably a pathway for US companies to become very successful in the 
long run in new energy vehicles. But within a totally closed ecosystem, can you 
do that without very strong support from the federal government?
Overall vehicle affordability 
<https://www.morningstar.com/news/marketwatch/20261003114/cars-have-become-unaffordable-for-many-americans-heres-what-the-numbers-show>
 has declined as the Fed raises rates, while the threatened 50% tariffs on 
Canadian autos would worsen the problem. But at least higher gas prices have 
given the EV sector a fighting chance again.

—Richard Abbey

Garbage In, Garbage Out: Data Edition

Some things never change. The latest non-farm payrolls figures 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/live-blog/2026-10-02/us-employment-report-for-september?cmpid=BBD100526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9673>
 featured huge revisions and big surprises, making clear judgments difficult. 
Distinguishing signal and noise is hard enough at the best of times, but the 
added uncertainty fromhuge changes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-03/world-economy-latest-us-employers-dial-back-hiring-wage-growth-slows?cmpid=BBD100526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9673>
 to numbers after they’re published - payrolls have been revised downward by 
60,000 jobs over the last two months - makes it almost impossible.

This raises the questions why markets are still geared to treat Non-Farm 
Payrolls Friday as such an event. Here is a splenetic reaction to the latest 
numbers from Peter Tchir of Academy Securities:
Thousands and thousands of people staring at screens. Not teenagers checking 
out TikTok, but highly educated, well-trained professionals, staring at 
screens. Millions, even billions, maybe even trillions of dollars, yen, euro, 
etc., poised to move around based on the data that comes out at 8:30 a.m.Then 
we get another mishmash of data, so different than expectations, with revisions 
bigger than the number itself, and are supposed to make something of the data?
He concluded:
We will use the numbers because “we have nothing better” but that increasing 
seems like a giant cop-out! Seriously, if you were in a class and were told 
billions of dollars would be made or lost on a number that even the people 
looking at the number don’t believe, you’d shake your head in incredulity, that 
this really was going on. Not just for a month or two, but for years and years!
I had a sense of deja vu, and had a look through the archive. Almost exactly 
20 years ago, on Nov. 2, 2006, I published a similar quote from Nigel Bond, 
then a strategist at Barclays, formy old employers at the Financial Times 
<https://www.ft.com/content/43f60ff4-6a98-11db-83d9-0000779e2340>:
This writer has always suspected that the bond market’s concentration on the 
payrolls number had very little to do with the economic import and a good deal 
to do with an endemic gambling dependency. Rather like roulette, the payrolls 
number is more or less random, but within a reasonably well-defined range. The 
economics profession sets the range for the roulette wheel and the 
random-number generation techniques of the Bureau of Labor Statistics do the 
rest.
His conclusion then was that “bussing in grannies for a monthly Bingo-Friday 
would be the more optimal way to allocate capital in the global bond market.”

Warsh does have a data task force working on what numbers the Fed should heed, 
and Tchir points to proliferating private sector attempts to measure employment 
- although many suffer from the legitimate noise that comes from big 
week-to-week and seasonal variations in hiring. A resolution is overdue - and 
markets really should wean themselves off NFP Friday if they possibly can.
