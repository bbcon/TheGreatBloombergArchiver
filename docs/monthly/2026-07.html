Monthly Brief

July 2026

US

  • June CPI fell month-on-month for the first time in six years, with core flat — well below consensus — though the print predates Hormuz re-escalation and PPI ex-food and energy remained elevated at 4.7% YoY.
  • Q2 S&P 500 EPS is tracking 26% YoY growth, but the Magnificent Seven shed $797 billion in market value on a single Thursday in late July — their largest single-day loss since April 2025 — as AI capex return assumptions came under scrutiny.
  • FOMC minutes revealed "a few" members favored an immediate rate hike at Warsh's first meeting, with 30-year Treasury yields above 5% for a 12-session stretch — their longest run since 2007.
  • Trump announced 10%–12.5% tariffs on roughly 60 economies and declined to renew USMCA, opting for annual reviews across $1.6 trillion in North American commerce.
  • The Supreme Court ruled 5–4 that Trump cannot immediately remove Fed Governor Lisa Cook, though the narrow procedural framing has emboldened the administration to explore alternative pathways.

Inflation and the Macro Outlook

The June CPI print — headline falling month-on-month for the first time since 2020, core flat — was the most broadly encouraging inflation data in years and triggered the second-largest post-CPI repricing of Fed meeting odds since 2008. The Atlanta Fed sticky-price gauge fell to its lowest since the pandemic. However, the relief is analytically narrow. The improvement was heavily driven by a 12% decline in gasoline prices, a condition that reversed sharply as Brent approached $100 in the final week of July. Core pipeline pressures remain elevated: PPI ex-food and energy at 4.7% YoY, home healthcare costs up 10.7%, and labor-intensive services showing persistent stickiness. May core PCE — the Fed's preferred gauge — had earlier printed at its sharpest non-pandemic reading in approximately two decades. The June payrolls miss in early July added a growth dimension to the picture: hiring decelerated materially below consensus, prompting Bloomberg Economics to defer rate cuts to 2027. The labor market narrative shifted within a single week — JOLTS openings had earlier moved back above pre-pandemic highs — illustrating the volatility of incoming data.

Monetary Policy and Fed Independence

Chair Warsh used Congressional testimony to push back firmly against premature optimism, reintroducing M2 as a policy input and characterizing the rate debate as an open "family fight." The directional signal from June FOMC minutes is unambiguous: the next move is more likely up than down, with "almost all" participants indicating firming would be warranted if inflation remained elevated. Market-implied probability of a July hike rose to one-in-three by month-end, before partial reversal on the CPI print. The Supreme Court's Cook ruling paradoxically emboldened the administration: allies are treating the decision as a procedural roadmap, and a companion ruling granting broad authority to dismiss SEC and CFTC officials carries direct implications for financial oversight. Reports that the administration is exploring grounds to remove Fed Governor Barr add a further political dimension.

Markets and Earnings

The S&P 500 closed H1 up 9.6%, its best quarter since 2020, with the KBW Bank Index outperforming at 13% year-to-date ahead of bank earnings — JPMorgan posting its highest-ever quarterly profit, Goldman recording $7.42 billion in trading revenue. AI-adjacent results were more mixed: Alphabet beat revenue estimates but guided capex as high as $205 billion, Tesla missed Q2 profit by 37%, IBM fell more than 25% on a single day in its worst session on record, and Netflix guided for slowing sales growth. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 13% month-to-date after rallying 105% from its March low to its June peak. Corporate insiders sold shares at a near-record pace through the period. Bitcoin fell to a 21-month low, down 33% in H1, while gold dropped 14% in Q2 before recovering 1.4% on the early-July payrolls miss. ---

Asia

  • China Q2 GDP grew 4.3% YoY — the weakest reading since at least 1992 outside the pandemic — missing consensus and the official 4.5%–5.0% target range, prompting forecast cuts from Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and ANZ.
  • Samsung reported Q2 operating income of 89.4 trillion won, beating consensus by approximately 6%, yet shares fell more than 10% in Seoul; the Kospi declined 28% from its June high by late July, entering a technical bear market.
  • TSMC reported a 77% YoY jump in quarterly profit, beat consensus, and raised full-year capex guidance to as much as $64 billion — yet its ADRs fell roughly 3.8% in premarket as semiconductor valuation concerns outweighed the fundamental beat.
  • The Bank of Korea raised interest rates for the first time since 2023, explicitly citing AI-driven demand as a structural inflationary force; New Zealand's RBNZ also hiked 25 basis points to 2.5%, its first increase in three years.
  • Vietnam's Q2 GDP printed at 8.4% YoY, materially above consensus and among the strongest readings in emerging Asia, as registered FDI reached $34.65 billion through end-June, up 61% YoY.

China: Growth Miss and Policy Response

The 4.3% Q2 GDP print crystallizes China's structural imbalance with unusual clarity. Electronics and IT contributed 1.4 percentage points to quarterly growth, illustrating how AI hardware demand is buffering broad domestic weakness — fixed-asset investment contracted 5.7% and property investment fell 18% in H1. Consumer spending barely expanded. The Iran conflict reduced Chinese oil imports to 2016 levels, and 60% of Q2's industrial production slowdown was concentrated in the oil supply chain. The GDP deflator turned positive for the first time in three years, but economists characterize this as cost-push rather than demand-driven, widening the squeeze on downstream manufacturers. Goldman Sachs estimates approximately 1.8 trillion yuan in undeployed government bond quota as a fiscal buffer, though Beijing's willingness to deploy it aggressively remains uncertain. State-linked ETFs absorbed record inflows in late July — the ChinaAMC STAR 50 ETF attracted an estimated 13.8 billion yuan in a single session — to support equity markets ahead of CXMT's $9.8 billion IPO. KKR and AEW Capital Management are both reportedly seeking to exit Chinese real estate holdings at 40–50% losses.

Korea and the Semiconductor Volatility Spiral

The Kospi's near-doubling in H1 — driven by Samsung and SK Hynix on AI memory demand — proved acutely fragile. Samsung's record quarterly results triggered a sell-the-news response in Seoul that cascaded globally: Micron fell 5.1%, Western Digital 5.6%, and Seagate 4.4% on the same session. The CSOP SK Hynix 2x leveraged ETF in Hong Kong, at $13 billion the largest of its kind globally, mechanically amplified intraday swings. Foreign investors extended an equity exodus to eight consecutive sessions in late June, with the won weakening toward 2009 lows. Regulators subsequently halted new listings of leveraged single-stock ETFs tied to Samsung and SK Hynix, a belated recognition that product design contributed to the volatility spiral. SK Hynix's US-listed ADRs traded at a 51% premium to Seoul shares by month-end, reflecting structural arbitrage impediments rather than fundamental divergence. The Bank of Korea's rate hike added a further headwind for leveraged domestic investors.

Japan, Regional FX, and AI Geopolitics

The yen weakened to approximately ¥164 per dollar by late July — breaching four-decade lows despite ¥11.73 trillion in intervention between April and May — as persistent real rate differentials overwhelmed policy signals. Goldman Sachs revised its yen forecast to 165 per dollar from 155, citing a Bank of Japan tightening path too gradual to compress differentials. Japan's Finance Minister offered verbal warnings while calling on the ¥293.6 trillion GPIF to increase domestic asset allocations. Xi Jinping's personal appearance at the World AI Conference in Shanghai and the launch of a China-led AI Cooperation Organization with 29 member nations — including Brazil and Indonesia — formalized Beijing's bid to shape global AI governance, a geopolitical dimension to what markets had treated primarily as a capital allocation story. China's test of a submarine-launched ICBM into the Pacific drew expressions of grave concern from Japan and Australia, signaling continued development of a credible nuclear second-strike capability. ---

Europe

  • Euro-area flash CPI came in below consensus in late June, reducing near-term ECB tightening pressure; the ECB held rates unchanged in late July, with Lagarde signaling September as live for a further increase.
  • UK 10-year gilt yields breached 5% for the first time since 2008 in late July, as markets assessed incoming Prime Minister Andy Burnham's fiscal credibility; the bund-gilt spread widened to nearly two percentage points.
  • ASML raised full-year net sales guidance to €43–45 billion, approximately 12% above the prior analyst consensus midpoint, driven by accelerating AI chip equipment demand.
  • France faces a fiscal deficit potentially approaching 7% of GDP by 2030, per a Finance Ministry-commissioned report, requiring €126 billion in savings over five years; Marine Le Pen confirmed she will contest the 2027 presidential election despite an upheld embezzlement conviction.
  • Volkswagen brought a restructuring plan potentially eliminating up to 100,000 jobs and closing four German factories before its supervisory board, reflecting EV transition pressures and softening Chinese demand.

ECB Policy and Eurozone Dynamics

The ECB entered July from a position of relative confidence, having led G7 peers with a post-Iran-war rate hike. The softer-than-expected eurozone flash CPI print in late June, combined with retreating oil prices during a brief ceasefire window, reduced near-term tightening pressure — ECB Governing Council member Kazaks confirmed the bank was "in no rush" to hike in July, and the hold was reached quickly. However, internal divisions remain meaningful: Bundesbank President Nagel and Austria's Kocher indicated openness to further tightening, while Chief Economist Lane warned of lingering second-round inflation effects from the energy spike. The Hormuz re-escalation in the final two weeks of July, with Brent approaching $100, materially complicated the September decision — Lagarde noted some policymakers had considered an immediate July hike as oil spiked. Thursday's euro-area private-sector activity beat and declining consumer inflation expectations added a further contradictory signal. Lagarde's stated intention to remain in post only through end-2026 introduced a succession uncertainty that could affect the pace of policy adjustment heading into 2027.

UK Political Transition

Andy Burnham entered Downing Street in late July — Britain's seventh prime minister in a decade — following a Labour leadership contest effectively resolved in a single day when he secured nominations from 322 of 403 MPs. Sterling rose to its strongest level in a year on a trade-weighted basis on reports of a centrist Chancellor appointment, but the gilt market delivered an immediate credibility test: 10-year yields rose 8.1 basis points to breach 5% on his first full day. The appointment of John Healey as chancellor implied upward budget pressure on defense, while Burnham's first announced measure — scrapping a 5% energy bill tax from October — reflects cost-of-living political imperatives that sit in tension with gilt market demands for consolidation. The May GDP beat provided a marginally constructive starting point, though UK companies still plan to raise prices by 4% over the coming year — double the MPC's 2% target — suggesting the Bank of England's hold at 3.75% reflects genuine uncertainty rather than comfort.

Germany and Corporate Restructuring

Germany's CDU-SPD coalition agreed on €10 billion in annual income tax relief and labor market reform, with Chancellor Merz projecting GDP growth above 1% in 2027 against a current 2026 forecast of 0.5%. Factory orders rebounded after months of Hormuz-related suppression and export data showed an unexpected May rise for a fourth consecutive month. The Hormuz re-escalation mid-July reintroduces energy uncertainty that could interrupt the sequential improvement. Volkswagen's supervisory board consideration of a plan eliminating up to 100,000 positions and closing four factories represents one of the most consequential corporate decisions in European industry this year. Separately, Europe's summer heat wave produced an estimated 20,000 excess deaths through July — Spain declared a national state of emergency over wildfires near Madrid, the first such declaration on record — with second-order economic costs for tourism and agricultural output that have not yet been fully quantified. ---

LatAm

  • Washington announced 25% tariffs on selected Brazilian imports effective July 22, following a USTR unfair trade practices investigation — the most concrete bilateral trade action against a major Latin American economy in the current cycle.
  • Washington's decision not to renew USMCA — opting for annual reviews — is the most consequential development for Mexico, creating structural uncertainty for an export-dependent economy underpinned by roughly $500 billion in annual bilateral US trade.
  • Moody's upgraded Argentina's sovereign credit rating for the third time in under three months, validating Milei's fiscal consolidation, though his approval rating fell to its lowest in over two years amid corruption concerns.
  • Keiko Fujimori narrowly won Peru's presidential election, becoming the country's first female president on a market-friendly, security-focused platform; Peru's central bank held its benchmark rate at 4.25% for a tenth consecutive meeting.

Mexico and USMCA Uncertainty

The Trump administration's refusal to seek a clean 16-year USMCA extension introduces sustained structural uncertainty rather than immediate rupture. By retaining annual review leverage, Washington can pursue full termination if demands — including raising regional vehicle content thresholds from 75% to 82% and requiring 50% US-sourced value — are unmet. The auto sector, representing nearly 18% of trilateral trade, faces the sharpest near-term exposure. Toyota's announced shift of some truck production from Mexico to Texas was cited by the administration as validating the approach; a broader replication by other multinationals would pose a structural risk to Mexico's automotive export base. Oxford Economics explicitly revised down forecasts for all three North American economies, and the announcement landed amid World Cup celebrations — a sharp contrast between the symbolic co-hosting partnership and the fracture in trade relations.
