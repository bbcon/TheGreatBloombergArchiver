Portland’s unhealthy air

By Brian K Sullivan 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AF4NRCMRVn8/brian-k-sullivan?cmpid=BBD080626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7425>

Wildfires burning across Washington and Oregon have left Portland with the 
worst air quality among major cities around the world, edging out Kinshasa in 
the Democratic Republic of Congo.
A Portland resident takes a picture of smoke from the Bench and Beachcomb 
fires along Highway 26 near Madras, Oregon, on July 25Photographer: Alex Milan 
Tracy/Sipa/AP via SIPAPRE
The air in both cities is classified as unhealthy, although the quality is 
improving in the Congolese capital on Wednesday, according to IQ Air, a 
commercial monitoring service. At the other end of the ranking, Canberra, Oslo 
and Stockholm have the cleanest air, with Washington DC currently the highest 
ranked US city.

Air quality alerts have been issued for both sides of the US-Canada border as 
smoke from wildfires across the Pacific Northwest continues to cloud the skies. 
That includes parts of Idaho, Montana, Wyoming and Colorado, as well as British 
Columbia and Alberta. There are also pockets of very unhealthy air around 
Oregon and Washington.

COP31’s carbon credits focus

By Alastair Marsh 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQvVwWuhxcQ/alastair-marsh?cmpid=BBD080626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7425>

Governments will be pressed at the annual United Nations climate conference 
<bbg://news/topics/COP> to help boost demand for carbon credits and unlock more 
than $50 billion in additional annual finance for developing economies to 
tackle tasks like reducing emissions, according to the summit’s host.

Elevating the use of carbon markets will be among objectives for the COP31 
meetings in Turkey in November, Murat Kurum, the country’s environment minister 
and president of the talks, said in an interview.

“Mobilizing private capital at the speed and scale required means ensuring 
every available tool is being used effectively, and carbon markets are one of 
the most underused,” Kurum said. “We want to see carbon markets take their 
rightful place in the climate finance architecture.”

To facilitate this aim, Turkey confirmed Thursday it had joined the Coalition 
to Grow Carbon Markets <https://coalitiontogrowcarbonmarkets.org/>, an 
initiativeformed last year 
<https://coalitiontogrowcarbonmarkets.org/government-coalition-to-issue-shared-principles-on-carbon-credit-use/>
 and led by countries including the UK, Kenya and Singapore to bolster 
corporate demand for high-integrity carbon credits. The move builds on the 
enactment last year of the country’s firstclimate law 
<https://iklim.gov.tr/en/turkiye-s-first-climate-law-adopted-by-parliament-news-4703>
, which provided the legal basis toestablish 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-07-03/turkey-approves-carbon-market-plan-as-part-of-net-zero-target?cmpid=BBD080626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7425>
 a domestic emissions trading scheme.

How much water?

By Michelle Ma 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWTYlfwjEUk/michelle-ma?cmpid=BBD080626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7425>

The artificial intelligence boom has inspired one of the largest 
infrastructure build-outs seen in decades: Tech firms are investing billions 
into constructing thedata centers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-ai-data-center-redesign/?cmpid=BBD080626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7425>
 powering AI. This has meant massive new demands on water supplies. Data 
centers need water to cool their hot servers and also consume it indirectly 
through the water required to generate the electricity they use. That indirect 
water footprint is typically orders of magnitude higher.
Demonstrators ahead of a Box Elder County Commission meeting to decide whether 
to approve the construction of a large data center in Tremonton, Utah, in May.
Photographer: Natalie Behring/Getty Images via Getty Images North America
Tech giants say they’re taking steps to reduce their water consumption, and 
some tech leaders have suggested 
<https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/23/openai-altman-defends-ai-resource-usage-water-concerns-fake-humans-use-energy-summit.html>
 the water problem is overblown. But as climate change intensifies drought 
stress in many regions, especially inthe western US 
<https://www.drought.gov/news/new-research-finds-rising-heat-driving-western-us-droughts-2024-11-08>
, data centers’ demands on water are raising questions from the public. An
investigation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2025-ai-impacts-data-centers-water-data/?cmpid=BBD080626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7425>
 by Bloomberg News last year found that data centers are increasingly being 
built in places that already have high competition over scarce water resources.

Data center water usage is projected to soar 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-27/arizona-s-ai-boom-is-under-threat-by-dwindling-colorado-river?cmpid=BBD080626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7425>
 as the AI build-out continues. Lawmakers are beginning to reckon with what 
that could mean for local communities.
An Amazon Web Services data center in Haymarket, Virginia, US, on Wednesday, 
July 9, 2025.Photographer: Nathan Howard/Bloomberg
There are two ways to look at data center water usage: consumption and 
withdrawal. Consumption measures how much of the water is effectively lost, 
usually through evaporation. Withdrawal measures how much water a data center 
draws from a source. Withdrawn water that doesn’t evaporate is typically 
discharged to surface water or groundwater and is eventually reusable after 
treatment. However, the water may be discharged warmer or with altered 
chemistry, which can poseecological risks 
<https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/TP121/how-data-centers-impact-surface-and-ground-waters/>
.

In the US, data centers directly consumed about 17.4 billion gallons of water 
in 2023 – equivalent to about 160,000 US households – and could consume as much 
as 74 billion gallons annually by 2028, mainly for cooling, according to
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory 
<https://escholarship.org/uc/item/32d6m0d1> estimates. (National data for 2024 
and 2025 is not available.) Aseparate analysis 
<https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02705> by UC Riverside 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/0360489Z:US?cmpid=BBD080626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7425>
 professor Shaolei Ren and his coauthors estimates that US data centers could 
consume as much as 110 billion gallons annually by 2030, or about 7% of total 
annual consumptive use in US public water systems.
