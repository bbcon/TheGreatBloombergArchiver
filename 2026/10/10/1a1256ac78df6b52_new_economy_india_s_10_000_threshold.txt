A week ago, I boarded my seventh flight to India in 18 months. Before then, 
the closest I’d been to this country was Bangladesh. So you might say I’ve been 
taking a crash course in all things Indian.

One reality was obvious from the start: No visitor could ever fully understand 
aneconomy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-08/four-views-on-india-s-finance-boom-and-the-tougher-second-act?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9935>
 or society as complex as India’s over a series of business trips. Everywhere 
you look, the disparities and contradictions are stark and confusing.

For example, Mumbai has so much wealth that one billionaire 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-08-26/indian-tycoon-ambani-buys-dubai-s-most-expensive-home-ever?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9935>
 built a skyscraper — all 27 floors and 400,000 square feet — as his personal 
residence. Yet nearby, shoeless children use patches of dirt in street medians 
as makeshift playgrounds. An estimated 42% of the city’s population lives in 
slums.

I needed a framework to process what I was taking in.

Then, at a meeting last month with Ashwini Vaishnaw, India’s minister for 
railways and broadcasting as well as electronics and information technology, I 
found one.

Vaishnaw and I began by trading thoughts on the tumult in the US Treasuries 
market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/wall-street-sees-an-ominous-sign-in-bond-market-s-latest-selloff?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9935>
 and growing concerns over the safety and security of artificial intelligence. 
I then asked him what he wanted to discuss in a roundtable with foreign chief 
executives at next week’s New Economy Forum in New Delhi. His answer surprised 
me.

Instead of India’s great advances in semiconductor manufacturing and 
electronics, an immense source of pride the two of us had previously covered 
(and the subject oflast week’s 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-10-03/india-s-manufacturing-future-is-not-the-china-model-new-economy?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9935>
 newsletter), Vaishnaw said he wanted to talk about the trajectory and 
resilience of his country’s economy. Later, as I was saying goodbye, he held my 
arms and said: “All that matters is that we reach $10,000.”

By $10,000 he meant annual GDP per capita, a key threshold on the way to Prime 
Minister Narendra Modi’s ultimate goal of transforming India into a “Viksit 
Bharat,” or developed country, by 2047 — the 100th anniversary of independence 
from British rule.

India’s current GDP per capita of about $3,000 a year 
<https://www.imf.org/external/datamapper/NGDPDPC@WEO/OEMDC/ADVEC/WEOWORLD/IND> 
makes it a lower-middle-income country, in the same ballpark as Kenya. Getting 
to $10,000 would bring it into the ranks of upper-middle-income economies such 
as Brazil and Georgia. Recently, Vietnamjoined the upper-middle income club 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-07-11/vietnam-s-paramount-leader-to-lam-has-a-grand-plan-for-his-nation-new-economy?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9935>
. The US, by comparison, has a GDP per capita of about $94,000.

It’s worth noting that India’s own modeling <https://www.niti.gov.in/node/2162>
 envisions per capita GDP of more than $18,000 by 2047. That would mean growth 
of about 7% annually for the next two decades — a torrid pace only a small 
number of economies have sustained, most spectacularly China.

For me, what mattered most was the resolve in Vaishnaw’s voice and the look in 
his eyes. I understood them to mean: “Whatever it takes.”

Finally, India — at least the India I was seeing — began to make sense. The 
disparities and contradictions reflected apolicy agenda 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2023-08-05/new-economy-saturday-india-s-big-reforms-come-with-controversial-baggage?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9935>
 with national prosperity as an overarching priority.

That explained how New Delhi can have one of the world’s greatest subway 
systems but also some of themost polluted air 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-12-03/india-smog-why-delhi-has-one-of-world-s-worst-air-pollution-problems?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9935>
 of any major capital; how India was able tocreate and operate UPI 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-08-20/india-s-upi-needs-reform-not-us-pressure?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9935>
, a ubiquitous and instantaneous national digital payments network used by more 
than half a billion people, while many of its cities still struggle to collect 
garbage, drain rainwater and process sewage; how India can construct a 
world-class, high-speed expressway that will cut the travel time from Delhi to 
Mumbai in half, yet still leave most of the country’s local roadways full of 
gaping potholes.
Narendra ModiPhotographer: Prakash Singh/Bloomberg
A government has only so much money, time and administrative capacity. Some 
problems inevitably are left to wait.

The same whatever-it-takes framework also helps explain why Modi has 
tolerated, and at times facilitated, an extraordinary concentration of wealth 
in a handful of industrial families. (Context for Elon Musk’sjibe this week 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/musk-attacks-unnamed-indian-oligarchs-for-locking-out-starlink?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9935>
 about whether one of them — Mukesh Ambani, owner of the aforementioned 
27-story Mumbai residence — is the “real boss” of the nation.)

The magnates are the ones who built the ports, refineries, telecommunications 
networks, power plants and factories India needed to attract investment and 
spur growth.

Since Modi first took office in 2014, India’s GDP has roughly doubled, to 
about $4 trillion. Now, he wants to foster an expansion of the banking system 
to provide more credit, lure more manufacturing from China and make India a 
leader in theglobal space race 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-03/india-s-space-industry-hunts-for-its-spacex-moment?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9935>
 — all part of the march to $10,000 per capita and — eventually — Viksit Bharat.

And if that creates even more disparities and contradictions, you’ll know why.
