The Buy-Side Secretary

In rescuing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-09/bessent-says-20-billion-us-argentina-currency-swap-finalized?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
 Argentina’s peso last October and joining Japan last week in moves to prop up 
the yen, Scott Bessent is proving in some ways the most active international 
statecraft practitioner as Treasury secretary since Robert Rubin — who bailed 
out Mexico and similarly tag-teamed with Tokyo on theyen 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-07/yen-surrenders-nearly-half-its-gains-from-us-japan-intervention?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
.

Both came to the job from finance careers involving trading. But they appear 
to have drawn different lessons from their experiences, which may offer insight 
as Bessent takes on the raft of economic, fiscal and geopolitical challenges 
currently confronting the Treasury, with its broad remit.

Rubin came up from the arbitrage desk at Goldman Sachs, making calculated bets 
on perceived pricing anomalies. In hismemoir <>, he described how his approach 
was dictated by a conviction that “nothing” can be proved to be certain. He 
spoke frequently about having to make “probabilistic” judgements.

Bessent came from a different corner of Wall Street: the hedge-fund world. 
Recounting <https://x.com/theallinpod/status/1902223565203665084> perhaps the 
most famous currency trade in modern history — the 1992 George Soros bet 
against the British pound, which Bessent played a part in — he highlighted a 
key takeaway in a podcast last year.

Soros had a “really sour look” when he heard that the proposal was to bet 100% 
of his fund on a crash in sterling. Bessent said Soros’s reaction was, “Why 
wouldn’t you do three times that?” He ended up making over $1 billion.
Scott BessentPhotographer: Jim Lo Scalzo/EPA/Bloomberg via EPA/POOL
The point was the power of leverage 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-07/us-sale-of-euros-for-yen-intervention-blindsided-europe-ft-says?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
. And at the Treasury, Bessent has similarly demonstrated a willingness to adopt
unorthodox methods 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-05/treasury-secretary-scott-bessent-draws-on-hedge-fund-tactics-to-help-rescue-yen?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
 to maximize returns. Among them: encouraging Japan to use a Federal Reserve 
facility to potentially expand its firepower in clawing the yen back from the 
four-decade lows it reached last month.

If Japanese authorities want to intervene to the tune of, say, $34 billion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/boj-data-point-to-34-billion-japanese-fx-intervention-on-friday?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
, one option is to sell that amount of their stockpile of US Treasuries (which 
was $1.14 trillionat last count 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-14/foreign-holdings-of-us-treasuries-rose-in-may-led-by-canada?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
), and then intervene to buy yen. But once that wad is shot, it’s gone.

Or, Tokyo could take those $34 billion of Treasuries to the Fed, and swap them 
for dollar cash via a program known as theFIMA Repo facility 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/fed-s-fima-repo-facility-went-unused-for-8th-straight-week?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
. See the Bloomberg explainer on thathere 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-05/japanese-yen-intervention-why-bessent-wants-fed-to-expand-fima-backstop?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
. While the maneuver isn’t free, it would allow Japan to revert back to its 
previous position once markets have calmed.

Even if Tokyo doesn’t use it, Bessent’s signaling of the possibility — echoed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/japan-s-use-of-fed-facility-may-ease-pressure-on-treasury-market?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
 by his Japanese counterpart — could in and of itself have an impact on 
dollar-yen trading. And not only did Bessent encourage Japan to use the tool, 
but he urged the Fed to consider “up-sizing” it. Thatraised eyebrows 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/bessent-seeks-fed-help-in-defending-yen-in-unusual-call?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
 among some former officials, including Mark Sobel.

“In my era, Treasury secretaries were loath to speak about issues related to 
Fed monetary operations in public,” said Sobel, who served at the Treasury for 
well over three decades.

But for a Treasury chief looking to maximize returns, the Fed tool offers 
valuable leverage. His bet on the yen itself can be seen as offering broader 
returns. He said on CNBC Tuesday that an “overly weak yen 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-04/bessent-says-yen-level-problematic-for-japan-other-currencies?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
” contributed to the 1997-98 Asian financial crisis. So stabilizing it now 
could help avert other Asian currencies tumbling, he suggested.

“He’s acting more like a macro trader than a traditional Treasury secretary,” 
said Stephen Myrow, who served at the Treasury in George W. Bush’s 
administration and is a managing partner at Beacon Policy Advisors. “We never 
had a true buy-side person” in recent history, he said.

Need-to-Know Research

The European Union is beginning to raise the cost for imported products that 
involve high carbon output, with the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism getting 
phased in from the start of this year. An unintended consequence may be a newly 
competitive Chinese export, according to Gavekal Research.

To comply with the new rules 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-01/china-calls-eu-carbon-border-tax-unfair-warns-of-counter-moves?cmpid=BBD080726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7469>
, China “is responding with ‘zero-carbon’ industrial parks, which combine 
renewable power, industrial clustering and shared carbon accounting,” AJ 
Cortese, Gavekal’s new energy lead analyst, wrote in a note this week. “If the 
model succeeds, it could enable China to start exporting industrial 
carbon-management systems to much of the developing world.”

In December, Beijing selected the first of 52 national zero-carbon industrial 
parks, Cortese wrote. They amount to “experiments in operating renewable 
microgrids around multiple industrial users.” As Chinese firms get engineering 
and construction experience, “that can be exported alongside individual bits of 
kit such as solar panels, batteries and power generation equipment,” he said. 
Countries “with weak or unreliable electricity grids could be natural 
customers.”
