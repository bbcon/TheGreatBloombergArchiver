**Energy Markets and Hormuz**
The Hormuz disruption is now reshaping global LNG trade structurally, not merely cyclically. With approximately one-fifth of global LNG supply effectively trapped inside the Persian Gulf — and no timeline for normalization — buyers across Asia are accelerating efforts to secure US supply. At the Gastech conference in Bangkok, industry participants privately acknowledged that LNG traffic through Hormuz may not fully normalize even after a ceasefire, driving a durable reorientation toward US Gulf Coast exports. Long-term Henry Hub-linked contracts are currently delivering LNG to Asia at roughly $8 per million BTU against spot prices near $30, a differential that creates powerful incentives to lock in supply but is drawing in rising construction costs and competition from data center power demand. Saudi Arabia's rerouting of crude through Hormuz following the East-West pipeline shutdown increases its own exposure to the conflict zone it had previously avoided.

**US-China Trade and AI**
The prospect of a pre-summit trade truce extension tempers, but does not eliminate, the structural tensions between the two economies. China has continued narrowing the AI capability gap with the US despite export controls, and the Trump administration's meeting with Anthropic to discuss AI safety risks reflects a broader uncertainty about how aggressively to manage technology diffusion. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is reportedly attending Trump's state dinner with Xi — an illustrative signal of how deeply AI competition is embedded in diplomatic interactions. The WTO has characterized this as the most consequential moment for international commerce in eight decades.
