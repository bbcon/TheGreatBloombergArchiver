Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Asian Agriculture Team Leader Megan Durisin takes a look at the initial 
reaction to the US-China leaders’ summit.

For commodities traders waiting for a readout on last week’s Trump-Xi meeting, 
measuring progress largely hinges on tariff cuts on a slew of agricultural 
products.

And at this stage, the markets are giving the summit between US President 
Donald Trump and his Chinese counterpart Xi Jinping the thumbs down.

China released today a list of more than 1,600 products 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/us-releases-details-on-30-billion-of-goods-with-tariff-cuts?cmpid=BBBXT092826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9370>
 on which it plans to cut levies, as part of a pledge by both sides to lower 
duties on $30 billion of each other’s goods.

The roster includes a vast array of farm products — covering major crops like 
rice, corn and wheat — to more obscure items like camel meat and live eels. 
Separately, Beijing also said it willbuy more US coal 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/china-s-us-coal-buying-pledge-looks-like-an-easy-promise-to-keep?cmpid=BBBXT092826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9370>
.

Tariff cuts could entice private traders back into agricultural markets, 
helping Beijing meet a promise to buy at least $17 billion annually in American 
farm products, on top of a separate soybean purchase agreement.

And yet, going by grains futures 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/grains-fall-as-traders-seek-china-buying-signs-after-tariff-cuts?cmpid=BBBXT092826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9370>
 on Monday, the market isn’t that impressed. Wheat, corn and soybeans were all 
down in Chicago, likely because traders want to see signs that more Chinese 
purchases will actually materialize.
The Trump-Xi summit hasn’t lived up to the hopes of commodities traders.
Photographer: Daniel Heuer/Bloomberg
Soybeans — China’s largest agricultural import — were notably absent from the 
list, with only seed varieties included. The country is already more than 
halfway to meeting a 25 million-ton purchase target for the year, but that’s 
mainly been from state-owned buyers.

Outside of agriculture, very little has been announced on energy and metals. 
The market had been hoping for Chinese pledges to buy more US liquefied natural 
gas if tariffs of 15% were removed by Beijing.

There was also nothing on rare earths, China’s biggest point of leverage over 
the US. Beijing was looking to offer moreexport licenses 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-21/china-rare-earth-magnet-exports-to-us-drop-before-xi-trump-meet?cmpid=BBBXT092826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9370>
 of the critical minerals as a bargaining chip in the talks, Bloomberg reported 
last week.

Beyond tariffs, Trump urged Xi during the summit to increase Chinese production
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-26/us-says-china-to-buy-10-million-tons-of-coal-in-2027-and-2028?cmpid=BBBXT092826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9370>
 of refined petroleum products to help stabilize global supply.

It’s unclear if more easing of tariffs or other measures will be announced in 
the coming days, while a big agricultural purchase could change the picture. 
But for now, crop markets are serving as an initial gauge of sentiment on the 
outcome of the summit — and it’s not positive.

—Megan Durisin, Bloomberg News

Chart of the day

In Japan, one of the world’s most densely populated and mountainous countries, 
developers arerunning out of sites 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-27/japan-has-no-space-for-solar-farms-apart-from-old-golf-courses?cmpid=BBBXT092826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9370>
 big enough to host major solar projects. After an initial boom in 
installations that peaked in 2015, the buildout slowed and the deceleration is 
posing a problem for the government, which is targeting solar supplying as much 
as 29% of electricity by 2040, nearly triple the current proportion. Golf 
courses, which multiplied in Japan during the boom years of the 1980s, are some 
of the last tracts of open land available for solar.

BNEF today

Global oil refining capacity could rise by a net 4.2 million barrels a day 
from 2026 to 2030, based on BloombergNEF’s bottom-up analysis. This is 2.6 
million barrels a day above the net rise from 2021-2025. That build-out could 
outpace demand for refined products by 2030.
