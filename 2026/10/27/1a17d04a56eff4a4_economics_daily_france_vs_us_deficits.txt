Dealing With Deficits

The US just capped another fiscal year with an outsize budget deficit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-16/us-posts-1-99-trillion-budget-gap-for-2026-as-spending-climbs?cmpid=BBD102726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10604>
 — likely weighing in above 6% of gross domestic product, once the GDP figures 
for the July-through-September quarter are released on Friday.

That will have marked the fourth straight year of deficits within a few 
decimal points of 6%, unusually high for a time of low unemployment — pointing 
to structural rather than cyclical issues behind the gap. And indeed, the 
ever-rising costs of Social Security and Medicare for older Americans are a key 
driver.

Another is interest costs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-17/us-interest-costs-hit-record-1-trillion-risking-debt-snowball?cmpid=BBD102726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10604>
. Total debt held by the public, at over $32 trillion, has soared by roughly 
$10 trillion over the past five years, so there’s a whole lot more to pay 
interest on. And with interest rates normalizing after a little over a decade 
of ultra-low levels, that’s taken debt servicing costs over $1 trillion a year.

France has been struggling with much the same dynamics — higher pension costs 
for an aging society, alongside bigger interest bills. The nation has been 
sustaining deficits inexcess of 5% 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-18/france-shouldn-t-throw-out-3-budget-anchor-lescure-says?cmpid=BBD102726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10604>
 of GDP over the past several years, much wider than the European Union’s 3% 
limit.

France in some ways looks better off — its deficit is fractionally smaller. 
And its interest rates are lower. In late Monday trading, its 10-year notes 
were yielding 4.90%, just under the 4.98% for Treasuries. That spread reflects 
that France is in the euro zone, and many of its peers have much stronger 
fiscal balances. Greece, epicenter of the 2010s euro crisis, isin surplus 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-05/greek-fiscal-plan-sees-budget-surplus-exceeding-target-in-2026?cmpid=BBD102726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10604>
.

But in one crucial sense, France’s challenge is far greater.

France’s deficit is large despite a relatively big tax bite. The IMF estimates 
government revenues this year will exceed 50% of GDP. That likely doesn’t leave 
space to boost revenue without incurring economic damage. (Far-left 
presidential candidate Jean-Luc Melenchon nevertheless is willing to give that 
a go, withhigher levies 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/melenchon-s-deficit-plan-relies-on-taxes-rejects-spending-cuts?cmpid=BBD102726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10604>
 on capital and wealthy households.)

France’s tax take is notably higher than for the Group of Seven advanced 
economies as a whole, where the average revenue figure is under 36%. If revenue 
can’t really go up, Paris then needs to decide which benefits to cut — or, as 
far-right presidential candidate Marine Le Pen has floated, to look at 
unorthodox options such as calling on the European Central Bankto intervene 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/le-pen-aims-to-bring-french-deficit-below-3-in-2032-at-latest?cmpid=BBD102726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10604>
 and cut interest rates.

By contrast, the US weighs in at under 30%. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent 
regularly argues that America has a spending problem not a revenue problem. 
Even so, the fact that the tax take is relatively limited gives Washington a 
whole lot more flexibility than Paris has. Granted, the current political 
calibration is hardly in favor of fiscal discipline, and it’s tough right now 
to see a swath of Republican lawmakers embracing higher revenues in the coming 
years.

But the economic option is there, where it may not be for France.

Need-to-Know Research

Back in August, Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh turned a spotlight on to 
the concept of thebreadth of inflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-02/fed-s-warsh-lays-out-data-dashboard-to-read-us-economy?cmpid=BBD102726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10604>
 across different categories of items — highlighting that a notable proportion 
in the US were notching increases above 3%. Now, Fitch Ratings has taken a look 
at things across different nations.

“The share of US CPI components with annual inflation above 3% was 46% in 
September, twice the 2018-2019 average of 23%,” Fitch Ratings found. By 
comparison, the figure for the euro zone is 36%, while Japan’s is 41%, and 
Britain’s is 42%.

“US inflation breadth began increasing before the recent acceleration in 
headline inflation,” said Olu Sonola, head of US Economics at Fitch. “This 
suggests that the rise in inflation is not solely an energy story. Even if 
energy pressures fade, broader price increases could slow inflation’s return to 
target and keep monetary policy higher for longer.”
