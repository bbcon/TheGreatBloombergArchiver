**Energy Economics and Electrification**
Europe's structural vulnerability to fossil fuel price volatility — oil and gas account for more than 60% of industrial, building, and transport energy consumption, versus less than a third of the electricity mix — has renewed policy focus on electrification as both a decarbonization and energy security strategy. The arithmetic is compelling: electric vehicles deliver roughly three times the distance per unit of energy versus internal combustion equivalents, while heat pumps provide approximately four times the heating output of natural gas boilers. These efficiency differentials underpin the EU's projection of €260 billion in annual import savings if electrification targets are met by 2040. The near-term constraint is capital: €584 billion in grid investment is required through the end of this decade alone, and mobilizing private finance at that scale remains unresolved. Industrial electrification above 200°C temperature thresholds presents an additional technological challenge, with costs rising steeply as process heat requirements increase.

**Political Economy**
Protests across France, Germany, and Italy reflect the near-term cost-of-living pressure that the energy transition must navigate politically. Public discontent, partly attributable to elevated fossil fuel import costs, creates urgency around the transition but simultaneously constrains governments' room to impose upfront investment costs on consumers — a tension the Electrification Action Plan does not fully resolve.
