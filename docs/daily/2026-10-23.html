US

US Equity and AI Sentiment

US equity futures stabilized on October 23 after the Nasdaq 100 suffered its worst single session since June, driven by a broad reassessment of AI valuations. The selloff was indiscriminate across the semiconductor and data-center supply chain, with hedge funds recording one of their worst days since the Covid pandemic — Goldman Sachs's prime brokerage desk reported global fundamental long/short managers down 1.4% and multi-strategy platforms off 1.9%. The proximate catalyst appears to be investor unease over the durability of AI capital expenditure, though Blackstone's Jon Gray pushed back, arguing the boom does not yet constitute a bubble. Whether this represents a structural repricing or a temporary rotation remains genuinely uncertain.

Monetary Policy and Rates

A notable shift in the rates market accompanied the equity weakness: Treasuries rallied as bonds reasserted their traditional role as an equity hedge — a function that had been largely absent for much of 2026. Cross-asset strategist Charlie McElligott at Nomura described the move as markets "repricing probabilities of a US growth scare" in real time. The 10-year Treasury yield stood at 4.98%, up slightly on the day, suggesting the bond rally remained modest.

Midterm Election Dynamics

With November 3 less than two weeks away, Wall Street consensus holds that a Democratic sweep of Congress would weigh on AI-exposed equities through the threat of increased regulation — though the analytical nuance is that Trump's veto power would constrain legislative action regardless of the outcome. Historical precedent is less alarming than current sentiment implies: the S&P 500 has risen in the twelve months following every postwar midterm election. ---

China Macro and Monetary Policy

The PBOC's governor Pan Gongsheng indicated the central bank is studying a shift in its primary open market operations tenor from the current seven-day benchmark to an overnight rate — a structural reform that would align China more closely with Fed and ECB practice. The practical implication is potentially lower borrowing costs, since the overnight rate sits below the seven-day rate. Markets appear to have already priced this as easing: 10-year Chinese government bond yields hit their lowest level in over a year this week. A PBOC-affiliated newspaper explicitly cautioned against interpreting the move as a rate cut, though the distinction may be difficult to sustain in practice. The reform arrives at a moment when China's economy faces deflationary pressure, weak consumer sentiment, and a prolonged property downturn.

Japan and Regional Markets

Japan's key price gauge rising above the 2% BOJ target for the first time in 2026 is a consequential development. The BOJ has conditioned further normalization on sustained inflation, and this print — combined with Prime Minister Takaichi's first year in office that has emphasized fiscal expansion and corporate reform — may bring December's meeting into sharper focus for rate watchers. Separately, the broader AI-driven global equity rout hit Asian markets, though the regional gauge erased early losses of 1.1% by session end. Julius Baer's head of Asia research argued the semiconductor correction represented a buying opportunity given supply still materially lags demand. ---

Eurozone: France Under Pressure

The deterioration in French economic and financial conditions is the dominant European story. October's PMI data confirmed that French business activity contracted, and the fiscal backdrop remains deeply challenging: the government is attempting to reduce a wide budget deficit ahead of elections scheduled in six months. French bond spreads have widened materially — sources describe levels comparable to the 2011 sovereign debt crisis — and French equities have declined broadly. Military deployments ahead of planned protests on October 25 underscore the degree to which political risk has become a market variable. Romanian President Dan's warning that Russia appears more inclined to escalate in Ukraine than negotiate adds a further layer of geopolitical uncertainty to the European risk environment.

UK Divergence

The UK PMI print — the fastest growth in eight months — stands in contrast to the continental weakness and suggests the British economy is, for now, tracking on a somewhat more constructive path. Chancellor Healey's first budget is expected to avoid major tax increases, focusing instead on regulatory relief and cost-of-living measures. The approach suggests fiscal caution, though critics argue it defers harder structural decisions on welfare and defense spending. ---

Brazil: Election in the Balance

The first-round result, in which Lula finished behind Flávio Bolsonaro, appears to have caught the incumbent off-guard and prompted a visible shift in campaign tactics, including Lula's adoption of a smartphone — symbolically freighted given the role of social media in Brazilian electoral politics. With polling at exactly 50-50, the outcome of Sunday's runoff is genuinely uncertain. The result carries significant implications for Brazilian fiscal policy, commodity sector regulation, and the country's posture on trade and foreign investment. Markets will likely see elevated volatility around the result.

Argentina and Regional Dynamics

The Buenos Aires water utility deal, valued at $340 million, represents the clearest evidence yet that Milei's privatization agenda is moving beyond rhetoric. Bloomberg Economics notes that Argentina's macroeconomic performance since Milei took office has surprised to the upside relative to most forecasts, though vulnerabilities remain. Chile faces a near-term headwind from El Niño-driven storms disrupting copper output — a development with potential global commodity market implications given Chile's dominant share of world copper supply. Turkey's rate cut, while geographically outside the region, reflects a broader emerging-market theme of central banks beginning cautious easing cycles as inflation recedes from its post-war peaks. ---

Energy Markets and the Iran Conflict

The Strait of Hormuz remains the central node of global energy risk. Brent's rise to $114 reflects both the ongoing US-Iran military conflict — which began in February and has persisted far longer than either Washington or Jerusalem anticipated — and the escalating Houthi campaign against Saudi infrastructure, including the evacuation of Aramco's Dhahran headquarters. Physical prices of some Middle Eastern crudes sold outside the Gulf have softened somewhat as traders grow more adept at routing cargoes through the strait, but the structural premium in global oil prices reflects the absence of any credible near-term de-escalation. Trump has signaled the possibility of additional strikes after the November 3 midterms, and Netanyahu has explicitly framed the goal as regime change in Tehran. Israeli elections on October 27 add a further variable, with polls suggesting no bloc will achieve a parliamentary majority.

AI, Capital Markets, and Global Monetary Divergence

The week's most consequential cross-asset development was the simultaneous sell-off in AI-exposed equities and rally in sovereign bonds across multiple markets — a pattern that suggests investors are beginning to question whether AI capital expenditure will generate returns commensurate with the investment cycle. Global hedge funds recorded losses consistent with a major risk-off episode. Central bank policy is diverging in meaningful ways: the BOJ appears to be moving toward December tightening, the PBOC is easing through structural reform, Turkey has begun cutting, and the Fed remains on hold. This divergence is shaping currency and capital flow dynamics globally, though the net direction of those flows — particularly toward or away from emerging markets — remains difficult to read cleanly against the backdrop of elevated oil prices and geopolitical uncertainty.
