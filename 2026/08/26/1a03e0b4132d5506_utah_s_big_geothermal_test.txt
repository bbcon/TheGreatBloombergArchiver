Geothermal’s big test

By Kyle Stock 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/ARKz8x17rNs/kyle-stock?cmpid=BBD082626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8197>

The future of geothermal energy, a renewable source that has charged up 
conservatives and liberals alike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/trump-administration-s-geothermal-energy-push-gets-test-in-utah?cmpid=BBD082626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8197>
, is being charted in a scrubby patch of sagebrush, halfway between Las Vegas 
and Salt Lake City.

That’s where a federally funded field laboratory just began a vanguard test 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/trump-administration-s-geothermal-energy-push-gets-test-in-utah?cmpid=BBD082626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8197>
 that may show whether fracking techniques used to produce oil can also tap 
underground heat to produce energy.

At the moment, there are scant places around the globe with the right 
properties to generate geothermal electricity; the current test, however, could 
make a case for geothermal plants virtually anywhere.
An exploratory geothermal well drilled by FORGEPhotographer: Eric Larson/Flash 
Point SLC/Utah Forge
Kristie McLin, manager of Utah FORGE, the Energy Department field laboratory, 
said she was excited and nervous as her crew starting pumping water into the 
ground. “It’s butterflies really,” she said. “With geothermal technology, we 
have seen that initial liftoff and we’re advancing significantly what we can do 
economically.”

The new drilling techniques are expensive, the industry is still tiny and the 
sole public player,Fervo Energy Co. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-03-19/fervo-energy-set-to-go-public-as-ai-demand-trump-propel-geothermal?cmpid=BBD082626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8197>
, has had a chilly reception on public markets.

However, if the new strategies work, the upside is immense, proponents say — a 
gusher of electricity that is cheap, constant and nearly emission-free. And 
while the Trump administration has severed federal support of solar and wind 
farms, it is still spending to try to make green energy happen in the Utah 
desert.
FORGE engineersPhotographer: Eric Larson/Flash Point SLC/Utah Forge
Recently, however, engineers and entrepreneurs have started working on a new 
approach: using the same drilling techniques for oil and gas fracking to create 
underground reservoirs where there were none. Water pumped down the well 
expands little cracks in the rock and then returns to the surface via another 
well, a so-called closed-loop system, essentially a subterranean steam plant 
that can spin power turbines up top for extended periods of time, if not 
indefinitely.

FORGE, which stands for Frontier Observatory for Research in Geothermal 
Energy, was launched during the Obama administration, primarily to test the 
thesis. More recently, Fervo and other startups have joined the push.

Geothermal is arguably the only renewable energy technology garnering 
widespread bipartisan support, including funding that has helped lower the cost 
of building new geothermal capacity in the US. Costs are now on par with other 
renewable sources and cheaper than coal plants, offshore wind farms and 
nuclear, according toa recent report 
<https://www.lazard.com/research-insights/levelized-cost-of-energyplus-lcoeplus/>
 from Lazard Inc.

If the FORGE test goes well, McLin said focus will shift to drilling farther 
into the Earth — where temperatures are hotter — and more efficiently. “Being 
able to go deeper for cheaper is how I say it,” McLin explained. “Eventually, 
we could unlock geothermal to be everywhere.”

Aluminum-powered data centers

By Michelle Ma 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWTYlfwjEUk/michelle-ma?cmpid=BBD082626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8197>

A California startup has raised $35 million to develop a system that turns 
scrap aluminum into a backup power source for data centers. The goal: to create 
a cleaner alternative to burning diesel.
Voya Energy CCO Matt Horton, from left, CEO Richard Wang, and CTO Steven Kaye.
Source: Voya Energy
Voya Energy’s financing round was led by Energy Impact Partners LP 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/1368599D:US?cmpid=BBD082626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8197>
 and included billionaire venture capitalist John Doerr, Mantis VC, StepStone,
Founders Fund 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/0303083D:US?cmpid=BBD082626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8197>
, Overmatch Ventures and Seven Stars, the firm said Wednesday in an emailed 
statement. Voya plans to use the money to build generators that use 
aluminum-based fuel for testing at sites by the end of 2027.
