Hi, this is Allen Wan in Shanghai.

It’s been a tough week to be a pitchman in China. Or the soccer team he plays 
for. Or the league that team is in.

They’ve all been reminded what many others have learned about doing business 
in China. It’s all fun and games — and profitable — until certain sensitivities 
are trampled.

It’s almost too easy to say French soccer star Kylian Mbappé scored an own 
goal in the world’s second-biggest economy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-28/mbappe-china-backlash-prompts-china-s-warning-for-global-celebrities?cmpid=BBD103026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10721>
.
Kylian Mbappe during a World Cup match in East Rutherford, New Jersey, this 
year.Photographer: Justin Setterfield/Getty Images
For comments that offended Chinese fans because they were perceived as 
insulting founding father Mao Zedong, several companies dropped him as a brand 
ambassador, state TV blocked his matches and social media firms froze his 
accounts.

The Chinese Football Association took aim, so did the Ministry of Foreign 
Affairs, and so did state media. Shares of companies linked to the star dropped.

The only real question was how long it’d take Mbappé and his advisers, Real 
Madrid and LaLiga to understand the ways forward were either a lifetime in the 
doghouse or an apology.

So the apologies flowed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-27/kylian-mbappe-mao-zedong-eastroc-beverage-china-mengniu-dairy-online-backlash?cmpid=BBD103026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10721>
.

Sanrio, the Japanese company behind Hello Kitty, also got in hot water this 
week. That episode was over a TV show that was criticized in China because of 
how it portrayed wartime history.

It apologized as well 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/hello-kitty-owner-sanrio-axes-japan-show-ties-after-china-furor?cmpid=BBD103026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10721>
.

The incidents and the responses can come off as random but that misses the 
point. In the West, lampooning historical and current leaders is fair game. But 
in China to glibly reference Maoor other leaders 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2018-02-26/as-xi-jinping-cements-hold-on-power-chinese-find-ways-to-vent?cmpid=BBD103026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10721>
 is to play with fire.

The same goes for a handful of events out of history. Think the Cultural 
Revolution, the Great Leap Forward, the Tiananmen protests.
An ad featuring Kylian Mbappé for Eastroc Beverage in Shanghai.Photographer: 
Jade Gao/AFP/Getty Images
And then there’s Taiwan, and other hot-button political issues of the day. 
These are issues that most Chinese people tend be very careful discussing 
publicly. Part of that is down to the fact they live in a country where 
political speech isn’t fully protected.

This is why Mbappé’s exact words can’t even be found in some Chinese news or 
social media accounts about the incident. Even just talking about Mao in too 
much detail makes people uncomfortable.

If all that is out then, what’s in? Well, Norwegian star Erling Haaland has 
legions of adoring Chinese fans and endorsement deals while remaining pretty 
active on social media. But then Haaland has stuck to the same jokes and topics 
that they do.

Mbappé might want to take some notes.

Interesting Times

Politics in China is about to get really interesting — and it has already been 
pretty riveting given the depth of President Xi Jinping’s anti-graft drive.

They’re getting that way because the countdown is now on to a congress of the 
ruling Communist Party in about a year that will likely hand Xi a fourth term 
in office.

The run-up to that event will also involve a lot of behind-the-scenes 
jockeying for positions, a scramble made even more turbulent by the fight 
against corruption that Xi seems to use to both clean up government and push 
aside officials who aren’t completely with his program.
President Xi Jinping, who became China’s party leader in 2012.Photographer: 
Yan Yan/Xinhua/Getty Images via Xinhua News Agency
The sweep of his campaign was on full display this week when the party held 
its biggest political event of the year 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-25/what-to-expect-from-china-s-fifth-plenum-xi-s-purges-and-tighter-party-control?cmpid=BBD103026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10721>
, a closed-door affair that brings together hundreds of top officials.

The event, known as a plenum, had record low attendance. Other data show that 
the average tenure of a provincial party chief has shrunk to 26.5 months in 
Xi’s current five-year term, down from 43 months in Xi’s first stretch.

The question now is who Xi deems trustworthy enough to remain 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-27/xi-s-corruption-purges-upend-hunt-for-china-s-next-top-leaders?cmpid=BBD103026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10721>
 in any position long enough to gain the experience that previously guided 
promotions.

He’ll have to decide because next year he’ll undertake one of the biggest 
personnel reshuffles in modern Chinese history. The elite seven-member Standing 
Committee will likely see five members retire if norms hold, and top diplomat 
Wang Yi, 73, is expected to step down.

The decision-making Politburo also needs replenishing after three members were 
ousted this term — the widest purge since the Cultural Revolution.

Xi will also need to restock China’s top military body, the Central Military 
Commission, which began this term with seven men but now has only one, 
excluding the president himself.

The plenum happening this week seems to have focused on strengthening ideology 
and governance within the party, including that anti-corruption campaign. This 
matters because tightening discipline will be key to the transition next year.

Xi took some steps toward filling key roles this week, naming a new member to 
the powerful Politburo 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/china-names-vice-military-chair-as-new-politburo-member?cmpid=BBD103026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10721>
.

Yet given Xi’s laser-like focus on cracking down on corrupt officials and 
maintaining strict discipline within the party of some 100 million members, we 
can expect to see more officials taken down.

That’ll create all sorts of drama on China’s political stage over the next 
year.

Behind the Great Firewall

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

The public is getting its first glimpse of Shanghai’s new Grand Opera House 
and so far the reviews are glowing.

Architectural Digest called the project 
<https://www.architecturaldigest.com/story/at-the-shanghai-grand-opera-house-the-gravity-defying-staircase-was-designed-to-look-like-a-hand-fan>
 “one of the most significant in contemporary architecture” and a visit showed 
why.

One of the most striking features is an outdoor, double-helix staircase that 
looks like a fan. It’s cool on its own but the rooftop terrace on top affords a 
fantastic view of Shanghai that should make for some memorable show nights.

The winding staircase brought on a touch of nerves even in our intrepid 
reporter, but that just adds to the fun.
Visitors outside the new Shanghai Grand Opera House on Wednesday.Photographer: 
Allen Wan/Bloomberg
“It is more than just an opera house,” wrote one Weibo user with 17 million 
followers. “It is a masterpiece of architectural art — stunning and 
spectacular.”

The venue is on the Huangpu River and sits on the grounds of what was the 
city’s 2010 World Expo. It was designed by the Norwegian company Snøhetta, 
which was involved in projects like Bibliotheca Alexandrina in Egypt, the Oslo 
Opera House and the 9/11 Memorial and Museum in New York.

The building has three main performance stages and dozens of rehearsal rooms. 
Some of the halls are awash in deep red so even though the opera house has the 
traditions of a European venue, it’s got a very Chinese vibe.

The venue, which officially opens this month, is expected to host classical 
music performances, concerts and experimental shows. One of its best features 
is that it was conceived as a public space, so locals and tourists can walk 
around without a ticket.
The 1,200-seat theater in the Shanghai Grand Opera House.Photographer: Allen 
Wan/Bloomberg
Shanghai has no lack of iconic spots. There’s the Bund waterfront with its 
early 20th century buildings. Across the way are the shining, photogenic 
skyscrapers of the Lujiazui financial district.

The Shanghai Opera House seems destined to take its place alongside those 
places in China’s most cosmopolitan city. It sure is a welcome sight as the 
government tries to revive consumption and domestic tourism, areas that have 
been holding back the economy.

Or in the words of a Weibo blogger who identified himself as an architect: 
“Shanghai is poised to welcome another remarkable new urban landmark.”
