Why one farmer in the Amazon chose carbon credits over soybeans

By Fabiano Maisonnave 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXmFWA_HFBY/fabiano-maisonnave?cmpid=BBD081926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260819&utm_content=7873>

Three years ago, Ademar Bedin bought an area of old-growth Amazon rainforest 
the size of San Francisco, aiming to turn dense groves into farmland as 
generations of Brazilians had done before him.
Ademar BedinPhotographer: Gustavo Minas/Bloomberg
In Roraima state, he planned to clear almost half of the property — up to the 
legal limit — and plant soybeans. Instead, a Sao Paulo-based carbon project 
developer, ZEG, persuaded him toleave the forest standing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-08-19/carbon-credits-protect-amazon-rainforest-and-benefit-farmers?cmpid=BBD081926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260819&utm_content=7873>
.

Now Bedin’s property has become a test of whether carbon markets can do 
something they have long promised but rarely demonstrated: make the 
preservation of tropical rainforest financially attractive to a farmer.

“With the model we’re doing, it’s a new type of agriculture,” Bedin said in a 
Zoom interview from his farm, near Brasília. “It’s conservation agriculture.”
Part of the Vale do Rio Branco REDD+ projectSource: ZEG
Initially, Bedin was skeptical. Carbon credits, he thought, were something 
everyone had heard about but few had actually seen work. The voluntary carbon 
market is still grappling with an erosion of trust after investigations found 
that developersoverstated climate benefits 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2023-03-24/carbon-offset-seller-s-forest-protection-projects-questioned?cmpid=BBD081926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260819&utm_content=7873>
 and that project certifications lacked rigor. In Brazil, carbon projects have 
also been linked to land-grabbing and other illegal practices.

Eventually, though, ZEG gained his trust. And the numbers became interesting. 

Bedin and ZEG compared the potential returns from agriculture with keeping the 
forest intact for carbon credits. Soy was more profitable. Carlos Jacob, ZEG’s 
CEO, estimates the project’s returns are about 20% lower than for producing 
soy, one of the main crops grown in the Amazon and a top export of Brazil.

But developing a new farm would require substantial investment and management, 
and would expose Bedin to swings in commodity prices, weather and costs. Carbon 
was potentially steadier.

“We really couldn’t match the economics of his soybean spreadsheet, but we 
were able to offer a competitive alternative,” Jacob said. The combination of 
less day-to-day work, greater financial stability and diversification was an 
option that Bedin was willing to try.

ZEG actively sought rainforest that could legally and profitably be converted 
to agriculture, then formed a profit-sharing partnership with Bedin in which he 
receives 60% of the returns while committing the land to conservation. The 
project has a 40-year commitment period and covers almost 6,000 hectares 
(roughly 15,000 acres) that could be legally cleared. That makes it an example 
of avoided planned deforestation, or APD.

ZEG and Bedin are betting that higher-quality credits can make it commercially 
viable to leave Amazon forest standing. The challenge now is whether buyers 
will pay enough to make that model work.

Hot oceans carry storm risks

By Laura Millan 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD081926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260819&utm_content=7873>

Rising temperatures in the ocean now have the potential to cause intense 
storms in Europe later this year, according to new research.

The Mediterranean Sea is heating up twice as fast as the average for oceans in 
the rest of the world, according to World Weather Attribution, a group of 
scientists that uses peer-reviewed methods to perform rapid studies of how 
climate change feeds into weather patterns. Its analysis shows that parts of 
the Mediterranean Sea and the European Atlantic coast recorded surface 
temperatures that were more than 6C hotter than would be considered normal in 
June.
Waves crash over the breakwater in Newhaven, England, in 2024.Photographer: 
Dan Kitwood/Getty Images
“When we have very warm sea surface temperatures, it provides added energy to 
storms so it can keep going for a lot longer,” said Catherine Gregory, a 
researcher at the University of Bern in Switzerland and a co-author of the 
report.

“It’s a bit of a newer area of research, but it’s probably one of the most 
important impacts when we talk about how marine heat waves affect humans,” she 
said.

Man-made global warming was the main driver of record-high sea temperatures 
registered around the Iberian Peninsula, as well as the Western and Eastern 
Mediterranean in July, researchers found. Europe, the world’s fastest-warming 
continent, is struggling to adapt to relentless heat waves that have already 
resulted in anestimated 25,000 deaths 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/heat-deaths-top-25-000-in-europe-as-scorching-weather-persists?cmpid=BBD081926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260819&utm_content=7873>
 this summer. That’s as rising temperatures hit theeconomy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-14/how-extreme-heat-is-impacting-europe-s-energy-industry-and-food-production?cmpid=BBD081926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260819&utm_content=7873>
 with commodity supplies, critical infrastructure and energy security all in 
the crosshairs.

How traffic curbs help London kids

By Olivia Rudgard 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWBnLoYN_tg/olivia-rudgard?cmpid=BBD081926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260819&utm_content=7873>

Since London introduced traffic restrictions in an effort to cut air 
pollution, there’s been a measurable improvement in children’s lung health in 
the city, according to a fresh study published in the Lancet.
A sign warns motorists of the ultra-low emission zone in London.Photographer: 
Chris J. Ratcliffe/Bloomberg
Scientists compared the lung capacity of children in Luton, a town about 37 
miles (60 kilometers) north of London, with that of children living in London. 
They found that children in the UK capital had smaller lungs before London 
introduced its ultra-low emission zone (Ulez), but that over five years this 
changed so that the two groups had similar levels of lung size and health.

The study also found that the proportion of children who had clinically 
impaired lung function fell twice as much in London as in Luton over the period.

Ulez, which was introduced in central London in April 2019 and expanded to 
outer London in August 2023, charges drivers to bring older vehicles with more 
polluting engines into the city. It’s designed to limit the amount of nitrogen 
dioxide in the air, a pollutant linked to breathing and circulation conditions 
like asthma and respiratory infections.
