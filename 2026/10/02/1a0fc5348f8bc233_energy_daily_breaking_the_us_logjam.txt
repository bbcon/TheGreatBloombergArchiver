Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Washington energy reporter Ari Natter explores the challenges ahead in the push 
to reform the US infrastructure permitting system.

US lawmakers are the closest they’ve been in years to fixing the nation’s 
notoriously slow process for approving key energy infrastructure.

After months of painstaking negotiations, a bipartisan group of senators 
reached a deal on Wednesday over a 417-page draft bill that would make it 
easier to issue permits for projects ranging from high-voltage transmission 
lines to oil wells.

It was a notable moment of comity over energy policy in Washington, where 
common ground between Republicans and Democrats is scarce. Now comes the hard 
part.

The legislation would shorten 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-25/us-permit-talks-include-faster-approvals-for-power-lines-pipes?cmpid=BBBXT100226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9643>
 environmental reviews that can take years. It has won applause from groups 
ranging from the National Association of Manufacturers to the American Clean 
Power Association.

But some members of the Democrats’ progressive wing and environmental groups 
say it gives away too much to the fossil fuel industry.
An electric power substation in Bethesda, Maryland.Photographer: Graeme 
Sloan/Bloomberg
Representative Jared Huffman <https://huffman.house.gov/>, who will have 
significant sway over the legislation as the top Democrat on the House 
Committee on Natural Resources, outlined a litany of concerns. He said the deal 
with Republicans “makes no sense” if Democrats flip one or both chambers of 
Congress in November, as is widely expected.

The Natural Resources Defense Council reacted warily, saying it was “obvious 
that there are stark tradeoffs being made” and the bill must be improved. 
Friends of the Earth was downright scathing, calling the agreement 
“shortsighted and politically bewildering.”

“This bill will make it easier to build data centers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/senators-reach-deal-on-bill-to-speed-permits-for-energy-projects?cmpid=BBBXT100226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9643>
 and oil and gas projects,” said Friends of the Earth President Erich Pica.

On top of all that, the agreement still has a key unresolved issue.

One of the Democrats’ top demands was for assurances that the administration 
of President Donald Trump will stop blocking fully permitted wind and solar 
projects from moving ahead. They’re still waiting for the final details on that 
provision from the White House.

The Senate doesn’t plan to vote on the legislation until after the midterm  
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-24/senate-permitting-vote-unlikely-before-election-whitehouse-says?cmpid=BBBXT100226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9643>
elections, allowing plenty of time for the deal to unravel.

So while the US is making progress, it hasn’t broken through its permitting 
logjam quite yet.

—Ari Natter, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

The International Energy Agency, US Energy Information Administration and 
BloombergNEF all widened their 2026 oil deficit estimates again in September, 
as Hormuz stayed restricted and attacks on Saudi Arabia’s Red Sea exports cut 
Gulf supply. The IEA made the largest revision, to a deficit of 1.7 million 
barrels a day from 1.3 million barrels a day previously.
