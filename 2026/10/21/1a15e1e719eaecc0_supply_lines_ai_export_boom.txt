Asia’s Chip Exports Soar as AI Bubble Debate Rages

The financial-market debate over a possible bubble in artificial intelligence 
continues to rage, with billionaire Ken Griffininsisting this week 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-20/ken-griffin-says-ai-almost-certainly-in-a-bubble-right-now?cmpid=BBD102126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10115>
 that such a predicament is “almost certainly” there, while other executives 
such as BlackRock’s Larry Fink push back.

What’s for sure is that at the level of physical supply chains, the boom is 
carrying on unabated, as Asian economic data on Thursday highlighted.

The most up-to-date numbers come from South Korea 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/s-korea-s-export-boom-stays-strong-ahead-of-bok-rate-decision?cmpid=BBD102126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10115>
, whose trade statistics are often a leading indicator for global commerce. The 
report for the first 20 days of October, adjusted for working-day differences, 
showed a 65.9% increase in exports from a year earlier, as buoyant demand for 
AI fueled semiconductor shipments.

Drilling down to the individual categories, overseas chip sales surged 222.5% 
on an annual basis, reinforcing a picture of an economy turbo-charged by the 
technology frenzy.

Japan has a similar story 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/japan-s-export-growth-stays-solid-supported-by-chips-cars?cmpid=BBD102126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10115>
. Exports rose 16.7% in September from a year earlier, and have climbed for 13 
consecutive months. Shipments of semiconductors and chip-making equipment 
jumped 49.4% and 43.9%, respectively.

How long can momentum keep up like this? Double or triple-digit increases in 
any indicator can be enough to make any economist wince, and these sort of 
numbers are likely to feed both sides of the argument among investors.

On the one hand, there’s the anxiety that took hold in stock markets recently 
about the durability of the AI boom, amid fears that capital spending needed 
will take longer than expected to translate into profits and productivity gains.

Last week, International Monetary Fund chief Kristalina Georgieva even spoke 
of the phenomenon around the technology as part of a “slow-moving tsunami 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/imf-chief-alarmed-at-slow-moving-tsunami-of-ai-for-jobs-taxes?cmpid=BBD102126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10115>
” that could disrupt the world economy.

Then again, Fink warned on Wednesday 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/deutsche-bank-chief-sewing-plays-down-griffin-s-ai-bubble-fears?cmpid=BBD102126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10115>
 that whether or not it’s stoppable, the build-out of AI shouldn’t be impeded — 
a sentiment likely to buoy those chip-focused exporters across Asia.

“If we don’t build out these data centers, it is actually more inflationary 
because the cost of compute is so high right now,” he said in an interview at 
the Berlin Global Dialogue. “And the faster we can build out more data centers, 
the more compute we have, the faster we can bring down the cost.”
