Insurers under pressure

By Olivia Rudgard 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWBnLoYN_tg/olivia-rudgard?cmpid=BBD112626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11911>

When a wealthy client’s home was flooded five years ago, one UK insurance 
company had to spend tens of thousands of pounds putting them up at the luxury 
hotel Claridge’s for several weeks. Another insurer racked up a giant bill 
paying rent for a flood-hit client who needed a London family home close to 
specific schools, and big enough to accommodate both a Bentley and a puppy. 
Brokers requested anonymity to discuss these cases without clients’ permission.

Flood claims made on “private client” policies, which are aimed at high net 
worth individuals, can stretch into hundreds of thousands of pounds, driven up 
by the cost of replacing and repairing high-end furniture, vehicles and art, 
and by clauses requiring the insurer to pay for temporary accommodation suited 
to the client’s needs.



In the UK, such payouts are increasingly backstopped by the state through 
Flood Re, a program designed to allow homeowners in flood-prone areas to access 
affordable insurance. It’s funded through a levy paid by insurers, a cost that 
is usually passed on to their customers. That is about to change, after the 
government announced a cap on payouts from Flood Re, starting in April 2028. If 
a claim exceeds the cap, insurers will now have to be ready to pay the extra 
amount themselves, or find cover on the commercial reinsurance market.

The change could make it harder and costlier for homeowners to get proper 
cover in ways that ultimately affect mortgage underwriting, brokers said. But 
it could also mean flood resilience measures like pumps and flood barriers 
start to make a real difference to premium costs, as insurers begin looking 
more closely at an individual home’s preparedness for flood risk. With less 
than 18 months to go until the cap is due to come into force, pressure is 
growing on wealthy homeowners to show they are flood-ready.

“If you have a claim that’s beyond that cap, then there’s going to have to be 
some sort of understanding of how much risk is being carried,” said Tracy 
Jenkins, an account executive specializing in high net worth and ultra-high net 
worth clients at Lockton, the insurance broker. “I think there’s a lot of 
education required from brokers to high net worth clients around how their 
homes can become more resilient.”

Political winds

By Thomas Gualtieri 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/ATE2PsR0P_A/thomas-gualtieri?cmpid=BBD112626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11911>
 andLaura Millan 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD112626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11911>

On Sunday, Spain’s general election 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-24/spain-election-2026-angry-generation-feels-left-behind-as-economy-booms?cmpid=BBD112626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11911>
 will reflect divided views over the country’s decades-long renewable energy 
bet.

Wind, solar and hydro power make up about 60% of electricity on the Spanish 
grid, far above the European Union average. The clean-power boom has allowed 
the Mediterranean country to cut reliance on imported fossil fuels and reduce 
power bills.

For the past eight years, the Socialist Party has led a government that has 
championed the clean-energy transition and put climate change at the forefront 
of government policy.

But as of Nov. 23 — the last day that polls could be published before the 
election, under Spanish law —voters 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-23/spain-poll-shows-feijoo-on-track-to-oust-sanchez-as-premier?cmpid=BBD112626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11911>
 looked likely to oust the current coalition in favor of the conservative 
People’s Party.

The PP broadly recognizes the impacts of man-made climate change and supports 
clean-energy policies, while calling for a more market-driven pace of change 
that retains nuclear power and supports the data-center buildout. Still, to 
form a majority government the PP would likely need the far-right party Vox, 
which denies that global warming is caused by human activity and describes the 
policies of the left as “climate fanaticism.”
Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez and Alberto Nunez Feijoo, leader of the 
Spanish opposition and president of the People’s Party, ahead of a televised 
debate on Nov. 19.Photographer: Oscar Del Pozo/AFP/Getty Images
Experts say Spain’s energy transition is on the way no matter what and that 
many existing policies will remain in place. But a nationwide blackout last 
year cast doubts on the grid’s ability to handle unexpected swings in power 
frequency — which are inherent to solar and wind power — and exacerbated the 
political debate around what Spain should do next.

Still, what may change is the pace of the transition. A change of government 
would also mean the EU loses one of its key climate champions, which could have 
knock-on effects beyond Spain.

Here is what’s at stake for Spain’s energy policy in this weekend’s vote:

Renewable Energy

Spain’s solar fleet keeps growing, with 9 gigawatts of photovoltaic capacity 
set to be added by the end of the year and a further 7 gigawatts by the end of 
2028, according to Wood Mackenzie Ltd. Over 70% of left-wing voters view the 
deployment of renewables favorably — about 10 percentage points more than among 
their right-wing counterparts, according to think tankCREAF 
<https://www.creaf.cat/en>.

The renewables expansion is currently taking place without financial support 
from the government and that’s unlikely to change, said María Pérez-Tabernero, 
Iberia product manager at Aurora Energy Research Ltd.

“Renewables, up to now, have been driven by very clear market signals,” 
Pérez-Tabernero said. “That’s set to continue, no matter the color of the 
government.”
The Santa Ana Hydroelectric Power Station in Huesca, Spain, on Monday, July 
13, 2026.Photographer: Angel Garcia/Bloomberg
Still, the Socialists are reaffirming the government’s goal to reach 81% of 
energy generation from renewable sources in 2030. Far-left political alliance 
Frente Amplio pushes that further in its manifesto, calling for 100% renewables 
by 2040.

Opposition conservative groups have criticized renewables’ instability and the 
lack of grid investments. The PP says it will boost grid connections to 
projects such as data centers and will not favor any particular form of power 
generation, while Vox has called for preventing the “uncontrolled” development 
of large-scale solar and wind farms on agricultural land and natural heritage 
sites.

Nuclear Power

The Socialist government has a long-term plan to shut all seven of Spain’s 
nuclear reactors by 2035. However, the overall phase-out remains uncertain. A 
prolonged tug-of-war with Spain’s main utilities led to a lifespan extension 
for the Almaraz plant in the country’s southwest, whichis set to close 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-05-29/spain-s-iberdrola-endesa-aim-to-extend-nuclear-plant-lifespan?cmpid=BBD112626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11911>
 in 2030.

The conservative PP advocates scrapping the existing closure plan so that all 
energy-generation technologies can “compete equally,” according to its 
manifesto. Far-right group Vox calls for building more nuclear plants.

The closure plan also faces fierce opposition from Catalan parties, as nuclear 
accounts for about 60% of the heavily industrial region’s total generation, 
said Javier Revuelta, power and renewables consulting vice president at Wood 
Mackenzie in Madrid.

“No country in the world has decommissioned so many reactors at the same time, 
and Spain isn’t going to be the first,” said Revuelta. “Grid operator Red 
Eléctrica isn’t going to authorize that much capacity to be shut down.”

The owners of the plants, Spain’s three largest utilities, argue that nuclear 
power is taxed too heavily. They may revive that battle if the plants remain 
open.

A phaseout of Spain’s nuclear fleet, which supplies about one-fifth of the 
country’s electricity, could at least initially increase reliance on gas-fired 
generation, complicating the government’s ambitions to cut CO2 emissions, 
according to Bloomberg Intelligence analyst Patricio Álvarez.

Batteries and Storage

Spain is lagging behind 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-06/spain-s-renewable-energy-lead-is-at-risk-without-a-battery-revolution?cmpid=BBD112626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11911>
 on battery deployment, with only 400 megawatts of installed capacity last 
year, far from the 22.5 gigawatts it’s targeting by 2030. Battery storage is 
essential to offset the uneven electricity production of renewables, but it 
needs a foreseeable revenue stream to ensure its viability.

In its manifesto, the PP vows to use batteries to reinforce the electrical 
system and prevent blackouts such as the one that left the Iberian Peninsula in 
the dark last year. It also says it will give favorable fiscal treatment to 
strategic innovation projects, including batteries, for seven years. Vox 
doesn’t mention batteries in its manifesto, while the Socialists talk about 
“accelerating the deployment of large storage.”
High voltage electrical power lines and transmission towers in Lleida, Spain, 
on Monday, July 13, 2026.Photographer: Angel Garcia/Bloomberg
Regardless of who wins on Sunday, the key to the future of large storage in 
Spain lies in introducing a capacity market, a mechanism that will pay 
generators to ensure they are available to supply power when needed. Several 
European countries have implemented them in recent years. Spain approved its 
own only in September.

“Beyond its market revenue, storage will be supported through the capacity 
mechanism, whose rules leave little room for political discretion,” said 
Revuelta.

Data Centers

Spain has emerged 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-04-16/spain-s-90-billion-ai-data-center-plan-draws-amazon-web-services-microsoft?cmpid=BBD112626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11911>
 as one of Europe’s fastest growing data-center hubs. However, newly approved 
government regulations introducing stricter renewable-energy requirements and 
limiting water use have sparked industry backlash, with operators warning the 
rules put billions of euros of planned projects at risk.

The Socialists say the rules will help minimize the environmental impact of 
data centers followingconcerns 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-04-16/spain-s-90-billion-ai-data-center-plan-draws-amazon-web-services-microsoft?cmpid=BBD112626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11911>
 about water usage and employment in rural areas.

The PP says it would boost power connections for data centers, a bottleneck 
that has effectively halted some projects.

“Given the billions of euros at stake, what this could mean for the economy, 
and the fact that this is such a cutting-edge sector that creates high-quality 
jobs, I think that, ultimately, whichever government comes to power will try to 
ensure that these data centers do get built,” Pérez-Tabernero said.

That would also be key to fostering the country’s power demand, whose growth 
is needed to drive investment and offset the solar oversupply the country is 
facing, she added.

As in any election, questions also remain over how the parties’ campaign 
promises will translate into policy once a government is formed.

The Socialist Party “tells you they’re going to get to 81% renewables and Vox 
tells you they’re going to build four new nuclear reactors, though nobody knows 
where,” said Wood Mackenzie’s Revuelta. “Whoever governs, neither of those two 
things is going to happen.”
