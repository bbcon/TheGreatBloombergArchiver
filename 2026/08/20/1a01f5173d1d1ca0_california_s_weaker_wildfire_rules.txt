Fire rules backlash

By Todd Woody 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVl1IPrK6vs/todd-woody?cmpid=BBD082026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7975>

A California agency on Wednesday approved long-delayed regulations 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-08-12/california-failed-to-roll-out-home-fire-protections-ahead-of-la-wildfires?cmpid=BBD082026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7975>
 designed to protect homes from wildfire, but they’re less stringent than 
defenses recommended by scientists and firefighters.

The new rules 
<https://34c031f8-c9fd-4018-8c5a-4159cdff6b0d-cdn-endpoint.azureedge.net/-/media/bof-website/board-meeting-information/monthly-board-binder-materials/archive/2026-monthly-board-binder-materials-workshop-archives/august-2026/full/full-8a-zone-0-regulation-summary--rule-plead-august-2026.pdf?rev=61084bcd20c94b9e9ece954da2c656f1&hash=6995E1441E12F3A819EE0167DD33306C>
, which apply to about 2 million homes, were mandated by a 2020 state law 
requiring residents in high-risk wildfire areas to establish an ember-resistant 
perimeter by removing vegetation and other combustible material within five 
feet of a dwelling. Scientists say that space, known as Zone Zero, nearly 
doubles a home’schance of survival 
<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-63386-2>.
Firefighters in the aftermath of the Palisades Fire in the Mandeville Canyon 
area of Los Angeles in 2025.Photographer: Kyle Grillot/Bloomberg
But after years of backlash from some homeowners over Zone Zero’s aesthetic 
and financial impact, the California Board of Forestry and Fire Protection on 
Wednesday reduced the non-combustible zone from five feet to a minimum of one 
foot. In the remaining portion of Zone Zero, some plants and grass would still 
be allowed and trees can remain if the limbs are trimmed away from the ground 
and roof. Wood gates and fences within five feet of a home must be replaced 
with non-flammable versions.

Todd Lando, a battalion chief with the Central Marin Fire Department in the 
Bay Area, told the board that the weaker regulations would be harder to enforce 
and could raise wildfire risks by allowing plants in Zone Zero. “I advocated 
for a simple, fully non-combustible Zone Zero because the science supports it,” 
he said.

Increasingly damaging wildfires over the past decade have destroyed nearly 
60,000 homes and have spawned acontinuing insurance crisis 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-22/california-s-fair-plan-faces-rising-wildfire-risk-from-luxury-homes?cmpid=BBD082026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7975>
 as insurers cancel policies in high-risk neighborhoods, forcing hundreds of 
thousands of homeowners to seek expensive, bare-bones coverage from the FAIR 
Plan, California’s insurer of last resort.

“That has affected our financial situation related to our ability to buy and 
sell homes, to insure homes and has put us in a very precarious place,” said 
Yana Valachovic, a University of California wildfire scientist closely involved 
with the regulations’ development.

Los Angeles residents who spoke at the board’s meeting opposed the less 
restrictive regulations as too draconian and pushed for further revisions.

“There is no perfect solution to the problems of creating safer communities,” 
responded Terrence O’Brien, chair of the forestry board. “Waiting and 
postponing is not, in my opinion, an option.”

London’s $50 billion climate bill

By Olivia Rudgard 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWBnLoYN_tg/olivia-rudgard?cmpid=BBD082026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7975>

Climate change could cost London as much as £36 billion ($50 billion) a year 
by the 2050s, the Mayor of London’s office said in a report.

Lost working hours, disruption to businesses and damage to infrastructure 
caused by extreme weather in a warmer climate are likely to reduce the 
capital’s GDP over the coming decades, according to the report, published by 
the mayor and the city’s councils.
Commuters exit an underground train during a heat wave in London on Aug. 13.
Photographer: Jose Sarmento Matos/Bloomberg
Businesses need to adapt to climate change, the report said, by adjusting 
working hours, allowing employees to work from home and relaxing dress codes 
and uniform rules. The city’s authorities will prioritize protecting and 
improving parks, advertising public cool spaces during hot weather and 
expanding access to public toilets and water, as well as adding shading to 
public spaces. The report also called on Transport for London, which operates 
the city’s underground train network, to add air conditioning to new tube 
trains and look for new ways to keep carriages and platforms cool.
