Environmental trade-offs

By Leslie Kaufman 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUZ8izNmriE/leslie-kaufman?cmpid=BBD100826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9871>
 andZahra Hirji 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVypnhGtqak/zahra-hirji?cmpid=BBD100826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9871>

When Senate negotiators unveiled a sweeping permitting reform deal last week, 
clean-energy advocates welcomed provisions intended to speed construction of 
renewable energy and long-distance power lines.

Now, however, many environmental groups are pushing back over what could be 
sacrificed to get those projects built — and questioning whether the bipartisan 
deal would deliver enough clean energy in return.

Read More: What the Senate Permitting Deal Means for US Climate Emissions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-02/what-the-senate-permitting-deal-means-for-us-climate-emissions?cmpid=BBD100826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9871>

The debate is still in early stages. A Senate vote isn’t expected until after 
the midterm elections, when the political landscape could look very different, 
and the417-page bill 
<https://www.energy.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/BipartisanAmericanAffordabilityandJobsAct.pdf>
 could change substantially along the way. But the initial reactions are 
beginning to reveal some of the biggest fault lines.

Environmental groups have raised concerns that fall broadly into three areas: 
changes to the Endangered Species Act, rollbacks to Clean Water Act protections 
and, more fundamentally, whether longstanding safeguards for nature would be 
lost without securing enough renewable energy in return.
Tidal wetlands near Newark, California.Photographer: Sam Hall/Bloomberg
The Sierra Club cautioned that the deal could also end up boosting fossil-fuel 
projects more than renewables. “There is no task more urgent than building 
clean energy and transmission,” the nonprofit’s Chief Program Officer Holly 
Bender said in a statement. But the legislation “cannot guarantee that more 
wind and solar projects will actually get built” while the Trump administration 
continues to block projects, she warned, and other provisions would provide 
immediate benefits to oil and gas companies.

On Tuesday, more than 200 organizations, including the Center for Biological 
Diversity and Friends of the Earth,sent a letter 
<https://biologicaldiversity.org/programs/government-affairs/pdfs/Oppose-ESA-attacks-in-permitting-deal.pdf>
 to Senate Democratic leaders opposing provisions they say would weaken the 
Endangered Species Act, one of the country’s foundational conservation laws. 
Among other changes, the draft bill would alter the “best available science” 
standard agencies use when deciding whether to list species as endangered and 
whether federal actions could affect them once listed. It would also allow 
states to take over some consultations currently handled by federal wildlife 
agencies.

Looser rules

By Zahra Hirji 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVypnhGtqak/zahra-hirji?cmpid=BBD100826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9871>

The US Environmental Protection Agency 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/0219210Z:US?cmpid=BBD100826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9871>
 said Wednesday it is readying a proposal to loosen federal rules designed to 
cut methane releases from oil and gas operations.

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin previewed the upcoming rule proposal in a speech 
at the New Mexico Oil and Gas Association annual meeting in Santa Fe. The Trump 
EPA “is days” away from announcing the new rules, Zeldin said in a statement 
released by the agency.

“This proposal takes on many of the problems American producers and operators 
have raised with us,” he added.
Exxon Mobil Corp. oil drilling rigs near Stanton, Texas, US.Photographer: 
Justin Hamel/Bloomberg
The agency said it will include changing Biden-era requirements for the 
lowest-producing wells, known as marginal wells, and rescinding the Methane 
Super Emitter Program designed to help spot the biggest methane releases.

Methane is a far more potent greenhouse gas than carbon dioxide in the short 
term, with a roughly 80 times higher warming potential in the initial 20 years 
after release into the air.

The oil and gas sector represents the nation’s largest industrial source of 
methane pollution. Methane is a key component of natural gas, and thus a 
valuable source of energy. Still, operators vent or flare the gas during 
operations, and it can leak from wells, pipelines and processing equipment.

Finance Gap

By Petra Sorge 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AV7cfwcPiCo/petra-sorge?cmpid=BBD100826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9871>

Germany has fallen short of a funding pledge for global climate action and 
adaptation, making it harder to plug a financing gap in the Global South left 
by the US withdrawal.

Budget spending by Europe’s biggest economy on the measures fell by more than 
one-fifth to €4.75 billion ($5.31 billion) in 2025, from €6.1 billion the prior 
year, according togovernment data 
<https://www.bmz.de/de/themen/klimawandel-und-entwicklung/klimafinanzierung>. 
That’s below Germany’s target of paying at least €6 billion annually to 
developing and emerging countries struggling with the extreme floods, droughts 
and storms that are becoming more frequent with climate change.
People wait amidst rain to cross the Trishuli river via a zip line, in the 
aftermath of August 26 flash flood, at Devighat in Nepal’s Nuwakot district.AFP 
via Getty Images
The decline leaves the countries most vulnerable to the effects of global 
warming, already short on options, having to turn to more expensive means to 
fund critical infrastructure projects and respond to disasters. They had looked 
to Germany to step up after US President Donald Trump’s sudden retreat, leaving 
the European nation as the world’s biggest provider of foreign aid last year,
according to data 
<https://www.oecd.org/en/data/insights/data-explainers/2026/04/a-historic-decline-in-foreign-aid-preliminary-2025-oda-data.html>
 from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development.

“These are grim prospects for climate finance,” said Jan Kowalzig, senior 
policy adviser for climate change at nonprofit Oxfam. Germany is heading to the 
United Nations’ COP31 climate summit next month in Antalya, Turkey, “having 
broken its word,” according to Kowalzig.

Germany’s Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development maintains that it 
has still contributed its fair share to a global $100 billion funding goal 
agreed by signatories of the Paris Agreement in 2015. That’s because the 
government also counts private donations and loans toward its international 
climate financing, which altogether reached €10.8 billion in 2025. Nearly half 
that sum included revolving credit lines to local or development banks, equity 
investments in funds and public-private partnerships, according to the ministry.

“The federal government remains committed to its goals for international 
climate action,” Reem Alabali Radovan, Germany’s development minister, said in 
a statement. “At a time of shrinking public budgets, we are increasingly — and 
successfully — leveraging capital markets and private investment.”
