Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Jack Ryan looks at why gold’s brutal selloff is challenging its 
reputation as a safe haven.

Gold’s slump is challenging a popular misconception that the precious metal is 
an all-weather safe haven.

As a non-yielding asset, gold is unusually reliant on narrative rather than 
income for its value. One popular theory among investors is that it can provide 
a hedge against all manner of threats to their portfolios, including 
geopolitical turmoil, inflation and an equity-market drawdown.

The outbreak of war in the Middle East helped bring all those risks to bear, 
but holding bullion would probably have amplified an investor’s losses this 
year. Gold is heading for itsbiggest weekly drop 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-19/gold-set-for-worst-week-since-march-on-hawkish-fed-ai-selloff?cmpid=BBBXT112026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11663>
 in months as a hawkish Federal Reserve and an AI-stock rout add to selling 
pressure. It’s down 36% from the January high and on course for its steepest 
annual decline since 2013.

Gold does have enduring relationships with other financial assets. 
Unsurprisingly for a metal priced in the US currency, when the greenback makes 
a decisive break higher or lower against a basket of other currencies, gold 
almost always moves in the opposite direction.

Gold also tends to move in the opposite direction to real interest rates. When 
Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, or TIPS, were introduced in 1997, some 
investors worried they would erode demand for other safe havens. Gold tripled 
in value in the following decade.

Higher real rates have put gold under pressure this year, as it’s harder to 
justify holding a non-yielding asset when you can earn nearly 3% above headline 
US inflation by holding TIPS. But bullion has bucked that relationship before. 
After the West froze Russia’s foreign-exchange reserves in 2022, central banks 
embarked on a gold-buying spree that helped overwhelm the effect of rising real 
rates.

At the Dubai Precious Metals Conference this week, traders pointed out that 
gold had proved a useful counterweight to major economic shocks, including the 
global financial crisis and the Covid pandemic. In both of those cases, gold 
initially sold off, before rallying as interest rates fell and investors dashed 
for safe havens.

That history offers some consolation to gold bulls. A major stock market 
collapse, for example, could bring about lower rates and a flight to safety — 
both tailwinds for bullion. Gold can still be a safe haven, just not an 
all-weather one.

—Jack Ryan, Bloomberg News
