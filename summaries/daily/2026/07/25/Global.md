**Regional Political Economy**
South Africa's handling of sustained xenophobic protests — in which crowds have forcibly displaced migrants and blocked access to health clinics — is inflicting measurable diplomatic and economic costs. President Ramaphosa's government has moved to contain escalation but stopped short of unambiguous condemnation, appearing to calibrate its posture ahead of November elections by emphasizing deportation enforcement and border tightening. The consequence is reputational damage that cuts against decades of post-Mandela positioning as continental leader and multilateral mediator. Ghana has requested Ramaphosa postpone a state visit; Nigerian politicians have raised the prospect of reprisals against South African businesses operating in-country; and ECOWAS has formalized its concern at the regional bloc level. The underlying drivers — high unemployment, failing municipal services, and pervasive corruption — are structural rather than migrant-driven, according to most analysts, suggesting the political response addresses symptoms rather than causes. The economic feedback loop is already visible domestically: labor shortages are emerging in sectors historically reliant on migrant workers, compounding existing growth headwinds.
