Hi, this is Philip Glamann in Beijing.

Less than a week before Chinese leader Xi Jinping travels to the US 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-16/china-considers-byd-to-join-xi-s-ceo-entourage-for-trump-summit?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 to meet President Donald Trump, AI has taken center stage in the bilateral 
relationship.

In some ways the rivalry is straightforward. Most benchmarks show American 
models have a lead in performance that Washington very much wants to maintain, 
for military and economic reasons.

As Trump put it this week: “Whoever wins AI, wins.”

China’s models are very nearly as good, though, and their lower cost makes 
them appealing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-17/can-china-turn-its-ai-reach-into-geopolitical-powerhttps://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-17/can-china-turn-its-ai-reach-into-geopolitical-power?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 in developing nations that Beijing has been courting for decades. Xi used a 
trip to India for a BRICS summit to champion his nation’s AI vision,positioning 
China to lead a “community” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-13/xi-pitches-his-ai-vision-at-brics-summit-as-china-duels-with-us?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 for open-source AI.

Oddly though, there is some agreement on AI of sorts. For one thing, Trump and 
the Chinese government have both pushed back on fears that AI will destroy us 
all.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-09-17/how-budget-ai-is-giving-china-an-edge-video?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-09-17/how-budget-ai-is-giving-china-an-edge-video?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
: How budget AI models give China an edge
This week, Trump dismissed worries 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/trump-s-ai-defense-defies-voter-unease-heading-into-midterms?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 raised by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and backed by OpenAI’s Sam Altman and 
Elon Musk as a “hoax.” Still, Trump did suggest that China could be behind 
those concerns, though he didn’t spell out exactly how.

China also lashed out at the fears raised by the tech titans 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-14/china-rejects-ai-fearmongering-after-amodei-urges-slowdown?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
, with one diplomat saying that “fearmongering, confrontation and vicious 
competition will only disrupt the process of global AI governance and serve the 
interests of no one.”

Both the Trump administration and officials in Beijing have their issues with 
Anthropic. Earlier this year, the White House was peeved because the company 
wanted assurances that its AI wouldn’t be used for mass surveillance of 
Americans or autonomous weapons deployment.

And China didn’t seem terribly pleased that Amodei singled China out in a blog 
post thatwarned about the risks of AI 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-12/anthropic-ceo-says-it-s-time-to-slow-pace-of-improving-ai-models?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
, as he saw them. “If we greatly restrain our AI capabilities in the belief 
that China will do the same, and then China defects, AI could be so powerful 
that such a defection could lead to their geopolitical dominance,” he wrote.

Chinese state media later hit back, calling the slowdown idea “self-serving.” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/chinese-state-media-dismisses-self-serving-ai-slowdown-call?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 (Anthropic also accuses Chinese companies ofpiggybacking off its technology 
through “distillation” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-09/what-is-ai-distillation-and-why-are-us-tech-companies-worried?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 but that’s a rabbit hole for another day.)

Underscoring Beijing’s belief that it should be one of the main writers of the 
rules for the AI road, the English-language China Daily wrote that “a global 
AI-safety framework that excludes China is not quite global.”

Yet China has its own worries about AI and humanity, ones that echo those of 
the tech figures in the US.

On Sunday, China’s top intelligence official, Chen Yixin, wrote that use of 
the tech by rivals could “directly threaten China’s political security, 
institutional security and ideological security.”

Chen’s article, which also warned about AI’s ability to create malware to 
exploit weaknesses in computer systems 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-14/china-spy-chief-warns-of-ai-risks-as-us-tech-leaders-urge-brakes?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
, was significant because it was one of the clearest accounts so far on how 
Beijing assesses the dangers that the flourishing tech poses.
Dario Amodei, Anthropic’s CEOPhotographer: Jason Henry/Bloomberg
For all the heated competition on AI, both sides do in fact share similar 
safety concerns. That’s got some people thinking about the ways in which the 
two sides could cooperate. After all, the US and Soviet Union did as much on 
nuclear weapons.

Media entrepreneur-cum-China analyst Bill Bishop suggested in a newsletter 
this week that Beijing and Washington create a high-level forum that meets 
regularly in a third country so they can sort through their differences.

He suggested that among the first topics of discussion should be “nuclear 
command and control, catastrophic biological risks” and “other areas where 
limited but meaningful cooperation is realistic.”

It is easy to be skeptical that’ll happen, especially at the upcoming summit. 
After all,for all the G2 talk 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2025-11-28/xi-trump-talks-revive-idea-of-a-g-2-running-the-world?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
, areas where the two sides agree are sometimes hard to spot. And expectations 
for outcomes from the Xi-Trump meeting on Thursday at the White House are low.

Right now it looks like the two nations will make a limited deal 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-16/us-china-discuss-cutting-some-tariffs-as-leaders-prepare-to-meet?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 on American agricultural and energy products as well as unfinished Chinese 
goods. In other words, just enough to extend the one-year trade truce, though 
maybe not for a full 12 months.

While there was some speculation China and the US would hold a meeting 
dedicated solely to AI issues before the summit, that never came to pass.

Right now it looks like officials in Beijing and Washington will have to have 
a close call with some of their worst fears on AI before they agree to hold 
serious talks about its dangers.

Thinking Big

A major reason the Chinese government may not be particularly interested in 
slowing in the AI race is that the world’s second-biggest economy is 
increasinglyreliant on the tech 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-09-15/how-ai-is-transforming-china-s-economy-video?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 for growth, now and in the future.

Economic data this week was largely disappointing. One of the few bright spots 
was factory output beating expectations in August, mostly because AI exports 
have been boosted by global demand for the tech goods the nation makes.

Other than that, consumer spending and investment languished 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/china-s-consumption-and-investment-wilt-as-factories-power-aheadhttps://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/china-s-consumption-and-investment-wilt-as-factories-power-ahead?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
. The jobless rate saw an unexpected uptick, and home prices continued to drop.

Data also showed that loan expansion weakened far more than expected in 
August. That’s also worrying for the economy, though the central bank governor, 
Pan Gongsheng,described it as a natural development 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-14/china-s-economy-enters-critical-phase-determining-2026-stimulus?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 during a transition toward a new normal of slower loan growth.

The slowdown in overall financing also helps keep the debt levels stable, he 
wrote in an article.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-05-18/china-economic-data-surprises-on-the-downside-hsbc-says-video?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-05-18/china-economic-data-surprises-on-the-downside-hsbc-says-video?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
: Economic data surprises on the downside
Still, worries about the economy, at least other than humming factories, 
remain. September will be pivotal, if that’s going to change.

Without a sharp turnaround this month, the nation’s economic growth is 
increasingly at risk of falling below the official annual target of 4.5% to 5% 
for a second straight quarter after a sharp deceleration in the April-June 
period.

Australia & New Zealand Banking Group said GDP growth was tracking at 4.2% in 
July-August, less than the second-half rate needed to meet the annual target. 
That goal was already changed to a range to give Beijing more wiggle room.

Despite mounting pressure to ramp up stimulus so domestic demand can recover, 
policymakers appear to be in little hurry to do that.

Instead, they want existing measures to make good. That includes pushing 
state-owned banks to deploy more funding from a program that canunlock 800 
billion yuan ($119 billion) for projects this year 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-24/china-s-119-billion-answer-to-sagging-investment-is-coming-late?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
, 60% more than in 2025.

That program set up in March 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-05/china-keeps-budget-deficit-target-at-record-high-to-spur-economy?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 has been slow to get off the ground.

With a focus on major initiatives such as the Six Networks, the financing 
could set off a new wave of infrastructure investment in the coming months.

The Six Networks program 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-26/how-china-plans-to-supercharge-its-ai-ambitions-with-six-networks-program?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
, part of the latest five-year plan, is important because it marks the first 
time the nation has brought its biggest building projects under a single, 
integrated program, rather than developing transport, energy and communications 
networks separately.

The aim is to not only backstop AI ambitions, but also strengthen the 
manufacturing base and advance the transition to a low-carbon economy. If it 
succeeds, it will bolster China’s resilience to geopolitical tensions, economic 
shocks and natural disasters.

So it’s a big bet and a long-term one. Yet the sooner it gets rolling, the 
better for the economy.

Bloomberg Economics economists Chang Shu and Eric Zhu said that based on the 
upbeat industrial production growth, “the government is unlikely to roll out 
extra stimulus.”

“The path of least resistance is to accelerate input in mega projects in the 
pipeline to ensure growth hits the target range,” they wrote.

Behind the Great Firewall

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

Beijing’s sweeping ban on owning recreational drones in the city following a 
plane crash sparked debate in China this week.

While some people backed tighter security measures in the capital, others 
lamented the potential economic fallout. Many questioned or mocked a rule that
extends to merely flying one of the devices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-14/beijing-bans-drones-in-sweeping-curbs-after-citic-tower-crash?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 for fun.

“Banning people from owning drones they legally purchased before the rules 
took effect makes little sense legally, logically or practically,” one Weibo 
user wrote in a post that drew more than 2,600 likes. “Isn’t that a bit 
ridiculous?”

The move tightened rules in place since earlier this year to cover the 
possession or even storage of recreational drones. The latest curbs — some of 
the tightest in the world — take effect in mid-November.

Drone owners have to either move their aircraft out of the city or turn to a 
government buyback program. The city is offering to pay up to 3,000 yuan unit.

The changes come after a small plane flew into Beijing’s Citic Tower in June, 
killing the pilot and injuring 13 others. The incident took place just miles 
fromthe headquarters of the ruling Communist Party 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-28/beijing-tower-plane-crash-shows-security-gap-near-xi-s-compound?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
 and residence of top leaders.
Citic Tower in Beijing after it was damaged by a small aircraft in June.
Photographer: Kevin Frayer/Getty Images AsiaPac
Officials said the pilot deliberately crashed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-02/beijing-tower-crash-was-a-deliberate-act-by-pilot-probe-finds?cmpid=BBD091826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=8966>
. The aircraft also nearly collided with a commercial jet before impact.

Some people said they supported the policy, arguing that the curbs reflect 
security needs in the capital, even though that would mean an end to things 
like aerial photography.

Others pointed to possible economic implications. “The low-altitude economy 
can bid farewell to Beijing,” one person wrote, referring to a sector that 
policymakers have promoted as a new growth driver.

Many reacted with humor. One Internet user highlighted posts from drone owners 
jokingly trying to dispose of their aircraft.

“Who wants one? Free, just pay the shipping. Help fulfill the dream of someone 
who’s never owned a DJI,” read one post, referring to a leading Chinese maker 
of the drones.
