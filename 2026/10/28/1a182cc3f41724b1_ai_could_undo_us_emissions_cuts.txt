Data centers could erase US climate progress

By Eric Roston 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQdgGkZhizQ/eric-roston?cmpid=BBD102826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10675>
 andOlivia Raimonde 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUdOpjWq8Dk/olivia-raimonde?cmpid=BBD102826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10675>

The surge in construction of fossil-fueled power plants to run AI data centers 
is set to undo 15 years’ worth of greenhouse gas reductions from US electricity 
generation, according to an internal analysis by the Sierra Club.

The US power sector would emit 2.1 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide by 
2030, after factoring in planned AI projects. That’s 618 million tons more than 
2025 levels, by the Sierra Club’s calculations, or about a 42% increase.

A year and a half ago, researchers at the nonprofit environmental group would 
have estimated that the buildout and its impact would have been a quarter or 
half of current projections, saidJeremy Fisher 
<https://www.sierraclub.org/other/authors/jeremy-fisher>, the Sierra Club’s 
principal advisor for climate and energy. “The numbers continue to sort of 
boggle our minds,” he said.

Many data center developers are turning to natural gas to meet the immense 
electricity demand, relying on power sources that are both on and off the grid. 
The bulk of the expected emissions by 2030 would come from sources that are not 
directly linked to the grid, often built by tech companies to avoid the long 
wait for a connection. These could exceed 460 million tons, Fisher said, 
roughly the same as the annual emissions of Mexico.

Helen Kou, a BloombergNEF analyst, said if there’s significant growth in gas 
power for new facilities, “you would presume emissions would increase from year 
to date.”

If the rush to build AI data centers leads to the upper bounds of the 
emissions projected by these researchers, it represents a reversal of a 
two-decade economic and policy trend toward less US power-sector climate 
pollution.

Electricity emissions peaked in 2007. In 2009, thanks to advances in hydraulic 
fracturing, the US became the world’s largest natural gas producer. The 
fracking boom in turn drove down the price of natural gas, displacing dirtier 
coal plants across the US. Meanwhile, the Environmental Protection Agency wrote 
regulations to dial down carbon from power plants, and former President Joe 
Biden aimed to decarbonize the grid completely by 2035.

Today, natural gas prices are historically low and President Donald Trump has 
undertaken a dramatic rollback of climate rules. That helped nudge US 
power-sector emissionsback up 
<https://www.eia.gov/environment/emissions/carbon/> in 2025.

The Sierra Club analysis draws on public filings and other groups’ data center 
projections, including the Environmental Integrity Project’scount of expected 
plants <https://environmentalintegrity.org/reports/the-power-behind-ai/> and 
emissions by 2030.

Many projects could be delayed or canceled. Just last month Oracle Corp. 
submitted aforce majeure 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-24/oracle-cites-force-majeure-to-shield-itself-on-controversial-data-center?cmpid=BBD102826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10675>
 notice to the developer of its Project Jupiter data center in New Mexico, 
saying there is a risk that it won’t come online by 2028 as planned due to 
regulatory setbacks and community opposition.

“The big question of the day is trying to create an accurate forecast of 
project demand,” BloombergNEF’s Kou said.

Methane blind spot

By Zahra Hirji 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVypnhGtqak/zahra-hirji?cmpid=BBD102826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10675>

A novel government program designed to help catch the US oil and gas sector’s 
biggest methane releases, called super emitter events, is on the chopping block.

Launched in late 2024, the Methane Super Emitter Program was set up to enable 
vetted third-party groups 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-02-13/biden-plan-enlisting-citizens-to-police-oil-wells-for-methane-ripped-by-industry?cmpid=BBD102826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10675>
 to use their own monitoring equipment — tools that may provide more 
comprehensive surveillance than what the energy industry typically uses — to 
help improve the search and reporting of the gas to both companies and 
regulators. Compared to carbon dioxide, methane has a roughly80 times 
<https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_Chapter07_SM.pdf>
 warming potential in the first two decades after release.

But the program had barely begun before the Trump administration delayed it 
last year. Now the EPA is proposing to end it as part of a larger overhaul of 
methane rulespreviewed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/trump-epa-readies-bid-to-weaken-methane-controls-for-oil-gas-companies?cmpid=BBD102826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10675>
 in early October and officially unveiled on Tuesday. When he announced it at a 
campaign event in Texas, Trump framed the move as a way to lower energy costs 
in a bid to address voter affordability concerns ahead of next week’s midterm 
elections.

While satellites, artificial intelligence and other technology are making it 
faster to spot methane leaks globally in near real time, there’s now a pullback 
on reporting and slashing methane emissions that extends beyond the US. The 
European Union recently proposed delaying its methane disclosure rules for 
energy imports by a year amid concerns the ongoing Iran war has raised the 
potential of a supply crisis this winter.
US President Donald Trump during a midterm campaign rally at Lubbock Memorial 
Civic Center in Lubbock, Texas on Oct. 27.AFP via Getty Images
Energy companies, researchers and others have long tracked methane emissions 
with ground-level monitoring devices combined with modeling. But newer 
technologies, including satellite imagery and other aerial measurements, have 
revealed methane clouds that were a lot larger than what had been previously 
estimated using traditional methods, said Joseph Goffman, a senior EPA official 
under Biden. The Super Emitter Program was set up to help fill the gap.

Not merely a data reporting program, it’s what’s called a “compliance 
assurance program,” meaning the reporting process was set up to spur companies 
to quickly investigate and fix big methane releases, said Goffman. “Suspending 
or repealing the program will result in significantly higher levels of methane 
emissions.”

The program “was the first of its kind that was developed to harness the 
ever-growing plethora of data from remote sensing platforms, satellites, aerial 
measurements, drones,” said Jonathan Banks, chief of programs and advocacy at 
the Clean Air Task Force, an environmental group.

Before it was put on hold, the EPA had approved one nonprofit, Carbon Mapper, 
to monitor methane releases with airborne technology. Over the course of a few 
days in January 2025, the group identified seven super emitter events and 
submitted the data to the EPA, which didn’t prompt any enforcement action.

If the EPA’s program disappears, Carbon Mapper warned in an email, it could 
lead to a more fragmented, state-by-state regulatory approach that would 
potentially be more complicated for companies to comply with than a single 
federal one. That’s already happening as some states continue to push forward 
on the work regardless of what’s happened at the federal level, with California 
launching a methane satellite program and New Mexico working on its own program.

EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin recently told reporters that the program’s fate 
won’t be finalized until after public comments are considered. “I’m not going 
to comment on the Super Emitter Program where it sounds like we’ve made our 
mind up, because we haven’t,” he said.
