‘Credible Fed chair’

Given a choice between keeping Donald Trump happy or soothing stock and bond 
investors,Kevin Warsh chose the latter 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-16/trump-says-interest-rates-should-be-1-or-less-after-fed-hike?cmpid=091726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260917&utm_content=8976>
.

The Fed chairman and his colleagues agreed unanimously yesterday to raise 
interest rates for the first time in more than three years and, after some 
waffling, investors decided that was a good thing. Stocks are headed higher 
this morning and the yield on the 10-year US Treasury note is back below 5%.

After all Trump’s jawboning about how rates should be at 1% or less, not 3.75% 
to 4% as they are now, Warsh still decided that the greater risk was letting 
above-trend inflation persist. (Trump wasstill at it 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-16/trump-says-interest-rates-should-be-1-or-less-after-fed-hike?cmpid=091726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260917&utm_content=8976>
 after the decision, though he blamed the rest of the Fed board, not his 
hand-picked chairman.)

For bond traders, that goes a long way to bolstering Warsh’s bona fides as a 
warrior against higher prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-16/treasuries-hold-gains-as-fed-hikes-rates-signaling-more-ahead?cmpid=091726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260917&utm_content=8976>
, the enemy of all fixed-income investments. Stock traders are taking comfort 
in the fact that, by raising short-term rates now and signaling more hikes if 
needed, the central bank will rein in inflation.

“The market is taking this as a much more credible Fed chair,” Jeffrey 
Rosenberg, a senior portfolio manager at BlackRock, said on Bloomberg 
Television. —Phil Serafino 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ABoP3GGeTtg/phil-serafino?cmpid=091726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260917&utm_content=8976>

Another worry for bonds

So it’s all clear for further gains in the bond market, now that the Fed has 
committed itself to fighting inflation, right? Well, maybe not.

Although energy prices have been the scourge of bond markets this year, 
investors are worrying that the next spike in priceswill come from food 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-17/food-prices-present-fresh-risk-for-global-bonds-after-selloff?cmpid=091726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260917&utm_content=8976>
.

They see a risk that a “Super” El Niño, tight fertilizer supplies, attacks on 
shipping and fallout from Europe’s record hot summer will push the cost of 
everyday staples higher.

“I think the next supply shock will be on food,” said Marie-Anne Allier, a 
bond manager at Carmignac in Paris. “I don’t think that’s priced in the market 
at the moment, especially as we expect it to be slow but persistent.” —George 
Nixon
