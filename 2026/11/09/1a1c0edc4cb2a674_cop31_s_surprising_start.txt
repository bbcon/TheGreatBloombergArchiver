COP31’s challenges

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD110926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11155>

Climate negotiators from almost 200 countries will spend the next two weeks at 
an annual United Nations summit under a dark cloud of waning ambition to tackle 
rapidly rising global temperatures.

The negotiating agenda at COP31, which opened Monday in the resort city of 
Antalya, Turkey, is narrower than at any other major climate conference in the 
decade since the breakthrough Paris Agreement. Yet two of the factors weighing 
on the proceedings — the conflict-fueled energy crisis and an AI backlash — 
offer potential to spur new action.
The venue for the COP31 summit in Antalya, Turkey.Photographer: Orhan 
Cicek/Anadolu/Getty Images
“War and geopolitical tension persist. Trade routes, energy networks and 
supply chains are being redrawn,” Murat Kurum, Turkey’s environment, 
urbanization and climate change minister, and the COP31 president, said in 
opening remarks. “As crises feed off one another, we must make sure our 
solutions strengthen one another too.”

Some 60,000 <https://transcripts.un.org/en/asset/k19/k19kdqv3oj> diplomats, 
officials and advocates – alongside modest contingents of world leaders and 
business chiefs – are expected to participate in talks aimed at advancing 
emissions reductions, increasing flows of climate finance and boosting efforts 
to adapt to higher temperatures. A signature push by the Turkish organizers 
aims to increase worldwide rates of electrification, an effort to curb 
greenhouse gas pollution by replacing fossil fuels in transportation, industry 
and heating.

It’s a diplomatic undertaking incommensurate with the bleak backdrop. The UN 
warned last month that the world can no longer keep global warming below 1.5C, 
a key threshold staked out by the Paris Agreement. A series of climate-fueled 
extreme weather events this year has underscored the risks that come with 
rising temperatures — including tens of thousands of deaths from lethal heat 
waves and wildfires across Europe and a devastating glacier collapse in Nepal.

Yet the wars in Iran and Ukraine that have strained international alliances 
and at times diverted funding from climate aid to defense spending have also 
revealed the financial toll of countries’ deep reliance on fossil fuels. The 
pain from rising oil and gas prices has provided a catalyst to shift toward 
cleaner energy globally.

Even more than last year’s summit in Brazil, the focus is on delivering on

World Resources Institute’s International Climate Initiative. “It’s not as 
flashy — but it is about getting the job done.”

Severe heat ahead

By Laura Millan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD110926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11155>

The El Niño phenomenon building on the Pacific Ocean will boost global 
temperatures by an additional 0.1C to 0.2C, making the world exceptionally hot 
for the remainder of this year and into early 2027, the World Meteorological 
Organization said.
A pedestrian uses a cooling fan in Barcelona, Spain, in August.Bloomberg
This year is already on track to be one of the warmest on record, with 
temperatures 1.47C (2.65F) above the pre-industrial average between January and 
September, the UN agency said in its State of the Climate report, released on 
Monday as COP31 kicks off in Turkey.

“We are supercharging our weather and El Niño will be a risk multiplier,” said 
WMO Secretary General Celeste Saulo. “El Niño and indeed the state of the 
global climate in 2026 gives new meaning to the phrase ‘off the charts.’”

Temperatures are expected to rise more than 1.5C above pre-industrial levels 
in 2026, according to data released last week by observation agency Copernicus. 
That threshold was already surpassed in 2024, the hottest year on record, and 
the UN expects global temperatures to rise above that level as a long-term 
average.

It forecasts an increase of 1.8C this century in a best-case scenario 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/un-warns-major-emissions-cuts-needed-to-limit-warming-overshoot?cmpid=BBD110926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11155>
, which would require significant reductions in the man-made greenhouse gases 
causing climate change beyond countries’ current commitments. Even if nations 
make good on their current emissions promises, the UN warns that the world is 
on track to warm by 2.6C.

“It’s challenging to adapt to a world that’s warming and warming and we see no 
change to this curve, unfortunately,” Saulo told journalists at a press 
conference in Antalya, Turkey. “The ambition is there, but with current levels 
of CO2 emissions we can’t see a limit of 1.5 degrees.”

Read More: UN Warns Major Emissions Cuts Needed to Limit Warming Overshoot 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/un-warns-major-emissions-cuts-needed-to-limit-warming-overshoot?cmpid=BBD110926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11155>

This year’s El Niño is the strongest on record. The weather phenomenon changes 
temperature and rain patterns across the globe, altering the likelihood of 
droughts, floods, heat and other extremes in various regions. The WMO expects 
it to persist until February, bringing temperature anomalies of as much as 3.7C 
in the waters of the central and eastern Equatorial Pacific, compared to an 
anomaly of 0.2C recorded in March.

“A super El Niño is turbocharging climate change, with impacts delivering a 
deadly double blow,” said the UN climate body’s executive secretary, Simon 
Stiell. “The climate crisis is now a global health crisis of pandemic 
proportions, from toxic pollution to extreme heat and climate disasters 
slamming through health systems and infrastructure.”

Greta’s back (and still a critic)

By Aaron Rutkoff 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ARM9-qdW8iw/aaron-rutkoff?cmpid=BBD110926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11155>

Greta Thunberg may be the world’s most famous climate activist — but that 
doesn’t mean she has universal celebrity status at the world’s biggest summit 
on global warming.
Greta Thunberg stands in the concourse of the COP31 venue in Antalya, Turkey, 
on Monday, November 9.
The 23-year-old stood in a long security line at COP31 late in the afternoon 
on Monday, the start of annual United Nations climate talks held this year in 
Antalya, Turkey. A few people recognized the founder of Fridays for the Future, 
a youth protest movement she began in Stockholm and that inspired legions of 
activists. Some quietly requested photos. Yet delegates from Libya and Somalia 
standing behind Thunberg didn’t know anything about her, or the activism she 
began as a teenager.

Thunberg’s participation in this year’s climate summit — her first appearance 
since the 2021 talks in Glasgow, Scotland — hasn’t been widely publicized, and 
she described herself as skeptical of the proceedings.

“We’ve had 31 COPs now, and we’re still speeding in the wrong direction,” she 
said in an interview in the lengthy queue for credentials that allow access to 
the UN-controlled blue zone, where negotiations will take place over the next 
two weeks.

She’s often upbraided diplomats and world leaders for their lack of urgency, 
including an incendiary 2019 speech at UN headquarters that laid the blame on 
adult inaction on global warming for “stealing her childhood.” She still sees 
the political and business leaders who turn out for climate talks as primarily 
engaged in greenwashing — and expressed concern over the Turkish host 
government’srecord on human rights 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-16/turkey-s-erdogan-widens-crackdown-on-dissent-to-target-entertainers-influencers?cmpid=BBD110926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11155>
.

“As long as we don’t focus on the real crisis, we are losing time,” she said 
Monday. “And that means more lives we’ll never have back.”

On the ground at COP31

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD110926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11155>
 andLaura Millan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD110926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11155>

US mayors, governors and lawmakers will be sweeping across Antalya this week 
in a show of force meant to demonstrate that the country is still making 
progress confronting climate change — even if it’s happening outside of 
Washington.

Seven city mayors and several governors led by New Mexico’s Michelle Lujan 
Grisham will be fanning across the venue, with a new headwind from US midterm 
elections that swept a wave of Democrats into office.
Sculptures on display at the COP31 venue in Antalya, Turkey.Photographer: 
Laura Millan/Bloomberg
International colleagues should see the delegation’s attendance “as a sign of 
hope and a sign that America’s commitment to climate action runs deeper than 
any one administration,” said Michael Cahill, the mayor of Beverly, 
Massachusetts who will be attending along with mayors from Haynesville, 
Louisiana; Raleigh, North Carolina; and Boulder, Colorado, among other cities.

Nearly 20 members of the US House of Representatives also are expected at the 
summit this year. Just one member of the US legislature — Senator Sheldon 
Whitehouse, a Democrat from Rhode Island, was able to attend COP30 in Brazil, 
after a government shutdown complicated travel.

Even the lawmakers’ path to COP31 is reflective of the current US dynamic. 
Because the US State Department has not provided COP31 badges for the 
delegation, they’ve had to work with other accredited parties to get access.

Also at the talks today:

👍 Negotiators reached a deal on the summit’s agenda, avoiding what’s 
traditionally been one of the toughest fights at COP meetings — agreeing on 
what to discuss.

😒 Delegates queued for hours in bright sun to get accreditations until the 
UN’s climate change secretariat advised attendees by mid-afternoon to postpone 
non-essential trips to the COP31 venue until Tuesday, due to the “exceptionally 
high level of interest.”

☕ For those who made it inside, COP attendees were welcomed by an array of 
coffee varieties (Australian, Turkish, Ethiopian — take your pick!), baklava 
and kokorec, a Turkish sandwich made with crispy lamb intestines.

🐼 At national pavilions inside the UN-controlled blue zone, countries 
displayed their strengths on climate — and on everything else. Stuffed panda 
bears were available for cuddling at the Chinese stand, and Saudi Arabia 
charmed visitors with cookies. Geopolitical tensions were evident too. The 
Ukraine, Iran and Palestine used their spaces to detail the impacts of ongoing 
conflicts.
