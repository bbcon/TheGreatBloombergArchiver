Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Grant Smith looks at the latest attacks on shipping through the Strait of 
Hormuz.

The recent recovery in oil exports from the Persian Gulf is looking tenuous 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-31/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-sept-1?cmpid=BBBXT090126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8397>
 again.

By the end of last month, a network of stealthy crude tankers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-27/hormuz-oil-flows-rising-as-gulf-giants-ramp-up-accelerates?cmpid=BBBXT090126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8397>
 had managed to restore flows through the Strait of Hormuz to roughly half 
prewar levels, by sneaking barrels out on shuttle runs to ports outside the 
chokepoint.

While still short of shipments seen before the conflict, the stream of about 6 
million to 8 million barrels a day has gone a long way inkeeping a lid <> on 
crude prices.

It’s also been cited by President Donald Trump and his officials as evidence 
that economic fallout from the war with Tehran isn’t too severe. But the latest 
resurgence of hostilities between the US and Iran puts that supply conduit in 
peril.

American forces hit an island in the Strait of Hormuz. and the Islamic 
Republic responded by launching attacks on the United Arab Emirates and Jordan. 
On Monday,two supertankers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/two-oil-supertankers-hit-by-projectiles-in-hormuz-marisks-says?cmpid=BBBXT090126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8397>
 were struck by projectiles while attempting to transit the sea corridor.

One of the vessels was operated by Saudi Arabian shipping line Bahri, while 
the other was owned by Sinokor — the secretive firm that has been at the heart 
of the Hormuz shuttle trade.

The Saudis had only just pivoted back to the gulf, ramping up operations at 
their giantRas Tanura terminal <> as attacks by Houthi rebels in Yemen disrupt 
the Red Sea route that Riyadh has been using as a backup throughout the 
conflict.

Now the kingdom’s exports are being slammed on both sides at once, just as the 
supply of fuels faces a precarious moment, with asqueeze on vital products 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-08/war-brings-winter-of-discontent-for-the-world-s-workhorse-fuel?cmpid=BBBXT090126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260901&utm_content=8397>
 like gasoline and diesel sending prices higher.

Trump has shrugged off growing criticism over the entanglement with Iran by 
describing it as a “relatively little war” by America’s standards.

But for the tankers that must navigate the Strait of Hormuz, and the global 
oil markets they supply, it remains consequential.

—Grant Smith, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

The Middle East and North Africa’s wind and solar additions are accelerating 
as deployment broadens across more markets, according to BloombergNEF. The 
region installed a record 16 gigawatts in 2025, with additions set to rise 37% 
in 2026. Saudi Arabia remains the largest market, while four other markets are 
expected to exceed 1 gigawatt of installations this year.
