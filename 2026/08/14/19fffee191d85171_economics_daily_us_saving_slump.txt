US Saving Dwindles

Keen readers of this newsletter may recall, three years back, we highlighted a 
question 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2023-06-30/china-s-perfect-storm-threatens-growth-outlook?cmpid=BBD081426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7662>
 asked by quantitative finance specialist Tan Kai Xian about whether the 
budding craze in artificial intelligence would result in a net addition of 
investment or simply divert capital away from other things.

Tan’s hypothesis was that higher AI capital spending would come, at least 
partially, at the cost of investment elsewhere. That argument has since 
practically becomeconventional wisdom 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-10-24/ai-data-center-boom-threatens-trump-s-manufacturing-revival?cmpid=BBD081426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7662>
. The AI “crowding out” debate has even gone on to look at whether the US 
Treasury has to pay more to borrow due to massive debt sales by the 
hyperscalers.

Now, Tan, US analyst at Gavekal Research, is flagging a new concern: where the 
funding will come from for consumer spending gains. Lately, some of that growth 
has come from Americanssaving less 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-17/americans-pull-back-on-retirement-savings-as-everyday-expenses-climb?cmpid=BBD081426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7662>
. The latest saving-rate reading, for June, was 2.7% — less than half where it 
was just two years ago, and not terribly far from the lowest on record in a 
data series going back to 1947.

Part of the drop in saving is likely due to the continued challenge to 
purchasing power frominflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-12/us-core-inflation-was-subdued-in-july-easing-pressure-on-fed?cmpid=BBD081426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7662>
. July data showed consumer prices rose more than average hourly earnings on a 
year-on-year basis. Some households just have less cash left over to sock away. 
Another is the rise instock market wealth 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-11/us-household-net-worth-posts-smallest-gain-in-a-year?cmpid=BBD081426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7662>
, giving fortunate asset owners confidence to spend more of their incomes.

What happens next if real incomes don’t start climbing? Tan wrote in a note 
this week that, after the saving rate hits 0%, the choice will be to either 
borrow or sell assets to keep boosting spending.

In the years before the financial crisis, households borrowed to fund their 
outlays — taking out home equity lines of credit in some cases as their 
property values swelled. After the crisis, they focused on rebuilding balance 
sheets. And at this point, there’sno sign 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-11/us-household-delinquencies-improve-in-ny-fed-quarterly-report?cmpid=BBD081426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7662>
 of an aggregate leveraging-up, Tan wrote.

His base case is for Americans to start selling assets to fund consumption. In 
part that may reflect the nation’s aging demographics. As people head deeper 
into retirement, they’re expected to be selling their holdings, Tan noted.

That could start something of a downward cycle: “If asset prices fall as a 
result, the consumer wealth effect will diminish. In turn, this will weigh on 
consumption and corporate profits,” Tan wrote. While the risk isn’t imminent, 
it’s worth thinking about, he concluded.

That makes the July retail sales report, due out at 8:30 a.m. Washington time 
Friday, all the more important as the latest gauge of US spending.

While support from Beijing can’t be seen as the only reason for the 
increasingly lopsided trade between the world’s biggest economies, it plays a 
significant role in key areas like cars, semiconductors, solar panels and wind 
turbines, Maria Grazia Attinasi, Lukas Boeckelmann, Isabella Moder, Til Pommer 
and Tajda Spital wrote.
via European Central Bank via Bloomberg
“Our findings suggest that they may meaningfully reshape trade within 
strategically important sectors,” the blog post said. “In particular, if 
subsidies boost exports beyond what underlying economic conditions would 
suggest, they may entail significant adjustment costs for trading partners, 
including job losses or de-industrialization, ultimately causing trade 
tensions.”
