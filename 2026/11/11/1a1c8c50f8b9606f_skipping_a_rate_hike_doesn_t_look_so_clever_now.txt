An Information-Rich Inflation Report

October turns out to have been an information-rich 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-10-29/fed-rates-lord-let-me-hike-just-not-now?cmpid=BBD111126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11228>
 month, as Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh would put it. The new information, in the 
form of inflation data for October, suggests that the Fed probably should have 
hiked last month, as many said at the time. Warsh tortured logic to justify the 
inaction, though it seemed clear that avoiding Trumpian attack before the 
midterms was a factor. However, these figures make abundantly obvious why 
voters were so angry when they went to the polls last week.

Year-on-year data aren’t available because the government shutdown nixed an 
inflation report for last October. But the 11-month inflation rate, from 
November, showed a rise on last month, while the 13-month rate topped 4%:

Month-on-month measures of core inflation — the trimmed mean excluding 
outliers, and the median — both also suggested that October saw a sharp 
increase that could not be attributed to oil prices:

Food inflation, which has grown politically sensitive in recent years, and 
services inflation excluding shelter — a number dubbed the “supercore” by 
former chairman Jerome Powell — also moved higher:

Unlike some recent bad inflation prints, this one couldn’t be blamed on an 
obvious outlier. As Barclays’ Pooja Sriram pointed out, the increase ranged 
across furniture, appliances, household supplies, toys, televisions, 
smartphones, laptops and PCs, cosmetics, and even prescription drugs.
In our view, this side of the report carries clear imprints of freight costs 
spilling over into consumer prices, as well as continued AI-related inflation.
All of this hit particularly badly after Fed Governor Christopher Waller 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/fed-s-waller-backs-december-rate-hike-says-open-to-larger-move?cmpid=BBD111126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11228>
, who last year was dissentingin favor of rate cuts 
<https://www.bis.org/speeches/20250805-case-cutting-now>, gave a speech in 
London 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-10/fed-s-waller-supports-rate-hike-in-december-video?cmpid=BBD111126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11228>
 suggesting that a “jumbo” hike of 50 basis points should be a possibility. 
Saying he supported a 25-basis-point hike next month, he predicted that 
inflation for October would come in at about 0.3%, implying that the annual 
rate would edge closer to 4%:
If inflation comes in substantially higher than I expect, more robust action 
by the FOMC may be called for. September’s inflation data were bad. There was 
no silver lining.
It’s not clear whether Waller meant to hint at a 50-basis-hike in one go, or 
just a further hike after December. After the data,Lorie Logan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/fed-s-logan-favors-at-least-two-hikes-says-pace-could-quicken?cmpid=BBD111126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11228>
, head of the Dallas Fed, directly proposed the latter:
It remains clear to me, given the current outlook, that the FOMC needs to 
implement at least two more 25-basis-point rate increases.
Neither Waller nor Logan dissented in favor of hikes last month, so the wholly 
reasonable response to this concertedly hawkish Fedspeak was to price in a hike 
for next month as a total certainty. This is how the expected number of hikes 
by the end of 2026, and by September next year, has moved since the Fed raised 
rates in September:

There’s now an 11% chance of a jumbo hike next month, so the market still does 
not take that idea terribly seriously — but the notional terminal rate for this 
cycle continues to be all over the place. Last week, the market doubted there 
would be even two more hikes in total; now three is regarded as a certainty.

The predictable result was to upend the bond market, again. The two-year 
Treasury yield, particularly sensitive to rate expectations, had its biggest 
daily rise since the reversal after the Liberation Day tariffs last April:

Meanwhile, the 10-year yield had appeared poised to plummet after long bonds 
grew heavily oversold at the end of last month. Instead, the yield hit the 
bottom of the marked upward trend it had formed for the year, and bounced 
higher. A failed attempt to break out of a trend like this could impact market 
psychology:

Easing bond yields, in combination with the falling oil price, have relieved 
pressure on a range of risk assets around the world in the last few weeks, and 
so it was perhaps reassuring that US stocks were relatively unscathed by this 
news. The S&P 500 fell 0.38%, while its equal-weighted version was virtually 
flat, easing only 0.02%.

So maybe one other useful piece of information for Warsh and his colleagues is 
that the stock market might be prepared to tolerate rate rises — if they head 
off longer yields at the scary heights of a month ago, and if they really do 
rein in inflation, which appears to be entrenched once more as a problem.

Anthropic’s New Dawn

Anthropic PBC’s imminent blockbuster initial public offering, we now know, 
will seek to raise up to $90.4 billion. That’s a lot of money, but it’s grown 
even more in significance thanks to timing: It arrives just as investors are 
worrying about the finance for artificial intelligence and grow concerned about 
tighter regulation.

The scale of Anthropic’s ambition is not in doubt. Its target includes a 
concurrent private placement, at a fully dilutedvaluation of about $1.4 trillion
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/anthropic-targets-64-4-billion-ipo-nearing-spacex-s-record?cmpid=BBD111126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11228>
, according to its latest filing with the US Securities and Exchange 
Commission. The combined fundraising would surpass the $75 billion raised by 
Elon Musk’s SpaceX in June, and set a new record for public-market financing:

Likely rate hikes make the path murkier. As arguably the most consequential AI 
IPO to date – the first frontier lab to enter public markets — the stakes are 
high. The company’s revenue growth over the last three years, as illustrated by 
Bloomberg colleagues below, is phenomenal — but it must show that it can raise 
the money successfully if investors’ confidence is to be sustained:

So far, the extraordinary sales growth laid out in Anthropic’s S-1 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TNJT0NRKV2TQ?cmpid=BBD111126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11228>
 has put wind in the company’s sails, providing something more tangible than 
the promise of AI’s transformative potential. While profitability is by no 
means assured, it’s possible to see a credible path toward it.

Even if the IPO falls short of the initial reported valuation of $2 trillion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-23/anthropic-ipo-why-a-2-trillion-valuation-faces-major-risks?cmpid=BBD111126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11228>
, Bloomberg Opinion colleague Chris Hughessuggests that 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TOAJN6KGIFPL?cmpid=BBD111126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11228>
 figure should be within reach by 2029. But that forecast comes with a caveat, 
Bloomberg Intelligence’s Mandeep Singh points out. The company has almost $450 
billion in compute commitments through 2030. That means it has to sustain its 
premium pricing compared to competitors such as Google’s Gemini, OpenAI’s 
ChatGPT and xAI’s Grok:
Intensifying price competition could make it more difficult to preserve gross 
margin while generating enough enterprise token consumption to absorb these 
infrastructure commitments.
The big hyperscalers — Alphabet, Meta Platforms, Amazon.com and Microsoft — 
all now have wide economic moats to bolster their pricing. It’s not possible 
for all the frontier AI labs to preserve them in perpetuity, and it is possible 
that one or more will fall by the wayside. Last year’s DeepSeek moment showed 
how vulnerable they remain to disruption.
Anthropic’s IPO will be a yardstick for OpenAI.Photographer: Michael 
Nagle/Bloomberg
More realistically, they can hope to maintain sufficient competitive edge to 
sustain healthy margins, possibly through high barriers to entry. 
Paradoxically, Thomas Tzitzouris of Strategas’ fixed-income team argues that 
regulations to slow down data-center construction, which look likely since the 
midterms, could help in this by making it harder for smaller competitors to 
build capacity and raise capital. If new facilities might never be completed, 
he argues, it “strangles smaller players’ access to” financial markets:
Why would I want to lend against that? I think this strengthens the argument 
for big tech players to slow down, improve their cash flow metrics in the 
intermediate term, and continue to reap the benefits of almost-oligopoly 
pricing.
Anthropic’s IPO will be a yardstick for Sam Altman’s OpenAI, expected to go 
public next year. The sheer size of the offerings could test the market’s 
capacity. If OpenAI does go public, Nathan Sonnenberg of Pitcairn argues, 
investors can commit only so much capital before having to sell something else 
to make room:
And markets tend to die one of two ways: if the Fed raises rates too much... 
or if you get too much supply in the market. We’ve also seen a lot of 
challenges to lesser IPOs. The market doesn’t want lower-quality names, as 
there’s a lot of concern about the longevity, the inevitability, and the 
profitability of AI.
Bob Elliott of Unlimited Funds argues that how this IPO trades will be 
crucial. Without strong performance, “investors would likely increasingly 
question whether all the planned capex can pay for itself as expected.” The 
extraordinary profit growth of the last few years has been dependent on high 
and accelerating capex, so this is crucial.

With all of that to consider, and the forthcoming publication of Nvidia Corp. 
results amid the ongoing spy-thriller-cum-soap-opera that is Jensen Huang’s 
affair with a possible Chinese spy, this looks like a pivotal month for the AI 
trade.

—Richard Abbey

Survival Tips

When in Rome, do as the Romans. Therefore, when in South America, go to a 
football match. I’ve taken advantage of a reporting trip in the region to see
Sao Paulo draw with deadly local rivals Corinthians 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q5XvqNT6lGU> in Santos, and then Boca Juniors 
clinch the Argentine championship <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lPC7Aj0vffA> 
with a 95th-minute goal. Several agencies (I usedthis one 
<https://homefans.com/clubs/boca-juniors>) can get club season-ticket holders 
to chaperone you through the whole process, which was a great deal.

Neither game was particularly good; I saw a total of one goal. But that didn’t 
really matter. It’s fun, and you learn a lot.Pele played his entire career 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JbzNGyFXDWQ> for Santos in a stadium that 
holds 16,000. It was intense, deafening, and seriously uncomfortable — there 
were places where you couldn’t even stand up:

English fans claim that flair players like Pele couldn’t survive a “rainy 
night in Stoke”; it turns out that he spent his entire career in just such 
conditions.

Meanwhile, at Boca’s Bombonera stadium, home of Diego Maradona, many fans were 
waving Swedish flags:

Why? Boca got their blue with a yellow stripe uniforms 
<https://www.google.com/search?sca_esv=f2ff985aa87c8f33&rlz=1C1GCEA_enUS1179US1179&sxsrf=AKL5yDi05jFksZeoCuMyFt0-f35mWtd9eQ:1794364502207&udm=2&fbs=ACAhTvwdifjn2dKXmjtOfBxVFl_koHVe5RLSnJlTH2QYaq_YVdbYfq_upmJPcFgYr-v9gSTAIQEOuzyhFqt8W8FJFzaCBHHB7ccG-C-SKFvWqUwLna5RNB-ZMFcFtQDobhylwIe-aiffUuyp6dSdiinv_jh7VwhUTII1tcKKGBbvRwD48ddsAgs5YiwaP5aH5KeU9PDQJKHpmgWHBUnRgX69H-CAhNoNxg&q=boca+juniors+uniforms&sa=X&ved=2ahUKEwj7joHW8P6XAxWhEFkFHSZjE7MQtKgLegQIExAB&biw=2250&bih=1202&dpr=0.8>
 in 1906 because of a clash with another club that wore black and white. They 
decided to adopt the flag of the first ship to enter the port of Boca the next 
day — and it was Swedish. They now have a Swedish shirts sponsor, and can’t get 
enough of their Swedish flags. If you’re ever in this part of the world, give 
it a try.
