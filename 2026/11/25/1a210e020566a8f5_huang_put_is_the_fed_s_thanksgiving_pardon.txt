The Fed Off the Hook

Has Jensen Huang just let the Federal Reserve off the hook? By the end of last 
week, the chorus was growing loud that the central bank was on the verge of a 
major policy error, and must relent in its intent to keep tightening rates.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who had previously been adamant that Nvidia 
Corp. needed a new CEO from outside the company, went on the record over the 
weekend to argue that the Fed wasovertightening 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/bessent-warns-fed-risks-overtightening-as-us-markets-swoon?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
. Numerous Wall Streetvoices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-23/us-stock-selloff-revives-question-of-a-fed-put-this-time-under-warsh?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
 joined him in a chorus as last week ended with a vertiginous selloff.

Whatever struggles went on behind the scenes, Bessent lost out over Huang 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-23/us-stock-selloff-revives-question-of-a-fed-put-this-time-under-warsh?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
. A reversal over firing Nvidia’s talismanic co-founder took the place of the 
much-urged pivot on monetary policy to drive a big market rebound on Monday. 
Rather than a Fed “put,” President Donald Trump and the Nvidia board provided a
“Huang put.” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-23/trump-puts-a-floor-under-ai-trade-with-push-for-huang-s-return?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>

That took heat out of the debate over the Fed. But Huang’s reinstatement 
didn’t end the issue. After gaining $968 billion in market cap on Monday (an 
amount somewhat bigger than the entire market value of JPMorgan Chase & Co.), 
it lost $282 billion (a bit more than the whole of Morgan Stanley) on Tuesday 
as the Nasdaq 100 dropped 1.99%. Anthropic PBC has now fallen below its IPO 
price in less than a week.

Through these tech gyrations, expectations for the Fed, as measured by the 
Bloomberg World Interest Rate Probability function, remained fairly steady. A 
hike next month is still priced as a certainty. By September of next year, a 
further 1.5 hikes are priced in — a significant rethink after the market braced 
for three more hikes in 2027 only a few weeks ago:

This is the last week for Fed officials to opine in public before the quiet 
period ahead of the December meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee. John 
Williams, head of the New York Fed, didn’t offer anything that could be called 
a pivotin a speech 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-23/fed-s-williams-says-officials-must-act-over-time-on-inflation?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
. He said that inflation “has continued to run too high,” and that he “would 
not exclude moving at consecutive meetings at all if that was what was needed.”

Implicitly, then, the plan is to hike next month, hold in January, and expect 
to hike again in March if necessary. That’s probably something the market could 
live with, despite the recent flurry of anxiety.

So far this year, the economy has been strikingly resilient, and it’s not 
clear that a bad few weeks for equities should force a change of direction. 
Ajay Rajadhyaksha of Barclays described equity investors calling on the Fed to 
relent as “some bunch of cry babies”:
The Nasdaq after the Iran war went up 30% in 10 weeks.. And I don’t remember a 
single equity guy saying the Fed should hike because asset prices were going up 
too quickly. And now we have given back 17% on the Nasdaq in the last eight 
weeks, which is a smaller move in percentage terms, and every single equity 
investor is coming out of the woodwork saying the Fed has lost its mind.
The US and global economies have survived the prolonged closure of the Strait 
of Hormuz far better than economists had predicted, just as last year’s tariff 
war saw the US suffer its greatest trade deficit on record while China enjoyed 
its biggest ever surplus. Rajadhyaksha said that with such signs of resilience, 
and with consumption in the first three quarters adding 1.8% to gross domestic 
product, “we don’t think the Fed is backing off.”

December’s meeting comes the day before the release of inflation data for this 
month, so today’s announcement of Personal Consumption Expenditure inflation 
for October could be the last significant chance to change the Fed’s direction. 
Core PCE has long been the Fed’s favored measure, and for many years showed 
inflation to be more moderate than the more widely cited Consumer Price Index 
measure.

Unfortunately for hopes of executing a dovish pivot, that has turned around in 
the last few years. Core PCE is now above the Fed’s upper range of 3% and 
projected to rise further:

Choice of metrics becomes important here as the trimmed mean prepared by the 
Dallas Fed, which excludes outliers and is beloved of statisticians, has been 
declining toward 2% in an unusually wide divergence. But the Fed cannot change 
its chosen benchmark at a point when it looks bad without doing severe damage 
to its credibility. Room for maneuver is limited.

The most coherent calls for dovishness center on the housing market. The 
upward swing in long-term bond yields has also brought mortgage rates far too 
high for comfort:

This is exacerbated by the fact that many exceptionally generous deals 
negotiated five years ago, when rates were being held low in the wake of the 
pandemic, are now expiring. As this chart from Capital Economics shows, the 
proportion of mortgages below 4%, or even 3%, is declining, while the share at 
more than 6% is rising fast. The proportionate hit on many borrowers if rates 
continue at their current levels could be worse than at first appears:

This has contributed to ongoing very sluggish growth in house prices, which is 
a further deterrent against selling and building turnover. Wealth effects, for 
now, need to come from the stock market, not from bricks and mortar:

That translates into frustratingly slow growth in construction. The pace of 
new houses and of new permits has slowed over the last year:

Absent a collapse of the circular financing underpinning AI (probably the 
greatest fear of the moment), the case for a more dovish Fed must rest on 
housing and its effect on consumption. But even there, the evidence is that the 
consumer isn’t hurting...

Counting Your Blessings

One other factor perhaps undermines the calls for the Fed to stay its hand; 
step by step, an upbeat inUS consumer confidence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-24/us-consumer-confidence-surges-to-highest-since-early-2025?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
 is taking hold. The Conference Board’s measure recorded a consecutive 
increase, jumping to its highest level since early in 2025. It appears that 
consumers are heartened by a relatively stable labor market and this month’s 
clear decline in gasoline prices. The revival doesn’t suggest that the factors 
responsible for the prior trough in sentiment have disappeared, but it does 
imply the economy is in little need of fresh stimulus.

In large part, this can be thanks to lower uncertainty after the midterm 
elections. Optimism — as measured by the University of Michigan’s equivalent 
survey — was particularly high among Democrats and independents, which is not 
surprising. But November’s increase was broad-based:

Consumers are feeling better about their personal income, business conditions, 
and the labor market, while their worries about inflation appear to have 
stabilized:

How long this lasts will depend in large part on the cost of everyday 
essentials — which might again be taken as a message to the Fed not to relent 
on inflation. As LPL Financial’s Jeffrey Roach points out, although fuel 
accounts for a relatively small share of household spending, gasoline prices 
remain an important driver of consumers’ perceptions of the broader economy.

The pullback in crude prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-23/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-nov-24?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
 has translated into a roughly 50-cent-a-gallonreduction at the pump 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-17/us-gas-pump-price-hits-lowest-since-march-as-oil-supply-recovers?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
 over the past month alone, offering some immediate relief. Even so, gas still 
sits well above pre-conflict levels. The gap that remains is a reminder of how 
deeply the Middle East has hurt optimism in the US, and how much a return to 
normalcy could help restore animal spirits:

Retail sales 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TONG0JKK3NYF?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
, another proxy of consumer health, are demonstrating resilience despite the 
stress from rising prices. October’s data print from the Commerce Department 
showed underlying demand rose a stronger-than-expected 0.6%, while the value of 
retail purchases, not adjusted for inflation, increased 0.2%. This outcome was 
certainly nothing exciting, but showed that consumers are still spending:

Beneath this, the signs of stress are growing harder to ignore. Consumer 
borrowing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/us-household-delinquencies-rise-slightly-ny-fed-report-shows?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
 has surged by the most in nearly four years, according to the Fed. Both 
revolving credit, which includes card debt, and non-revolving credit recorded 
sizable increases. This points to a growing reliance on borrowing to sustain 
the pace of consumer spending — even if overall household debt remains 
considerably more manageable than it was in the wake of the Global Financial 
Crisis:

Next month’s rate hike will continue this vicious cycle, but it remains to be 
seen whether it will be enough to pull back spending. Fitch’s third-quarterUS 
Consumer Monitor 
<https://www.fitchratings.com/research/us-public-finance/us-consumer-monitor-3q26-12-11-2026>
 shows spending grew by an annualized 3.8% in both the second and third 
quarters. The rating agency found growth across services and most goods 
categories, with particularly strong gains in food services and accommodation, 
transportation, healthcare, furnishings and clothing.

Fitch’s Olu Sonola points out that although nominal disposable income rose by 
about 4.6% year-over-year in September, real income growth slowed to just 1% as 
inflation ate into household purchasing power.

With inflation expectations still elevated, the squeeze on consumers looks set 
to intensify, most painfully in food prices over the holiday season. The latest
Bloomberg Price Index 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TP0K9MKIUPS6?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
, an alternative-data estimate of US CPI, showed grocery prices posting their 
biggest monthly increase since April. Joe Brusuelas of RSM US warns this is 
only the beginning:

A lot of this is being driven by factors far beyond the Fed’s control and 
doesn’t necessarily justify higher rates. Rising input costs driven by 
fertilizes, tariffs and bad weather are likely to offset any relief from lower 
energy costs. An intenseEl Niño 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-12/super-el-nino-s-odds-of-being-worst-ever-are-edging-lower-us-cpc-says?cmpid=BBD112526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11782>
, in particular, is reshaping rainfall patterns and threatening key commodities 
that the US doesn’t produce itself. Brusuelas expects prospects of higher food 
prices to persist into the first half of next year. For now, at least, consumer 
confidence is recovering regardless.

—Richard Abbey
