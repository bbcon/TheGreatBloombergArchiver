Be-Bop-a-Lula! <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vDU9FP5_B2M>

Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva is Brazil’s incumbent president. He has already held 
power for 12 years this century, which were generally prosperous ones for his 
country — and madefantastic profits 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2022-11-01/lula-could-be-a-good-friend-to-brazil-investors?cmpid=BBD102726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10591>
 for investors in Brazilian stocks. Yetpolls 
<https://www.as-coa.org/articles/poll-tracker-brazils-2026-presidential-election>
 ahead of Sunday’s second round of presidential elections were too close to 
call. Bear this in mind before viewing the extraordinary market reaction to his 
come-from-behind victory over the right-winger Flavio Bolsonaro.

Stocks greeted the news with a selloff of almost 25% for an exchange-traded 
fund that tracks the MSCI Brazil index — which eventually clawed back ground to 
finish off only 18% for the day. This was a selloff on the scale of the 1987
Black Monday 
<https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/stock-market-crash-of-1987> 
crash, when the S&P 500 fell 20.5%:

That ETF is priced in dollar terms, and this reflected a currency effect. 
Brazil’s real had the kind of day that normally only happens when a country 
gives up on a currency peg and intentionally devalues. In Brazil’s case, this 
hadn’t happened since 1999, at the tail end of a series of emerging markets 
crises. Amazingly, even though the real is now free floating, its initial 
selloff against the dollar was even greater than on the day it gave up the peg, 
as this terminal chart illustrates:
Bloomberg
Why so terrible? Bolsonaro’s demand for the result to be annulled isn’t great 
for the prospects of social and political harmony. Neither is President Donald 
Trump’s comment that the result“looks rigged.” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/trump-says-brazil-election-looked-rigged-after-lula-victory?cmpid=BBD102726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10591>
 But the margin of 3.6 points looks difficult to overturn, and cannot be 
explained merely by the decision to extend voting hours. Rafael Ch of Signum 
Global predicted that the judiciary would be reluctant to annul the election, 
with targeted supplementary voting in affected areas (unlikely to change the 
result) the more likely outcome.

Assuming there is no great fear of unrest and a contested result, the market 
reaction only makes sense if Lula’s victory was a) very surprising and b) very 
damaging for Brazil. Let’s take those in turn.

The big factor counting against Lula was his second-place showing in the first 
round 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-10-06/a-top-heavy-stocks-rally-is-daring-bond-yields-to-break-it?cmpid=BBD102726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10591>
. Since Brazil has had its current format, the first-round winner had always 
prevailed in the two-candidate run-off. But Lula had been the narrow favorite 
last month. Polls in the past week suggested that the race was tightening, and 
prediction markets continued to give him him a 20% chance. That’s bad in a 
two-horse race, but showed considerable doubts about the outcome. Still, it was 
evidently not enough to prevent stock and bond investors from pricing in a 100% 
chance for Bolsonaro:

This was a surprise, but no “black swan.” The narrative that it was part of a 
generalized right-wing wave across the continent — which always ignored big 
differences between countries and voters’ increasing reluctance to view 
politics on a left-right spectrum — gave Bolsonaro a sheen of inevitability. 
But even if this was an upset, did it justify a selloff on this scale? Across 
asset classes, the reversal was historic:

Lula had promised a 15% increase in social welfare payments 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-09-29/what-s-going-to-break-in-the-bondpocalypse?cmpid=BBD102726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10591>
 in the campaign’s closing days, which would worsen fiscal issues, while 
Bolsonaro was aiming to cut spending by 1.5% of gross domestic product. It 
therefore makes sense for bonds to sell off on the news, but this still looks 
like a wild overreaction. Lula’s party lost ground in the legislature, where 
Bolsonaro’s Liberals will hold more than a third of the seats in the Senate. 
The room for irresponsibility by Lula is limited — and many had doubted that 
Bolsonaro would find his projected cuts.

Besides, Lula has a long history in power, and has shown a persistent strong 
streak of pragmatism. His instincts are plainly market-unfriendly, and he 
hasn’t ever delivered the kind of deep-seated reforms that Brazil needs, but 
he’s been smart enough to keep the country away from crisis.

It’s unquestionably a surprising and disappointing result for markets. But 
there was no reason for it to prompt a Black Monday-level selloff.

That it did so was down to investors, not candidates or voters. Latin American 
stock markets are driven by foreign passive flows, which have a strong and 
well-marked tendency to freak out at any sign of victory for candidates on the 
political left. This creates buying opportunities. Variant Perception created 
this chart two years ago, before the recent run of victories for the right. The 
returns in the year after elections that had been considered contentious proved 
vast:

This is all predictable, but investors go through the same dance moves, 
creating the same buying opportunities. Variant describes it as follows:
Perceived risk premium is the driver of equity returns; the realization 
post-contentious election that market-unfriendly policy changes are unlikely 
benefits risk assets the same way pre-election jitters harms them.
International investors don’t matter as much as they used to. Latin America’s 
main countries have spent two decades working strenuously to develop domestic 
pools of capital that will buy in local currency. As Societe Generale SA shows, 
foreign investors account for only about 10% of Brazilian real-denominated 
debt. The chance for a sudden crisis, as happened many times in the 1990s, is 
far more limited:

Many investors were caught out, so the selling may well not be over, but there 
do appear to be buying opportunities. Marc Chandler of Bannockburn Global Forex 
said he “would be tempted to buy a local currency bond yielding about 12.8%” as 
market positioning had been the main driver, while the selloff had been 
sufficient to alter the risk-reward trade-off. SocGen’s Brendan McKenna argued 
that the best opportunity would be in the currency, as likely higher rates from 
the central bank to combat inflation would help the real while pushing up 
yields.

Is Lula going to deliver prosperity for the people who have put their faith in 
him for a record fourth time? Probably not. Will this prove to be another Lula 
buying opportunity? In all likelihood, yes it will.

Not Claude’s Forte

Anthropic PBC’s mega initial public offering is a step closer and the latest 
test of investors’ conviction in artificial intelligence. Depleting free cash 
flows, concerns about the technology’s existential risks, and rising demands 
for compensation from debt investors have fueled a market selloff. Anthropic’s 
debut next month will offer the most important gauge yet of willingness to keep 
backing the boom.

The company’s S-1 regulatory filing 
<https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm>
, which arrived Monday, shows strong fundamentals. As Tema ETFs’ Yuri 
Khodjamirian points out, Anthropic is already profitable on an adjusted basis, 
delivering a 19% operating margin in the second quarter and generating $3.9 
billion in operating cash flow in the first half of 2026. Whether this 
performance is sustainable largely depends on the company’s economic moat. For 
now, the read from regulatory filings suggests it is on course to top the $75 
billion raised by SpaceX in June:

How Anthropic fares will have a profound impact on the AI trade. But a strong 
debut wouldn’t give other companies going public or raising debt a free pass, 
because it might be attributed to Anthropic’s own strong fundamentals.

The company’s revenue surged to $16.3 billion in the first half 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-anthropic-ipo/?cmpid=BBD102726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10591>
 of 2026 from $1.2 billion in the same period of 2025, which Michael Monaghan 
of Founder ETFs argues makes it the fastest-growing company in history. The 
growth and profitability of its rivals are less established.

An undersubscribed debut would likely carry lasting consequences, with 
investors already skeptical about the timing. Sam Altman’s OpenAI had toshave 
off about $150 billion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-22/openai-shifts-value-to-1-25-trillion-as-funding-talks-finalize?cmpid=BBD102726_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10591>
 from its valuation after investors pushed back during a recent funding round. 
It can ill afford a damp squib from Anthropic. But it remains unclear how long 
OpenAI can afford to stay private, given the scale of its capital requirements 
and the limits of what private markets can support.

Even public companies are finding it hard to finance AI infrastructure. 
Bloomberg Economics’ Estelle Ou argues that when capital expenditure exceeds 
operating cash flow, companies without existing reserves to draw on must seek 
external financing. This exposes them to shifts in funding conditions. But 
PGIM’s Chief Investment Strategist Robert Tipp suggests that there is plenty of 
capital that can be deployed:
Demand is there, too. Questions remain about the competitive landscape and 
whether margins will be high enough to justify the investment once the software 
and hardware reach the market.
Anthropic’s debut will also draw comparisons with the reception for SpaceX 
earlier this year. Marta Norton of Empower notes that IPOs often carry some 
volatility. As with SpaceX, we should look beyond the immediate reception:
I’m not sure the market will, though. Because AI is such a polarizing issue, 
people may draw sweeping conclusions from day-to-day movements — about whether 
AI is good for humanity, whether it’s overhyped, or whether it’s a good 
investment. We should try to look past the short-term volatility. What people 
will want to track is demand. 
In the end, Anthropic’s debut could offer a clear signal of cracks in the AI 
complex — or its strong fundamentals might just drive a favorable reception 
that masks broader AI weakness.

—Richard Abbey
