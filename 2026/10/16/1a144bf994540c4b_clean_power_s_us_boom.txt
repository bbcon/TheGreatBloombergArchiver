Renewables record

By Leslie Kaufman 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUZ8izNmriE/leslie-kaufman?cmpid=BBD101626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10180>
 andWill Wade 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQKkcaEIb6M/will-wade?cmpid=BBD101626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10180>

The US is on track to install record amounts of clean energy this year and 
next as developers race to claim expiring federal tax credits and as soaring 
data-center electricity demand stimulates growth.

But the same data-center boom is also driving a major expansion of natural 
gas, undercutting the climate benefits of the renewables buildout.
Racking systems to hold solar panels at the site of a solar farm under 
construction in Portage, Pennsylvania.Photographer: Justin Merriman/Bloomberg
The findings are part of BloombergNEF’s latest US Clean Energy Market Outlook, 
released Friday

“When we look at the picture holistically, we still see data center growth as 
a tailwind to renewable energy, but it is tainted by the growing gas pipeline,” 
said Hall Wellborn, an author of the report.

BNEF expects the US to add a record 89 gigawatts of clean energy this year and 
to surpass that level in 2027. The surge is coming even as developers contend 
with more expensive financing, trade barriers and uncertainty over equipment 
costs and availability.

At the same time, 67.8 gigawatts of new gas-fired power could be added through 
2030, according to BNEF. With just 14.3 gigawatts of gas generation slated to 
close over that period, the pipeline would amount to a substantial net increase.

The rapid growth of artificial intelligence and data centers is increasingly 
shaping US energy forecasts. BNEF now expects installed data-center capacity to 
reach 118 gigawatts in 2030, which is 52% higher than its projections made only 
last December. Average data-center power demand could reach 120 gigawatts by 
2035, roughly five times current levels, with AI-related computing responsible 
for nearly half.

A significant portion of new data centers will be powered by on-site natural 
gas systems. While the US is the world’s largest supplier of the fossil fuel, 
all that demand is going to tax domestic supply, and BNEF expects the industry 
to swing from surplus production to a deficit as soon as 2028.

Yet the boom is also improving the outlook for clean-energy projects. BNEF 
revised up by 15% its forecast through 2036 for onshore wind additions, to 99.7 
gigawatts, partly in light of data-center demand.

A power bill divide

By Michelle Ma 
<https://bloomberg.com/authors/AWTYlfwjEUk/michelle-ma?cmpid=BBD101626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10180>
 andNadja Popovich 
<https://bloomberg.com/authors/AXGJc4xBk9o/nadja-popovich?cmpid=BBD101626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10180>

Eleanor Beshai, 70, keeps the air conditioning off as long as she can on hot 
days and uses only the lights she needs. Monthly electric bills for her 
Worcester, Massachusetts, home still sometimes top $500.

Less than 2,000 feet away, across narrow Lake Quinsigamond, Dave Katz, 71, is 
less concerned about his power bill. It averages about $130 a month, he said, 
even though his home — in the neighboring town of Shrewsbury — is slightly 
larger than Beshai’s. In hot summer months, when the AC is running high, it 
reaches $150 to $160.

“I pay attention to my bill,” Katz said. “But do I worry about it? No. It’s 
less than streaming TV.”
Illustration: Cassandra Klos and Nadja Popovich /Bloomberg
The two neighbors weather the same storms, heat waves and cold snaps and live 
so close that each could probably spot the other’s house if they knew where to 
look. But they get their electricity through very different providers. Beshai 
is served byNational Grid Plc 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/NG/:LN?cmpid=BBD101626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10180>
 — a large, publicly traded utility — while Katz buys his electricity from 
Shrewsbury Electric and Cable Operations, owned and operated by the 
municipality.

Shrewsbury’s average residential electricity price was under 16 cents per 
kilowatt-hour last year, one of the lowest power prices in the state, according 
to the US Energy Information Administration. National Grid’s average price for 
comparable service, which includes both electric supply and delivery, is more 
than twice as high: 34 cents per kilowatt-hour. The gap has widened in recent 
years.

Beshai electric bill.Photographer: Cassandra Klos/Bloomberg
That growing divide extends across much of the country. The two-thirds of US 
households who get their electricity from big, investor-owned utilities have 
long paid more, on average, than customers of the nation’s public power 
agencies. In recent years, they’ve seen prices rise faster too: 34% since 2020, 
compared with 19% for customers of publicly owned utilities, according to a 
Bloomberg News analysis ofEIA data 
<https://www.eia.gov/electricity/data/eia861/>.

Unaffordable electric bills have become one of the most potent political issues
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2026-power-bills-midterm-election-issue/?cmpid=BBD101626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10180>
 in this year’s midterm congressional elections. And while many politicians are 
railing against the power-hungry data centers their voters blame for high 
prices, some are focusing on the big electric utilities themselves — 
questioning how well their business model still works.

Biodiversity risks

By Frances Schwartzkopff 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AOY6BUObWPw/frances-schwartzkopff?cmpid=BBD101626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10180>

Slow progress on preventing irreversible damage to the world’s natural 
ecosystems is threatening people and their livelihoods, according to a fresh 
United Nations-sponsored report on the state of biodiversity.

“Safeguarding biological diversity is essential to the health of our planet, 
the resilience of our economies and the well-being of all people,” UN 
Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said in the first formal review of the 
progress made so far in meeting the 2030 targets of the Kunming-Montreal Global 
Biodiversity Framework. He said urgent actions are needed.

The landmark nature pact, signed by close to 200 countries, set 23 medium-term 
goals including stopping species extinction and making urban areas more 
livable. But gains have been limited since the framework was adopted in 2022, 
as inflation, defense and artificial intelligence consume policymakers’ agendas.

The State of Biodiversity Action report comes as governments gather in the 
Armenian capital of Yerevan for the 17th United Nations Biodiversity 
Conference, from Oct. 19-30. A key goal of COP17 will be hammering out detailed 
plans for addressing the gaps identified in the report.
