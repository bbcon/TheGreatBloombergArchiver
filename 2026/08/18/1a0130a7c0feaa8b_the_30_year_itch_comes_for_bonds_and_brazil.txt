The 30-Year Itch

The summer doldrums continue. So does the inexorable rise in the longest-term 
government bond yields. Monday saw the yield on the 30-year US Treasury top 
5.3% for the first time since the eve of the Global Financial Crisis in 2007, 
and that’s in line with global experience; Japanese 30-year yields have risen 
above 4% for the first time in their 27-year history, while equivalent UK gilts 
yield their highest since 1998:

Generally, rising long bond yields signal concern that growth will drive 
inflation and require higher interest rates further down the line. Some recent 
strong regional manufacturing surveys in the US might just support such a 
narrative. It’s hard to attribute all of this move to inflation anxiety, 
however, as bond market inflation forecasts have remained low and stable.

Anshul Pradhan of Barclays argues that rather than inflation expectations, 
“three factors are at play: the budget deficit outlook, AI-related corporate 
issuance, and the changing Treasury buyer base.” With companies issuing huge 
amounts of debt to fund AI capex, long Treasuries have a new competitor that 
might force them to offer a stronger yield, while the growing budget deficit 
never goes away as an issue.

Steadily rising yields create an issue for asset allocators as bonds become 
less effective diversifiers in bad times for stocks. Since the worst of the 
pandemic, buying US stocks relative to Treasuries — proxied in the chart by the 
ratio between the main ETFs tracking the asset classes — has handily 
outperformed the S&P 500 itself, while a classic 60:40 portfolio (60% stocks 
and 40% bonds) has lagged badly:

This is most obviously bad news for the mortgage market. Higher 30-year yields 
obstruct hopes to bring down fixed mortgage rates. If it benefits anyone, 
Alexander Altmann of Barclays Equity Technical Strategies argues that it’s 
stocks with strong balance sheets that don’t need to do much borrowing, and 
which have been relatively out of favor:
If yields keep going up due to fiscal concerns, then investors will want to 
find some comfort in strong balance sheet businesses, especially when long end 
real yields are threatening the peak GFC levels. If yields go down as a 
function of a growth scare, then you’re shifting to a more defensive posture 
anyway.
Almost by definition, the long bond market moves slowly, but shifts of the 
tectonic plates can have a serious effect. If there’s any risk of a major quake 
to disturb the current calm, this is where it comes from.

Lots of Caffeine in Brazil

Speaking of a 30-year itch... It looks ever more likely that Brazil is going 
to give its 80-year-old president, Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, a fourth term in 
October’s election. That would be a remarkable feat of political longevity for 
a seven-time candidate who first ran for the office in 1989, particularly after 
spending nearly two years in prison for corruption between his second and third 
terms. Win or lose this time, he’s been an unescapable force for more than 
three decades. And investors don’t seem comfortable with the prospect.

Lula’s improving odds in polls and prediction markets have coincided with 
downward pressure on Brazil’s stock market in recent weeks:

To be clear, Lula hasn’t always been unpopular with investors. His first 
election in 2002, at his fourth attempt, was greeted with an epic selloff, and 
the country was already reeling from its financial crisis in 1999. When Lula 
proved far more pragmatic in office than expected, stocks began to take off. 
Then Brazil’s commodity exports benefited to the full from the rise of China. 
The Bovespa index rose almost 2,000% in dollar terms in the six years from 
Lula’s victory to the onset of the GFC, giving the man a lasting place in 
market history as the progenitor of an all-time great buying opportunity:

If there’ s one single reason why it’s likely to be different this time, it 
comes from fiscal policy. Lula inherited a government debt-to-GDP ratio that 
was considerably lower than it was now, and then did a good job of keeping it 
under control throughout his first two terms. Current projections, as 
illustrated here by Societe Generale SA, suggest that debt will keep rising and 
top 100% of gross domestic product:
Societe Generale SA
Worries about fiscal policy have found expression in higher bond yields, which 
until recently have not cramped the style of the stock market:

It’s not at all clear how long this can continue. Felix-Antoine Vezina-Poirier 
of BCA Research argues:
The near-term risk is that a Lula win dashes expectations of meaningful fiscal 
tightening. A weaker currency should force the central bank to reverse its 
easing, and higher borrowing costs point to a notable growth slowdown in 2027. 
Brazilian equities have decoupled from rising local yields since early this 
year, a gap our colleagues expect to close through lower share prices.
It would also be unwise to assume that the stock market has already taken 
evasive action to guard against a Lula victory. Investors did this to an 
excessive degree 24 years ago, and turned his win into a buying opportunity — 
but such a response this time looks unlikely. The Bovespa hit an all-time high 
as recently as April, and is still up 3.5% for the year. By contrast, the index 
had dropped 56% in the 30 months leading up to his first election win.

Dirk Willer of Citi pointed to market reactions in Hungary and Colombia 
earlier this year, where widely anticipated victories still led to strong 
market reactions to show that “even high prediction market implied likelihoods 
does not mean the market will not react.” The difference with Brazil, compared 
to those two countries, is that “the less market-friendly candidate is the 
favorite.”

That implies there is further to fall — although multiples of forecast 
earnings (shown on a rolling basis for the next two years) are already back 
near their lows from the GFC:

It’s possible, of course, that the market is overdoing its Lula-angst again. 
If so, there’s a chance of a nice buying opportunity if his opponent, Flavio 
Bolsonaro, pulls out a victory.

Indian Doldrums

For the financial embodiment of fears that artificial intelligence will 
disrupt time-tested business models, look no further than India’s stock market. 
Once a darling in emerging markets, Indian equities have underperformed peers 
by nearly 30% this year. The drag is even more pronounced in the MSCI India 
Infotech index of technology stocks, down by as much as 50% from its peak two 
years ago. The malaise is reflected in the Nifty, currently on a five-day 
losing streak and down by about 7% year-to-date:

To put this in context, between 2021 and 2025, the Nifty outperformed EM by 
more than 80%. India has retained much of those gains, but the tide has turned 
strongly against it in 2026. To find a reason for this lackluster performance, 
the so-called SaaSpocalypse <https://saasocalypse.com/saaspocalypse> — the 
sharp selloff in software stocks triggered by concerns that AI could disrupt 
the traditional Software as a Service (SaaS) business model — offered a 
convenient explanation. But with UBS’ measure of SaaS companies in the US 
having surged from recent lows to near-record highs, that explanation is 
becoming harder to sustain:

A more idiosyncratic challenge may be at play. India’s technology sector, 
built on a labor-intensive outsourcing model, may be facing a world where AI 
lets clients do more with fewer engineers. Employee headcount growth at two of 
the biggest technology-services companies, Infosys and Wipro, has slowed 
sharply since ChatGPT’s launch in 2022. As AI transforms software development’s 
efficiency, it’s plausible the traditional model of adding workers to deliver 
incremental growth is ending:

The energy crisis caused by the Iran war is also a contributing factor. With 
more than 80% of India’s oil imported, a price surge weighs on thecurrent 
account 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/india-s-july-trade-gap-widens-as-imports-rise-on-robust-demand?cmpid=BBD081826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7811>
, the rupee, corporate margins, and consumer demand. Crude oil prices are still 
hovering around $90 per barrel, and remain more than 30% above pre-conflict 
levels. This shows up in slowing economic activity, with July’s HSBCpurchasing 
managers index 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-23/india-s-economic-activity-slowed-in-june-flash-pmi-shows?cmpid=BBD081826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7811>
 falling to 54.3, predominantly due to services:

Last month, inflation climbed to 4.45%, a 19-month high, above the Reserve 
Bank of India’s 4% target for a second month. Persistent inflation outside of 
the central bank’s comfort zone strengthens the case for a rate hike. Bloomberg 
Intelligence’s Kumar Gautam argues that Indian equities have the longest 
duration among emerging markets, making them unusually sensitive to changes in 
discount rates. For now, while a tightening isn’t the base case, even the risk 
of one disproportionately hurts equities. That makes a return to a 
disinflationary path particularly important:

A weaker rupee 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-14/rupee-swings-fizzle-out-as-india-s-central-bank-tightens-grip?cmpid=BBD081826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7811>
 is also part of the problem. The currency has depreciated by about 7% this 
year, with bouts of equity selling coinciding with macro pressures as higher 
oil prices pushed up current account deficits:

As pronounced as the downturn has been, Indian equities haven’t fallen off a 
cliff. They’ve preserved the bulk of their gains from recent years and, as 
Capital Economics’ Megan Fisher points out, valuations no longer look too high 
after several years when India had by far the most expensive stocks in EM. 
Rising valuations elsewhere have narrowed India’s premium:

Over the next 18 months, Fisher predicts that India’s relatively low exposure 
to AI stocks could help a turnaround. In the event of a tech-led broad bear 
market next year, which Capital Economics anticipates, India stands to be a 
relative winner: Fisher projects the MSCI India to fall 5% next year, compared 
to an 18% fall for the broader emerging markets index.

A recovery remains far from assured. But it’s possible that just holding on to 
gains from recent years will come to look like a good outcome.

—Richard Abbey

Survival Tips

From Brazil and India to Mexico. I’d like to celebrate the recovery in popular 
culture of my old home, Mexico City. Because it’s so big and dirty, it’s 
generally never had a great or glamorous reputation (the Denzel Washington movie
Man on Fire <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ALKCTuH79iE> probably captured the 
way people think of it). But somehow, it’s steadily captured the world’s 
imagination. Theopening scene of the Bond movie Spectre 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lR9Mg2y-87M> is a great example. So is the 
growing fascination with everything to do withFrida Kahlo 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dlrqxAgdY58>. And even pop videos are set 
there now — try U2’s new videoStreet of Dreams 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hRnS0Wf64Is>, Coldplay’s Headful of Dreams 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vGZMvV9KBp8> or Westlife’s “Fool Again” 
(totally insipid song;beautiful video 
<https://www.tiktok.com/@teamwestlifeworld/video/7622018921394179350>). The 
place is a feast for the senses, and that doesn’t even include the cooking.
