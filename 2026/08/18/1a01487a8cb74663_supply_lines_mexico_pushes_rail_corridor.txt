Ships idling outside the Panama Canal — and balking at multimillion-dollar 
auction fees 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-14/panama-canal-traffic-spurs-4-6-million-payment-for-a-crossing?cmpid=BBD081826_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7740>
 for skipping the line — might want to listen to Mexico’s pitch and try a 
shortcut farther north.

Since 2019, Mexico has spent at least $4 billion reviving a “dry canal” 
linking the ports of Coatzacoalcos on the Gulf of Mexico with Salina Cruz on 
the Pacific.

The investment has put more than 500 miles of railway across the broader 
network back into service, upgraded bridges and ports, and secured land for 
industrial development. (Click here 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-08-15/as-global-waterways-face-squeeze-mexico-pitches-ciit-as-alternative-corridor?cmpid=BBD081826_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7740>
 for the full story on the Interoceanic Corridor of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, 
or CIIT.)

For shippers, the benefits include time saved and optionality. Salina Cruz is 
less than four days from Los Angeles and about 15 days from Shanghai — compared 
with as many as seven days and about 20 days, respectively, from Panama. 
Coatzacoalcos is about 36 hours from Houston and Mobile, Alabama, and around 
two weeks from Rotterdam and Marseille.

Mexican officials say cargo can cover the corridor’s 190-mile main line in 
seven hours, with the full port-to-port transfer completed in as little as 24.

The shortcut isn’t new. A railway opened in 1907, and US freight companies 
used it to move Hawaiian sugar across Mexico before sending it on to 
Philadelphia. Revolution disrupted the route, and the opening of the Panama 
Canal soon pushed it aside.

Now Mexico wants a comeback, but cargo alone won’t deliver it. Even its 
optimistic target of 1.4 million containers by 2035 would be a fraction of 
Panama’s annual traffic.

The government’s bigger bet is creating cargo at the source, but that’s a 
struggle, too. Investment in new factories along the route isn’t pouring in as 
the area lacks high-capacity power and other services in abundance.

The opening is historic, the shortcut is real and, after more than a century 
of trying, the opportunity is Mexico’s to lose.
