Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Jess Shankleman looks at the implications of the government’s approval 
of a new gas field in the UK.

In Aesop’s Fables, the jackdaw has a habit of overestimating its importance. A 
similar situation is playing out for a gas field that shares the name in the 
UK’s North Sea.

For some people, not least US President Donald Trump 
<https://truthsocial.com/@realDonaldTrump/posts/116947019736199780>, its 
approval could herald the resurgence of drilling in the region. But while 
there’s no doubt the field has become a political totem, it will have a 
negligible impact on Britain’s energy and climate policy.

Jackdaw was given the go-ahead 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/uk-approves-jackdaw-field-sees-gas-output-as-soon-as-this-month?cmpid=BBBXT102126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10359>
 today by Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh, following a long legal battle 
over whether its carbon emissions were incompatible with the nation’s climate 
targets.

It’s a relatively small project, producing about 10 billion cubic meters of 
gas over its lifetime, equivalent to roughly 2% of the UK’s projected imports 
between 2025 and 2050.

Even its proponents acknowledge it would have little impact on natural gas 
prices, which are currently nearfour-year highs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-20/european-gas-prices-rise-above-90-for-first-time-since-2022?cmpid=BBBXT102126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10359>
 in Europe because of the Iran war.

Neil McCulloch, chief executive officer of Adura, Jackdaw’s operator, told BBC 
News <https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/live/cw8rzgvlmjznt> he is keen to do more 
exploration in the North Sea. But there’s little reason to think that any 
transformative discoveries remain in a basin that’s been drilled intensively 
since the 1960s.

While Norway has had some success in continuing to explore its portion of the 
North Sea — helping to replace Europe’s imports of Russian gas — the British 
part is 90% depleted for commercial resources, according to consultant Wood 
Mackenzie Ltd.
Miatta Fahnbulleh, UK energy secretary, speaks at the Labour Party annual 
conference in Liverpool on Monday, Sept. 28.Photographer: Chris 
Ratcliffe/Bloomberg
Jackdaw did come to represent a much bigger battle over the direction of UK 
climate policy, but in this regard its relevance may also have been overstated.

Fahnbulleh has insisted the ruling Labour Party remains committed to its 
pledge to ban new oil and gas licenses. Jackdaw only squeezed through because 
it was actually approved under the previous Conservative government.

It only became a question for the current government when the field’s 
developmentconsent was quashed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-01-30/uk-oil-ruling-sets-up-growth-versus-climate-test-for-government?cmpid=BBBXT102126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10359>
 last year by a court in Edinburgh, which ruled that its carbon emissions 
hadn’t been properly considered during the original approvals process.

The only other North Sea field in the same situation is Rosebank, also 
operated by Adura.

In that respect, no matter how many times Trump raises the matter in phone 
calls with UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham, Jackdaw’s approval does little to 
lure back oil and gas companies that already left the North Sea for better 
opportunities elsewhere.

—Jess Shankleman, Bloomberg News
