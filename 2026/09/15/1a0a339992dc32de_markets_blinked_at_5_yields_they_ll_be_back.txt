Blinking at 5%

For weeks, there have been fears that a 5% yield on Treasuries, the most 
important number in global finance, could be a “tipping point.” Once the 
psychological level was breached, the worry was that selling would take hold 
and yields would surge higher.

The tipping point <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RmXrwKydM9k>, made famous 
by a book by Malcolm Gladwell, is an infuriating concept, because it’s 
impossible to know in advance what point will cause a market to tip. But in 
this case, it looks like another Gladwell tome might be more relevant:Blin 
<https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/40102.Blink>k 
<https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/40102.Blink>. Traders went through 5%, 
saw what they had done, and in the blink of an eye took it back down again. The 
subtitle ofBlink is The Power of Thinking Without Thinking 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FeegLvAkbY0>, which seems about right; in this 
case, we witnessedtrading without thinking 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8H-FiCIMh20>. At least until we hear from the 
Federal Reserve on Wednesday, there isn’t the appetite to take the cost of 
long-term money above 5%:

We’ve established that a preponderance of people still think a guaranteed 5% 
yield from a very safe investment is a good deal. There’s room for argument 
about why yields have risen as far as they have, but it’s plain that shifting 
expectations are more important than anything else. That turnabout has happened 
steadily, and it’s easy to lose sight of it.

Twelve months ago when the Fed, still under Jerome Powell’s chairmanship, cut 
the federal funds rate down to 4%, the assumption was priced that it would drop 
to 3% or below by the summer of 2027. Since then, the rate itself has reduced 
to 3.5% — but futures now imply a return to 4.5% by next summer:

The war in Iran is of course the single biggest driver of this. A major energy 
shock automatically made it harder to cut rates, while the surprising 
intensification of the conflict in recent days has brought crude prices back 
above $100 per barrel. But there’s more to it than that, and new Fed Chair 
Kevin Warsh’s speech at Jackson Hole managed to push up the expected terminal 
rate for federal funds by a full half-percentage point. Exclude energy, plainly 
driven by forces beyond the Fed’s control, and inflation was 2.5% last month, 
as low as it’s been since early in 2021.

Briefly, once the Fed embarks on tightening, it generally continues for a 
while, and yields continue to rise, generally by more than a percentage point. 
Judging by history, as surveyed byDeutsche Bank AG’s resident financial 
historian Jim Reid 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2019-09-24/bonds-face-decades-of-pain-if-history-of-debt-is-any-guide-k0xbbl2y?cmpid=BBD091526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8898>
, we should brace for the 10-year yield to reach 6% before the Fed is done:

If this is a real tightening cycle, as markets believe, Monday’s bounce from 
5% will be thoroughly overcome in the fullness of time. As Reid shows, there’s 
plenty of historical precedent for yields to rise this much, and this selloff 
does not seem to be driven by worries about Uncle Sam’s ability to pay. Growth 
in nominal gross domestic product (it’s more usually cited in real terms, 
taking out inflation) is a strong driver of 10-year yields, as this chart from 
Michael Howell of Global Liquidity Indexes shows:

Although it doesn’t feel like it, nominal gross domestic product is growing 
about as fast as it ever has since the 1980s, once the extreme distortions 
caused by the pandemic shutdowns are excluded:

So we had better assume that this is the start of a tightening cycle, and not 
just the removal of one of last year’s “insurance” cuts when the labor market 
briefly seemed to be drifting into trouble. What should we then expect of 
stocks?

The answer is less negative than might be thought, because most tightening 
cycles happen when nominal GDP is rising — good conditions for share prices. 
But rate hikes would clearly imply that the US stock market should be flat at 
best for the rest of this year.

This chart from LPL Financial looks at S&P 500 performance after the start of 
the last six Fed hiking cycles. The March 1997 round, in yellow, is an outlier 
as on that occasion the Fed, in the wake of Alan Greenspan’s “irrational 
exuberance” speech, decided to hike only once — the sole time that’s happened 
since the fed funds rate was adopted as the main policy instrument in the early 
1990s. If Warsh were to announce that this week’s hike will also be “one and 
done,” that would imply big stock returns ahead (as well as major ructions for 
bonds), but it’s probably wiser to look to the other cycles:

The overall message is reassuring. A year into a hiking cycle, the stock 
market is almost invariably up, with 2022 — when the Fed started a long way 
behind the curve — the only exception. The reason is that a central bank 
generally doesn’t hike unless it needs to. It’s during rate-cutting, usually as 
the economy is tipping into a recession, when the market damage can be done.

That said, there’s always the risk of a policy error. And the levels of 
government indebtedness, after more than a decade of being able to borrow at 
rock-bottom rates, are higher than in previous incidents. A rise in short order 
to 6% — which is quite plausible — would put that under strain, while also 
forcing traders to confront yields that hadn’t been seen this century:

This won’t be the last test of 5%. There’s nothing inherently unhealthy in 
yields going considerably higher while growth is strong, but markets will need 
to see that to be convinced.

AI Slowdown Trade

Demands for guardrails around artificial intelligence aren’t new. Within a 
year of ChatGPT’s debut, major techchief executives warned 
<https://www.cnn.com/2023/09/13/tech/schumer-tech-companies-ai-regulations/index.html>
 of the need for greater regulation. Events since that meeting with US Senate 
Minority Leader Chuck Schumer only reinforce the case, even as money pours into 
AI.

That incident was dismissed as an abundance of caution. But the market can no 
longer brush aside CEOs’ concerns about the potentially apocalyptic effects of 
frontier large language models (LLMs). In recent months,Anthropic PBC 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-04-16/how-anthropic-discovered-mythos-ai-was-too-dangerous-for-release?cmpid=BBD091526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8898>
 andOpenAI 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/openai-says-its-ai-used-for-unprecedented-hugging-face-breach?cmpid=BBD091526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8898>
 have both reported that AI models they’re training have broken free from 
controlled test environments and gone rogue. The latest global selloff in 
chipmakers suggests that investors are at last taking notice:

But this increasingly looks like an ideological or cultural battle. President 
Donald Trump responded to the calls for a slowdown byAnthropic CEO Dario Amodei 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-09-13/amodei-essay-anthropic-ai-apocalypse-warning-is-far-too-weak?cmpid=BBD091526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8898>
 by belittling such concerns as“a SICK conspiracy.” 
<https://truthsocial.com/@realDonaldTrump/posts/117269745153543631> With the 
S&P 500 down 2.4% from its all-time high a month ago, however, investors appear 
to be taking this seriously — even if there is no great market alarm.

There would be winners. Monday’s 3.2% rally for the S&P 1500 software index 
showed that some investors believe a slowdown in AI development could be a 
lifeline for companies most threatened by it. Fears that AI would upend 
software business models have given rise to theSaaSpocalypse 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-09-02/the-saaspocalypse-has-a-remarkable-number-of-survivors?cmpid=BBD091526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8898>
 phenomenon:

Beyond Trump’s pushback, the broader question is whether these companies can 
reach a consensus on their own. Makers of frontier models appear to be caught 
in aprisoner’s dilemma 
<https://heritage.umich.edu/stories/the-prisoners-dilemma/>, the famous 
game-theory thought experiment showing that two rational actors may fail to 
cooperate even when doing so would be in their mutual interest. None wants to 
slow down if the others keep going; all prefer to decelerate, if someone can 
ensure that there areno free-riders 
<https://www.investopedia.com/terms/f/free_rider_problem.asp>.

Then there are geopolitics, on which Trump prefers 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TLCYOJKK3NY8?cmpid=BBD091526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8898>
 to focus. Any pause thatdoes not involve China 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TLCSWVRKV2W1?cmpid=BBD091526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8898>
 would risk ceding America’s advantage, giving AI skeptics a high bar to clear. 
Noah Smith, who writes theNoahpinion <https://substack.com/@noahpinion> 
newsletter, argues that coordinated restraint could also hurt the incumbents’ 
bottom lines by giving upstarts a chance to catch up. Bank of America’s Justin 
Post notes that such concerns have so far damaged sentiment about AI, notably 
for the hyperscalers.

Much of the more than $200 billion revenue backlog at Alphabet Inc. and 
Amazon.com Inc. is tied to spending by the leading AI labs, which are 
themselves burning cash they don’t yet have. A slower pace of training for the 
models could delay revenues even further. Post further notes that slower 
development of proprietary models could allow open models — mostly from China — 
to narrow the gap. That would put even more pressure on hyperscalers’ margins 
and infrastructure spending.

Thus a slowdown could easily undermine the technology’s lofty promise even 
before it’s had a chance to mature, which makes it all the more significant 
that the major players plainly want it. To quote Smith, “The fact that they’re 
still calling for a slowdown, in defiance of their own financial interests, is 
a clear sign that their worry about human extinction is sincere.”

A deceleration in AI can have devastating consequences elsewhere. BNY’s 
Geoffrey Yu notes that for many economies, AI is one of the few investments to 
make a genuine contribution to growth. Any sudden slowdown, without obvious 
substitutes, could be felt well beyond the sector.

If there’s a silver lining, it concerns inflation pressures — which will 
likely prod the Fed into hiking this week. The AI complex adds to supply 
constraints and inflation pressures, while its borrowing adds to the upward 
push on Treasury yields. For these concerns, an AI deceleration might 
ultimately prove welcome. But Yu cautions that engineering a fall in inflation 
without triggering a hard landing will be difficult adjustment. The apocalypse 
may not be at hand, but it’s a difficult situation.

—Richard Abbey
