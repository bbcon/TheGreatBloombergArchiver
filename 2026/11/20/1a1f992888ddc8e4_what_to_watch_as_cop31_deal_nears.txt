What to watch in the COP31 endgame

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD112026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11679>

<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD112026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11679>
 andAzman Usmani 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AYPlpafOt_4/azman-usmani?cmpid=BBD112026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11679>

International climate talks are heading into their final hours, with 
negotiators from nearly 200 countries scrapping over financial support for 
developing countries and how to assess progress against rising global 
temperatures.
Activists at the COP31 Climate Conference in Antalya, Turkey.Photographer: 
Sean Gallup/Getty Images via Getty Images Europe
Delegates arriving for the COP31 summit in the resort city of Antalya, Turkey, 
were confronted with a long to-do list that set aside some 165 items for formal 
negotiation. Turkey, which holds the COP31 presidency, also added its own 
priorities, including a bid to encourage the world to use more electricity.

Here are four key issues to watch as negotiators keep haggling: 

Fossil fuels
The world’s consumption of oil, gas and coal is the key driver of climate 
change — and countries signed on to the Paris Agreement already committed in 
2023 to transition their energy systems away from fossil fuels. Yet it’s 
unclear this year’s summit in Turkey will explicitly name “fossil fuels” in a 
final declaration. There may not even be new acknowledgement of the roadmaps 
meant to outline shifts away from the energy sources that were championed by 
the Brazilian hosts of last year’s COP30.

There’s also a pitched fight over how much the conference will embrace some 
oil industry priorities. A draft text circulated Thursday contained a pledge to 
accelerate the use of carbon capture and storage technology, which critics see 
as a bid to prolong the world’s reliance on fossil fuels. Explicit references 
to curbing planet-warming methane pollution from the oil and gas sector were 
also abandoned in favor of more general language.

Finance
Finance is the linchpin of the annual UN-backed climate conferences, with 
long-running disputes over who pays, how much and on what terms at the very 
heart of the negotiations.

Developing countries being asked to shun fossil fuels say they need financial 
support from wealthier nations — which grew their economies by burning oil, gas 
and coal — to deploy emission-free alternatives and contend with the 
consequences of climate change. And the failure of rich countries to deliver 
quickly, or fully, on past financial promises has eroded trust on the issue.

At COP31, a key debate has been whether the world can agree on an explicit 
baseline level to benchmark an existing, one-year-old commitment to triple 
adaptation funding by 2035. Developing nations who’ve pushed for a $120 billion 
annual threshold have met resistance from the European Union and other blocs. A 
potential compromise would be to have no immediate resolution at all, and 
instead punt a decision on the baseline to next year’s summit in Ethiopia.

Science
A dispute over the timing of upcoming reports from the Intergovernmental Panel 
on Climate Change has spilled over into the COP31 negotiating rooms.

The three reports — set to cover climate science, impacts and mitigation — are 
seen by some nations as essential to inform a second “global stocktake” 
assessing the world’s progress toward Paris Agreement goals.

Kenya, India, Saudi Arabia and other countries have pushed back against 
language that would encourage the reports to be delivered in time for the 
culmination of the stocktake in 2028. Concerns have been raised about 
completing the work on a compressed timetable and the dominance of scientists 
from wealthy nations in developing the reports.

While potential compromise language with a less explicit timetable was floated 
in Thursday’s draft text, it’s not yet gone far enough for some nations.

“This process must be anchored by the best available science,” the Alliance of 
Small Island States coalition said in a statement Friday. “We must also see a 
clear call for the delivery of all working group reports” in time for the 
stocktake.

Electrification
Turkey already won support from more than 75 countries for a voluntary 
campaign to boost electricity’s share of global final energy demand to 35% by 
2035, from just over 20% currently. But the country has also prioritized 
getting some iteration of that goal in the final COP31 declaration, a process 
that’s required horse trading with resistant nations.

A bloc of developing countries, including India, had been wary of the 
approach, arguing against enshrining any new top-down targets in a formal 
decision. And it may be bound up with other contentious issues: language in 
support of carbon capture added to Thursday’s draft text was a bid to lure 
support from oil-producing nations for the electrification target, according to 
a person familiar with the matter, who requested anonymity to discuss private 
talks.

The issue now is less about whether electrification language survives in a 
final decision but how it is included, especially if it enumerates a specific 
2035 percentage target.

A ‘conference of petroleum’

By John Ainger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD112026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11679>
 andJennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD112026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11679>

Global climate talks entered their final hours with nations in Europe, Central 
America and the Pacific criticizing a proposed agreement’s failure to address 
fossil fuels, and accusing the oil and gas sector of swaying negotiations in 
the industry’s favor.

A draft deal presented to delegates from nearly 200 countries omitted any 
reference to fossil fuels,the largest contributor to global warming 
<https://www.un.org/en/climatechange/science/causes-effects-climate-change>, 
and pledged support for carbon capture technology, a climate tool favored by 
energy producers.

“There are some who don’t want us to use the word fossil fuels at this COP,” 
Germany’s Environment Minister Carsten Schneider told a meeting Friday at the 
United Nations COP31 summit in Antalya, Turkey. “There are some who make a lot 
of money with that.”

The European Union has concerns that the draft text published Thursday casts 
the two-week-long meeting as a fossil fuel COP and sees a risk of talks ending 
without any agreed deal if there aren’t substantial changes, said a senior 
official for the bloc, speaking on condition of anonymity to discuss private 
deliberations.
COP31 President Murat KurumPhotographer: Chris McGrath/Getty Images via Getty 
Images Europe
COP31 president Murat Kurum, Turkey’s environment, urbanization and climate 
change minister, along with other officials, insist the text doesn’t require a 
specific acknowledgement on the need to transition away from fossil fuels — as 
that ambition was agreed to by nations at the 2023 talks held in Dubai and 
still stands.

Envoys and government officials, including Saudi Arabia

’s Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman, were continuing to wrangle over 
the summit’s potential outcomes Friday, with discussions expected to continue 
into the weekend.

JPMorgan’s El Niño warning

By Leslie Kaufman 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUZ8izNmriE/leslie-kaufman?cmpid=BBD112026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11679>
 andEric Roston 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQdgGkZhizQ/eric-roston?cmpid=BBD112026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11679>

The current El Niño could keep pushing up food prices and straining water 
supplies well into 2027, even after the Pacific Ocean warming that drives it 
begins to fade, according to a new JPMorgan Chase & Co. analysis provided to 
Bloomberg News.

The problem isn’t simply El Niño itself. The natural warming of the tropical 
Pacific is occurring on top of long-term climate change and colliding with 
drought, wars and other disruptions to food and commodity markets.

“It’s going to be inflationary,” said Sarah Kapnick, global head of JPMorgan’s 
climate advisory, and it could produce a longer tail of impacts on agriculture 
and commodities than historical experience would suggest.

El Niño typically develops in spring, peaks during late fall or winter and 
dissipates the following spring or summer. But the heat it releases into the 
atmosphere takes longer to disappear.

Think of the ocean as a giant heat battery, said Kapnick, a former chief 
scientist of the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Even if 
Pacific Ocean temperatures shift from El Niño to cooler La Niña conditions by 
June or July, the heat may linger until fall or later. The second year of an El 
Niño is typically when the planet experiences the most heat, she said.
