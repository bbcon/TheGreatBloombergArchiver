With Senator Susan Collins facing a tough re-election campaign in Maine, the 
presidentdecided on Friday 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-23/trump-to-lower-some-canada-tariffs-maine-senator-collins-says?cmpid=BBD102626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10485>
 that lifting tariffs on certain types of plywood, paper and machinery from 
Canada might help her case. The political message was clear even if the White 
Housefact sheet 
<https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/10/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-further-modifies-canadian-products-subject-to-tariffs-under-section-338/>
 insisted this was just an effort to “refine the scope” of his trade war with 
Canada.

On Sunday the president went further, declaring in Michigan that what he’d 
once called his favorite word had slipped in the rankings. “I was just 
decimated by the press,” Trump said. “I said tariffs is my favorite word in the 
dictionary. Tariffs. And the press the next day just decimated me because they 
said, ‘What about God? What about wife, family, relationship? What about all 
these other words?’ So now I made tariff my fifth favorite word.”

You don’t have to look far for an explanation. Voters have been making clear 
in polls for months now that they don’t like tariffs and that they associate 
them with higher prices they’d rather not pay. A Fox News poll taken Oct. 9-12 
found 69% of registeredvoters disapproved 
<https://static.foxnews.com/foxnews.com/content/uploads/2026/10/fox_october-9-12-2026_complete_national_topline_october-14-release.pdf>
 of Trump’s tariffs, which was worse than the president’s own dismal rating in 
that poll. In a September Ipsos survey, 81% of respondents said they believed 
theduties led to higher prices 
<https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/most-americans-now-understand-how-tariffs-work-more-see-costs-fewer-see-benefits>
, while just 24% said they would yield more US jobs.

The result is that Republican candidates around the country are facing heat 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-15/trump-says-cornyn-rove-to-blame-if-paxton-loses-senate-race?cmpid=BBD102626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10485>
 over their support for Trump’s trade policies. Which is a factor in once 
reliably red farm states such as Iowa and Kansas, and battleground states like 
Michigan and Maine.

The Tariff Salesman

That’s given US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer a new remit. No longer is 
the laconic trade lawyer just the behind-the-scenes architect of Trump’s tariff 
policies. He’s increasingly becoming the man the White House is hoping can sell 
the country on the tariffs.

It’s clear Trump thinks that job is important. As my colleague Alicia Diaz 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVmRlguxhlo/alicia-diaz?cmpid=BBD102626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10485>
 and I write in anew Bloomberg Businessweek profile 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/jamieson-greer-on-being-tasked-with-rebuilding-trump-s-unpopular-tariff-wall?cmpid=BBD102626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10485>
, during this past April’s Easter egg roll, Trump declared Greer to be “the 
most powerful man in government.” Though he also added a recognition of Greer’s 
relative anonymity outside Washington trade circles: “Have you heard of him?”

Greer’s sales strategy for now, as he made clear in two separate interviews 
with us, is to argue that the long-term payoff of tariffs will be worth it and 
that the short-term pain is overdone.

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-24/volkswagen-plans-new-audi-plant-in-georgia-handing-trump-a-win?cmpid=BBD102626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10485>

“We’re trying to create another American century here,” Greer told us. What he 
called “discontent in the populace” is really over “micro issues” unrelated to 
tariffs, he argues.

“When people are saying, ‘I’m upset about the cost of living,’ what they’re 
upset about is housing. They’re upset about healthcare, upset about education. 
They’re upset about energy. None of that has anything to do with tariffs.”

The problem Greer faces, of course, is that the evidence is actually building 
that together with the Iran war, tariffs have contributed to the persistent 
inflation that voters are upset about. In arecent paper 
<https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/10/how-fast-do-tariffs-pass-through-into-consumer-prices/>
 economists at the New York Fed documented not only that the cost of tariffs 
passed through to consumer prices but also that Trump’s duties had effectively 
brought an end to the falling inflation he inherited.

Greer isn’t a fan of that work. He’s also signaling that whatever happens in 
the midterms the administration will be proceeding with its plans for more 
tariffs. “I doubt it,” he answered when we asked if we’d seen peak tariffs.

301 Probe

Greer told us the widely anticipated Section 301 investigations into policies 
encouragingexcess industrial capacity 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-31/trump-s-latest-tariffs-how-he-s-keeping-his-trade-wall-in-place?cmpid=BBD102626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10485>
 in China, the European Union and more than a dozen other economies would 
likely be completed before the end of the year and certainly before a March 
2027 deadline. They were delayed not by politics or the midterm elections, he 
said, but by complexity. The issues being uncovered and their potential 
responses were all “bespoke,” he explained.

He also insists that the tariffs now being rolled out won’t suffer any more 
legal setbacks like the February Supreme Court decision that Trump had 
illegally invoked the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose the 
bulk of his import taxes in 2025.

Greer clearly knows he’s fighting an uphill political battle for now, however. 
Let’s see what the midterms bring. But going into them voters are definitely 
signaling that tariffs are far from their favorite word. And Trump is certainly 
noticing.

 | A provincial election in British Columbia is exposing the limits of using 
Trump as a political foil, even in a place hardest hit by his tariffs. British 
Columbia Premier David Eby called an election two years early, seeking to win a 
stronger mandate by tapping into Canadian patriotism in the face of Trump’s 
threats, but he is currently five points behind his BC Conservative opponents. 
The election highlights the trade-war dilemma weighing on Canada: Eby’s rivals 
say the way to save the lumber industry is less about opposing Trump and more 
about cutting bureaucracy, revamping environmental rules and reforming 
Indigenous land rights. Separately, the Canadian private jet maker Bombardier
