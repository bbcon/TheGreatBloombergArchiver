Deep Dive: Risks to Hydropower
A damaged section of a highway in Nuwakot, central Nepal on Aug. 27.
Photographer: NurPhoto/NurPhoto
The devastating deluge that swept through mountain valleys along the 
Nepal-China border is apotent reminder 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-02/nepal-flood-exposes-risk-to-hydropower-in-himalayas-due-to-climate-change?cmpid=090226_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8422>
 of the growing threat climate change poses to clean-energy projects in one of 
the world’s most hazard-prone areas.

 * Hydropower dams high in the Himalayas seem to be a ready solution for an 
energy-starved region. But a changing climate is bringing with it unstable 
glaciers and unpredictable rain, creating new risks and raising the potential 
costs.
 * The region has already had warnings, including a glacier collapse in the 
Indian state of Uttarakhand in 2021 that left at least 200 dead or missing, and 
floods in Pakistan that left a third of the country under water in 2022.
 * The disaster wiped out about 10% of Nepal’s hydropower capacity, striking 
at the heart of one of Asia’s most ambitious clean-energy buildouts. Nepal, 
Bhutan, India and China have all dramatically expanded plans for hydropower in 
recent years.
 * All high mountain areas with ice, glaciers and permafrost — the frozen 
ground that holds rocks together — are at risk, according to Holger Frey, a 
researcher leading a group studying hazards and risks in mountains at the 
University of Zürich. “The big difference between countries is the amount of 
people and the amount of infrastructure close to these sites.”
