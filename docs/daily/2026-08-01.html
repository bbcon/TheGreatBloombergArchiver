Saturday, August 01, 2026

US

  • No major US macro data releases on the calendar for August 1, 2026; markets awaiting next week's payrolls report.
  • Treasury Secretary Bessent's China strategy under scrutiny as the US pursues bilateral engagement with Beijing rather than coordinating with Europe.
  • Goldman Sachs analysts flagged that Treasury's July 23 currency report softened language on yuan undervaluation, dropping a January characterization of the currency as "substantially undervalued."

Fiscal and Trade Policy

The principal US development of the day is a strategic one rather than a data-driven one. Treasury Secretary Bessent, who had previously articulated a vision of coordinating allied pressure on China's trade and currency practices, appears to have allowed a meaningful diplomatic opportunity to pass. Germany and France have in recent months moved toward precisely the China-centric multilateral framework Bessent once described — including Chancellor Merz's call for a currency dialogue modeled loosely on the 1985 Plaza Accord. Instead of engaging this opening, the administration has pursued its own bilateral track with Beijing.

Currency and Markets

The Treasury Department's semi-annual currency report, released July 23, drew particular attention for what it omitted: the January description of the yuan as "substantially undervalued." Goldman Sachs analysts interpreted this as a softening of stance, though a Trump administration official pushed back, noting yuan appreciation since the prior report and confirming no joint G-7 yuan pressure effort is underway. The IMF has separately estimated yuan undervaluation at as much as 21%. Whether the administration's posture hardens ahead of the next report — following an October EU-China trade deadline — remains to be seen. ---

Asia

  • Indonesia's central bank governor unexpectedly resigned, injecting uncertainty into one of Southeast Asia's most systemically important monetary institutions.
  • China's yuan remains a focal point of international tension; the IMF estimates the currency is undervalued by as much as 21%, while the US softened its own assessment in its July 23 Treasury report.
  • Japan continues to seek US coordination on currency matters and is engaged in a bilateral geoeconomic dispute with China following Beijing's export controls on key industrial inputs.

Currency and Trade Tensions

China's currency policy is emerging as the defining fault line in Asian macro. The yuan's undervaluation — estimated at up to 21% by the IMF — is generating coordinated concern across the region, though that coordination remains fragmented. Japan, which faces its own bilateral export-control dispute with Beijing, would logically benefit from a G-7 collective approach, as would South Korea, whose won has faced pressure in part from competitive dynamics with China. To date, no such multilateral framework has materialized, with each affected economy navigating the relationship with Beijing individually.

Institutional Risk

The abrupt resignation of Indonesia's central bank chief introduces a meaningful source of uncertainty for Southeast Asian markets. Bank Indonesia has been regarded as a credible, independent institution, and unexpected leadership transitions at systemically important central banks can temporarily unsettle domestic rate expectations and currency dynamics. The circumstances and succession timeline warrant close monitoring, as investor confidence in the institution's policy continuity will be a key variable in the near term. ---

Europe

  • Germany's Chancellor Merz has called for international coordination on China's currency, explicitly referencing the 1985 Plaza Accord as a potential model, with EU finance ministers now formally taking up the issue.
  • France's Macron has warned the EU may need to act against what he describes as an unsustainable trade deficit with China; a French government think tank recommended a 30% tariff or a 20–30% euro depreciation against the yuan.
  • Europe's shift on China trade policy is occurring against the backdrop of continued Russian military activity in Ukraine and growing concern about Beijing's support for Moscow.

Trade Policy Realignment

The most consequential European development this week is strategic rather than data-driven: a meaningful shift in the continent's posture toward China. Germany, historically among the strongest advocates for open economic engagement with Beijing, is reconsidering that stance as several industrial sectors face deteriorating competitive conditions. Merz's references to a Plaza Accord-style arrangement signal that Berlin now views the yuan's undervaluation as a matter requiring coordinated political action, not merely market adjustment. France has moved in the same direction, with its planning commission warning of a "destructive destruction" scenario for European industry absent a decisive policy response.

Geopolitical Context

Europe's hardening trade stance toward China is reinforced by security concerns. Brussels has grown increasingly uncomfortable with Beijing's continued economic support for Moscow during the Ukraine conflict, adding a geopolitical dimension to what might otherwise be framed as a purely commercial dispute. The combination of industrial pressure and security unease appears to be producing a more unified European position — yet without US engagement, the practical leverage available to the EU in any currency or trade dialogue with China remains materially constrained. ---

LatAm

  • Peru is entering a new presidential term, offering a potential opportunity to break what analysts have characterized as a persistent cycle of political instability.

Political Economy

Peru's political transition represents the region's most notable development for the period. The country has cycled through significant executive instability in recent years, and the incoming administration faces the challenge of restoring institutional credibility while managing a commodity-dependent economy exposed to global demand conditions. Whether the new government can consolidate a functional governing coalition — a prerequisite for meaningful fiscal and structural reform — remains the central question for investors assessing Peruvian sovereign risk. No significant macro data releases for the broader Latin American region were reported for this period. ---

Global

  • The US is pursuing bilateral engagement with China on trade and currency rather than coordinating with Europe and Japan, squandering what analysts describe as a rare alignment of allied interests.
  • China's AI sector produced a notable development, with "China's Moonshot" releasing a breakthrough model for public download, intensifying competition in the global AI race.
  • Cross-regional currency misalignment — particularly the yuan's estimated 21% undervaluation per the IMF — is emerging as a structural macro theme with implications for trade competitiveness across Europe, Japan, South Korea, and Southeast Asia.

Multilateral Coordination Failure

The most consequential global theme this week is the absence of coordinated action. The US, Europe, Japan, and South Korea all share grievances about China's trade and currency practices, yet each is engaging Beijing separately rather than through a unified framework. Analysts at the Council on Foreign Relations and the Mercator Institute for China Studies have independently characterized this as a significant strategic miscalculation, with one observer invoking the fragmented Allied response to Napoleonic France as a historical analogue. The window for a Plaza Accord-style multilateral initiative has not yet closed — an October EU-China trade deadline and a forthcoming US-China summit both represent potential inflection points — but the current trajectory favors continued fragmentation.

AI and Technology Competition

China's release of a new large-scale AI model adds momentum to what is increasingly a defining dimension of US-China strategic competition. Analysts close to the Trump administration suggest the president's primary focus on the bilateral relationship is the AI race rather than exchange rate dynamics, which may partly explain the softening of US language on yuan undervaluation. This framing — prioritizing technology competition over currency coordination — has material implications for allied strategy, as it suggests the US may be willing to deprioritize trade balance concerns in exchange for perceived advantages in the broader technology contest.
