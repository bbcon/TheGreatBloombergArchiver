Deep Dive: A Spanish Inquisition
An encampment was set up at Madrid’s Puerta del Sol as part of social protests 
regarding housing issues in Spain.
A giant banner featuring Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’s smiling face hangs 
over the main entrance to Madrid’s central Sol station. A few weeks earlier, it 
was the epicenter of some of thebiggest protests 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-24/spain-election-2026-angry-generation-feels-left-behind-as-economy-booms?cmpid=112426_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11785>
 the country has seen in a generation.

 * Demonstrators railed against politicians and their failure to deal with a 
housing crisis, which has become a symbol for broader anger about the cost of 
living and inequality. Many commuters shared the frustration, enduring delays 
on trains taking them to jobs that barely pay enough to cover rent.
 * The protest camp is gone now, removed by police. But the outrage that drove 
people there in the first place remains. And Spanish voters will have their say 
this Sunday after Sánchez called a snap election.
 * The mood among large swathes of the population is at odds with the image of 
Spain on paper. It looks like a model economy, expanding more than twice as 
fast as the eurozone, cutting its budget deficit and reducing unemployment.
 * The numbers, however stellar, don’t capture reality for many Spaniards, 
especially the younger generation, whose lives are dominated by worries about 
money, job security and what the future holds. Housing looms large. Property 
prices have risen about twice as fast as wages for young workers. Two-thirds of 
adults under the age of 34 still live with their parents.
