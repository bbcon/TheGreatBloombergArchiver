Seeking a payoff

SK Hynix’s results show that the companies that make the picks and shovels for 
AI are raking in cash, with no slowdown in sight. But investors are still 
worried about the tech giants that are spending all that money on computing 
power. Will it pay off, and when?

Case in point, Microsoft and Alphabet. The two stocks have gone in different 
directions since their last earnings reports. As the two prepare to report 
again after the bell today,recent trading indicates investors give Microsoft an 
edge 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-28/microsoft-and-alphabet-earnings-put-focus-on-ai-cash-flow-drain?cmpid=102826_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10628>
.

Microsoft shares have soared 36% since its earnings on July 29 showed the 
fastest cloud growth in four years, sparking their best day since 2008. 
Alphabet, meanwhile, has lost 6.3% since its results on July 22 revealed that 
its free cash flow turned negative due to a substantial increase in AI 
spending. Year to date, though, both stocks are underperforming the market.

Google’s parent is projected to have negative free cash flow this year and 
next, while Microsoft’s free cash flow is expected to decline in its current 
fiscal year, which ends in June, and then rise for the foreseeable future.

“The companies that can show they are producing cash for shareholders are more 
in the driver’s seat in this market than companies that aren’t,” said Luke 
O’Neill of the Catalyst Dynamic Alpha Fund. “That’s why Microsoft has been 
doing better than the other hyperscalers over the past few months. It is 
showing an ability to navigate this spending and returns environment better.”

So investors will be looking at the growth in the companies’ cloud-computing 
businesses, which is often seen as a proxy for AI-related demand, when judging 
their spending.

If the companies disappoint on cloud growth or otherwise fail to make the case 
that their spending is paying off, their shares could get hit like Meta 
Platforms did after reporting earnings last week. —Ryan Vlastelica 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AT3hUmpnSRM/ryan-vlastelica?cmpid=102826_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10628>

Losing buyers

Being one of Washington’s closest partners no longer means reliably funding 
the US deficit.

Countries and regions most aligned with the US bought a net $174.3 billion of 
Treasuries, excluding bills, in the first eight months of 2026, according toan 
analysis of Treasury and State Department data 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-28/us-friendly-nations-slash-treasury-purchases-by-60-led-by-asia?cmpid=102826_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10628>
. That was the lowest for the period since 2021 and down more than 60% from a 
year earlier.

The shift is noteworthy in Asia, where economies running large surpluses are 
putting less of that money into US debt. Japan, the biggest foreign holder of 
Treasuries, bought just $389 million in the first eight months — down 99% from 
a year earlier.

There’s no single explanation for the shift. Governments have said little 
about any plans to move away from Treasuries, and the data don’t separate 
government buying from private investment at the country level. Several factors 
may be contributing, including weak currencies, rising yields at home, growing 
demand for stocks and unease over US policy.

“The Treasury market isn’t losing foreign buyers — it’s losing automatic 
buyers,” said Masahiko Loo at State Street Investment Management. “An 
increasingly unpredictable US makes large structural overweights and the 
automatic recycling of capital into Treasuries harder to justify.” —Ruth Carson 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS35-mFs7Fw/ruth-carson?cmpid=102826_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10628>
