Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Europe team leader for gas, power and renewables Rachel Morison looks at how 
grids are struggling to keep pace with the rapid buildout of wind and solar.

Europe spent the past decade racing to build wind and solar farms. The power 
grid hasn’t kept up.

A wind or solar farm can take just two to three years to build, while a new 
transmission line can take as long as a decade, according to BloombergNEF. That 
gap is turning the grid into one of the biggest constraints on Europe’s energy 
transition.

Britain showed the consequences this week. The grid operator issued warnings on
four consecutive days 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-27/uk-grid-issues-another-warning-for-tight-power-supplies?cmpid=BBBXT112726_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261127&utm_content=11941>
 that its safety margin was too thin — not because the country lacked power, 
but because it couldn’t move enough electricity where needed.

Wind farms in the north were being paid to switch off because the network was 
congested, while power stations further south were being paid to switch on. 
Consumers ultimately pay for both.

There’ve been five days with warnings so far this winter, more than twice as 
many as in the previous five winters combined and the most since 2020, when 
there were six, according to data from the National Energy System Operator.

Germany faces the same problem. Much of its wind capacity is concentrated in 
the north, while major industrial demand sits in the south. When the network 
cannot carry enough electricity between the two, grid operators have to 
intervene.

Germany spends about €3 billion ($3.3 billion) on these congestion-management 
measures per year, shifting the equivalent of more than 30 terawatt-hours of 
electricity around the system. The country is building major north-south 
transmission lines, including SuedLink, but until they are operating the
Bundesnetzagentur says 
<https://www.smard.de/page/en/topic-article/5892/220534/volume-of-measures-stable-in-the-year-as-a-whole>
 redispatch will be the country’s main tool.

Europe still needs more renewable generation. But building more wind and solar 
farms is no longer enough. Increasingly, the challenge is getting the 
electricity they produce to where it is needed.

Fixing that will be expensive. The European Commission estimates 
<https://energy.ec.europa.eu/topics/infrastructure/european-grids_en> €584 
billion of investment in electricity grids will be needed by 2030.

Until the networks catch up, consumers can end up paying to switch off cheap 
renewable power in one place and activate more expensive generation elsewhere — 
while also funding the grid expansion needed to fix the problem.

—Rachel Morison, Bloomberg News
