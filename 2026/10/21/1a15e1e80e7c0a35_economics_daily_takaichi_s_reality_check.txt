Delivery Test

Sanae Takaichi has made a big impression on the world stage during her first 
year in office. Now she needs to match that on the home front, and especially 
on the economy.

Japan’s first female prime minister faces the test of delivering on her 
promises, my colleagues Sakura Murakami and Yoshiaki Noharareport in an overview
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/one-year-on-japan-s-takaichi-faces-reality-check-on-tax-cut-defense-promises?cmpid=BBD102126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10348>
 of her achievements and prospects. Not all those challenges are economic ones, 
but some of the biggest are.
Sanae TakaichiPhotographer: Kiyoshi Ota/Bloomberg
That includes inflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-16/boj-s-ueda-can-t-tolerate-underlying-inflation-further-over-2?cmpid=BBD102126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10348>
, something Japan’s not used to after decades without it. Takaichi came to 
power with Japan still adjusting to new realities of a steadily rising cost of 
living. Japan’s inflation rate is lower than most global peers, but frustration 
is growing and a recent poll showed almost 60% of respondents aren’t happy with 
how she’s handling it.

That adds urgency to the premier’s pledge to slash the sales tax 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/japan-s-cabinet-approves-temporary-food-sales-tax-cut-to-1?cmpid=BBD102126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10348>
 on food from 8% to 1% next April. Some details have yet to be worked out – 
including how to pay for it, since Takaichi has also promised to lower the 
country’s debt-to-GDP ratio, which is among the world’s highest.

Then there’s her signature promise to kickstart investment. Takaichi’s ¥370 
trillionblueprint 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/japan-to-map-out-first-five-years-of-takaichi-s-investment-plan?cmpid=BBD102126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10348>
 will require her to corral support from corporate giants in industries like 
chips and aerospace, encouraging them to match government investments.

Need-to-Know Research

The AI investment boom is premised on the idea that demand for computing power 
is unlimited. A recent slowdown in spending on compute tokens suggests that’s 
not so when users have to pay, according to Torsten Slok of Apollo Global 
Management Inc.

The signs are instead that demand for compute is “highly price-sensitive,” 
with buyers ready to dial back their purchases or seek cheaper alternatives 
when there’s even a modest price change, Slok wrote in a note this week.

He sees a squeeze on the supply side of the data-center industry too, as 
returns on capital decline. All of this points to an “economic flaw in the AI 
bull case,” he wrote. Companies will pay a high price for compute if they can 
turn it into higher revenues and profits or lower costs, but there’s little 
evidence so far it’s happening at scale. “Maybe that will come in the future. 
Maybe not.”
