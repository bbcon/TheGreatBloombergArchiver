France’s Deluge

Paris is burning <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JjI3TM__A5o> and it’s 
terrifying to behold. Indeed, with riots inother cities 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I6XijqjtJoA>, much of Europe is burning 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JjI3TM__A5o>. Does it matter?

France is a much smaller share of the global economy than it used to be, and 
now accounts for less than 3% of global gross domestic product, less than half 
its level from 1980. It matters less than it once did to the rest of the world:

There’s also some evidence that France’s fiscal woes aren’t driving contagion 
to other euro zone members in the way that happened during the crisis of 
2010-12. If anything, it’s US Treasuries, rather than French OATS, that appear 
most contagious. Spreads of Italian and Greek bonds over German bunds widened 
sharply during September, when Treasury yields were surging — although they 
always lagged the sharp rise in French yields. So far this month, increasing 
French spreads in response to the scenes on the streets have been accompanied 
by narrowing premiums for Greece and Italy:

The euro zone crisis’ acute contagion stemmed from banks, who were heavily 
exposed to peripheral countries’ debt. That is no longer the case, and this 
chart from Absolute Strategy Research shows that while French bank stocks have 
tanked during this crisis, others in the region have not:


It thus makes some sense that US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-20/bessent-sees-no-spillover-from-france-bond-market-s-turmoil?cmpid=BBD102126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10316>
 averred Tuesday that French problems wouldn’t spill over elsewhere. In a 
highly connected global financial system, the risk of contagion is always 
present, but in this case, Bessent argued, “The French have a tough hand, but 
President Macron’s economic team, whom I know very well, are really first rate.”

But greater issues remain. Whatever Bessent’s opinion, investors have lost 
confidence in Team Macron 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-20/political-risk-makes-france-a-go-to-market-for-short-sellers?cmpid=BBD102126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10316>
 as the president nears the end of his 10 years in power. He was feted for 
stopping a third populist black swan after Brexit and Trump when he beat the 
far right’s Marine Le Pen for the presidency in 2017. But his disastrously 
misjudged snap legislative elections in 2024, losing what control his centrists 
still retained, brought French spreads over bunds back to their worst levels 
from the 2017 campaign. They’ve now ballooned far higher:

With investors’ alarm growing, Ian Harnett of Absolute Strategy argues that 
contagion risks are “likely to revolve around the 57.5% of French government 
debt held by foreign institutions, not banks.”Insurers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-20/french-riots-seen-as-insurance-test-before-new-regime-kicks-in?cmpid=BBD102126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10316>
, in particular, could be an issue. By comparison, only 35.9% of Italian BTPs 
are foreign owned.

Further, the room for either Macron or his successor to maneuver is limited. 
At nearly 110% of GDP, French government debt is similar to the US (while the 
rest of the euro zone has been more stringent):

The problem is that France’s private sector is already leveraged to the hilt 
in a way that far outstrips the US or the rest of Europe. That means that the 
country cannot readily raise more debt by selling to its own private sector:

Markets outside France are treating this with relative calm because of the 
experience of 2010-2012, when the European Central Bank famously intervened. 
Jonas Goltermann of Capital Economics argues:
The strengthening of the euro zone’s institutional framework and the 
improvement of ECB’s crisis-fighting toolset over the past decade and a half 
provides some comfort that France’s fiscal problems arestill a long way from 
sparking a full-scale re-run of the 2010-12 crisis 
<https://www.capitaleconomics.com/publications/europe-economics-update/how-big-are-contagion-risks-france>
.
But French politics will still ensure that the situation gets worse before it 
improves. Goltermann takes the Italian political crisis of 2018, when an 
incoherent coalition of left- and right-populists attempted a fight with 
Brussels over the deficit, as a template. Italian yields’ spread over German 
bunds topped at 325 basis points before the government backtracked.

Investors think they see how this ends — but the drama still needs to play out 
in the streets and ballot boxes, and in insurers’ balance sheets.

Housing Gloom Thickens

Mortgage rates aren’t that high. Four decades ago, they were in double-digits 
and made the American Dream of home-ownership prohibitively expensive. But for 
a generation accustomed to being able to fund housing purchases at rates near 
4%, the surge in rates over the last five years has had much the same effect.
Pending home sales 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-20/us-pending-home-sales-fall-to-record-low-as-mortgage-rates-rise?cmpid=BBD102126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10316>
, tracking signed contracts for which transactions have not closed, plunged 
last month to their lowest level since the National Association of Realtors 
began keeping the numbers in 2001:

Is this a cyclical response to rising borrowing costs, or a more meaningful 
breakdown in housing affordability? Perhaps a combination, since these aren’t 
mutually exclusive. Bankrate.com’s national average for a 30-year fixed 
mortgage, which follows rates available to borrowers at the application stage, 
recently topped itshighest level since 2024 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/us-mortgage-rates-hit-7-5-extending-slump-in-purchase-activity?cmpid=BBD102126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10316>
. The benchmark Fannie Mae 30-year bond yield, tracking the market where such 
mortgages are financed, is more sensitive to the Fed’s policy trajectory. It 
has declined this month, but is still hovering near a three-year high:

This surge in mortgage rates, driven primarily by the Treasury selloff, offers 
the most plausible explanation for the housing market drying up. It simply 
makes homes less affordable. While the slight pullback inTreasury yields 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-20/treasuries-rise-as-oil-retreats-from-highest-levels-in-weeks?cmpid=BBD102126_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261021&utm_content=10316>
 should halt the upward march in mortgage rates, Thomas Ryan of Capital 
Economics argues that a more sustained decline is needed to repair sales 
activity in any meaningful way:
As a result, we expect sales to remain very weak through the end of the year, 
with transactions unlikely to rise above the 4 million mark. Next year should 
bring some improvement, as lower energy prices and a slightly less hawkish Fed 
tightening cycle than investors currently anticipate help to ease some of the 
pressure.
How soon this unravels depends on what’s driving the slump. Oxford Economics’ 
Bernard Yaros says it’s no longer possible to attribute it to people deferring 
their demand. Unlike in past cycles when housing slows this badly, the Fed at 
this point is more likely to hike rates than cut them, as the spending on the 
AI buildout is totally out of sync with the state of the real estate market. 
Homebuyers and sellers cannot expect cheaper mortgages to rescue them, at least 
in the near term.

Lower rates will only be possible if inflation — propped up by tariffs, 
geopolitics, and AI investment demand — trends clearly toward the Fed’s target:
Some move toward fiscal rectitude would help bring down long-term interest 
rates, but the window for fiscal reforms isn’t until early next decade, when 
Social Security’s trust fund will run out of reserves.
That would be a long time for the housing market to stay becalmed. Building 
permits issued also fell last week, reinforcing the weak demand narrative. But 
there is more nuance beyond the headlines. September’s fall was driven by a 
decrease in single-family permits, while those for multifamily units shot up. 
The data are strongly seasonal, and the more reliable three-month moving 
average suggests that activity is not slowing down anything like so badly:

Barclays’ Colin Johanson notes that the trend reflects persistent weakness as 
the double whammy of high rates and elevated prices weigh on buyers. Sluggish 
activity also hurts homebuilders. The S&P 1500 Homebuilding index is down more 
than 10% year to date, and almost back to the nadir after last year’s 
Liberation Day tariffs:

Demand destruction from high rates has much to do with this, but it’s only a 
part of the story. Carl Weinberg of High Frequency Economics argues that 
building materials such as lumber, steel, copper, and aluminum, made costlier 
by tariffs and prone to unpredictable swings, are compounding the problem. 
Skilled labor is also in short supply as a surge in data-center construction 
competes for the same workers:
Even though the supply of houses for sale is short, don’t expect a rapid 
recovery in homebuilding until inflation uncertainty is addressed, monetary 
policy uncertainty is removed, bond yields stop rising, and corrosive 
uncertainty about costs, tariffs, and other policies is resolved. With the 
population declining, no fundamental pulse is driving demand for new homes 
anyway.
Even a prompt rate cut wouldn’t turn housing into an economic motor. The 
broader economy is strong despite weakening consumer sentiment, but Jay 
Hatfield of Infrastructure Capital Advisors argues that further declines in 
housing activity triggered by Fed tightening could drive a recession. For now, 
the AI boom may shield the economy from the effects of further hikes.

The greatest risk then becomes that rates rise enough to shut off the 
high-yield market for companies trying to finance AI infrastructure. Whatever 
happens, housing is unlikely to boost the economy any time soon; as ever, the 
focus has to stay on AI.

—Richard Abbey

Survival Tips

More on the joy of long shots. The long pass is more of an established part of 
American football than the lob from the halfway line in soccer, but it’s 
amazing when it happens.This compilation 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vQiHbCmWBOI> offers the best long throw in 
every NFL team’s history. In college football,Doug Flutie’s game-winning pass 
for Boston College <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5-_uYyVlxrk> over Miami in 
1984 was all anyone wanted to talk about when I arrived in Boston. It still 
defies comprehension. In ice hockey, a goal scored by a goalie from his own net 
is up there for sheer implausibility — seethis one 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TQYtmc0Imio> from the New York Rangers’ Igor 
Shesterkin against Tampa Bay this season. In baseball, the home run is part of 
the game, and there are no extra points for distance — but shots likelast 
year’s longest, by Nick Kurtz <https://youtu.be/LZimPg-LYwM?t=476>, are still 
awe-inspiring.

Also, my apologies to Carli Lloyd of the US for missing this 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mBosyOJ3PIY> from the 2015 women’s World Cup, 
and to Crystal Palace. David Beckham’s 1996 wonder goal was against Wimbledon 
not Palace. I was confused because the game was at Palace’s Selhurst Park, 
where Wimbledon were tenants at the time.
