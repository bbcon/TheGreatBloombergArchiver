Japan’s Costly Cure

For decades, Japan had a problem most economies could only dream of: prices 
that barely moved. Now it is discovering that escapingdeflation comes with 
problems 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-08-13/japan-s-inflation-paradox-a-pensioner-backlash-in-an-era-of-economic-revival?cmpid=BBD081726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7773>
 of its own.

The return of inflation has helped set in motion some of the changes 
policymakersspent years 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-03-19/boj-ends-massive-easing-program-with-first-rate-hike-since-2007?cmpid=BBD081726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7773>
 trying to engineer. Companies are making more money, workers are securing their
strongest pay gains 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-04/japan-s-nominal-wages-rise-again-backing-boj-rate-hike-case?cmpid=BBD081726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7773>
 in decades and households are putting more savings into stocks.

The Nikkei 225 has more than doubled 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-14/japan-stocks-rise-as-us-rate-hike-bets-ease-ai-chip-shares-gain?cmpid=BBD081726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7773>
 since President Donald Trump’s initial tariff shock in April 2025. “Japan is 
back,” Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi declared in Washington this year.

But the latest economic data offer a reminder that Japan’s new era comes with 
its own set of issues.Growth slowed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-16/japan-s-economic-growth-slows-as-mideast-conflict-clouds-outlook?cmpid=BBD081726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7773>
 to an annualized 1.1% last quarter, with private consumption flat and capital 
investment down 1.2% on a non-annualized basis.

Weakness in consumption gets at the paradox confronting Japan’s policymakers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-08-13/japan-s-inflation-paradox-a-pensioner-backlash-in-an-era-of-economic-revival?cmpid=BBD081726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7773>
. Young, educated workers in Tokyo are more likely to be getting raises that 
beat inflation, while homeowners and investors have benefited from soaring 
asset prices. By contrast, though, pensioners and lower-paid workers, 
particularly outside the biggest cities, are going backward asliving costs rise 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-28/takaichi-s-long-term-vision-may-fail-to-dispel-inflation-angst?cmpid=BBD081726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7773>
.

It also makes the Bank of Japan’s job trickier 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-16/japan-s-economic-growth-slows-as-mideast-conflict-clouds-outlook?cmpid=BBD081726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7773>
. Traders still see a September rate increase as likely, but lackluster 
domestic demand makes it harder to argue that a strong economy requires tighter 
policy. The weak yen — itself acontributor to higher import costs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-31/jpy-usd-why-is-japanese-yen-so-weak-and-will-the-government-intervene?cmpid=BBD081726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7773>
 — looms larger in that calculation.

For Takaichi, meanwhile, cushioning households through subsidies and tax cuts 
risks adding to a public-debt burden 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-21/s-p-sees-risk-of-japan-s-sales-tax-cut-worsening-fiscal-position?cmpid=BBD081726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7773>
 already exceeding twice the size of the economy.

All that means though Japan may finally have broken free of deflation, the 
task now is managing an economy in which the cure is creating problems of its 
own.

The Week Ahead

UK data on Wednesday are set to show inflation accelerating for the first time 
in four months, with the median economist estimate in a Bloomberg survey 
predicting a 2.9% jump in July. The Iran war is making plane travel more 
expensive and feeding through to household energy bills, while a crunch in 
AI-related components drives up electronics prices.

It’s the start of a lift-off expected to continue through the second half, 
cutting short a spell of positive news on price pressures. The figures may 
leave BOE rate-setters increasingly uneasy as the Iran war starts to show up 
more clearly in prices — especially with a resilient economy.
