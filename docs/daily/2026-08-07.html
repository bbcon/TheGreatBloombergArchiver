Labor Market and Fed Policy

The July jobs report is the dominant near-term macro event for US markets. Bloomberg consensus anticipates approximately 80,000 payrolls added — a pickup from June's 57,000 but still among the weakest prints of the year — with the unemployment rate expected to hold at 4.2%. The asymmetric read-through is notable: a strong number risks reinforcing expectations for a Federal Reserve rate hike at the September meeting, while a soft print would ease that concern. JPMorgan's Market Intelligence team assigns 65% odds to payrolls exceeding 60,000, and estimates the S&P 500 could fall as much as 1.8% if payrolls top 150,000. St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem reinforced the hawkish case, stating policymakers cannot afford to tolerate higher inflation — though President Trump has reiterated his preference for lower rates, underscoring the political tension around monetary policy.

Markets

Ahead of the report, S&P 500 futures were marginally positive. The 10-year Treasury yield stood at 4.66%, with bonds under mild pressure as higher energy prices revived inflation concerns. BofA's sentiment gauge reaching its most extreme bullish level since 2021 may signal a contrarian caution point, though elevated positioning alone does not determine near-term direction. ---

China Trade and Macro

China's export machine continues to outperform despite broader domestic weakness. July exports surging 23.9% year-over-year — with technology hardware leading — provided a stark contrast to soft domestic demand, which contributed to weaker-than-expected Q2 GDP growth. The services PMI reading of 50.4, barely in expansion territory and the softest in nearly two years, reinforces the picture of an economy dependent on external demand while the consumer sector remains constrained by the prolonged property downturn. Policymakers have designated domestic consumption a 2026 priority, though structural reform on that scale — including social safety net expansion — is a multi-year undertaking.

Yen Intervention and Currency Markets

The joint US-Japan yen intervention, which saw the Bank of Japan deploy an estimated $87 billion, is losing traction. The yen's retreat toward 158.40 reflects unchanged structural headwinds: a wide rate differential with the US, Japan's debt dynamics, and persistent carry trade demand. Overnight index swaps imply roughly a 60% probability of a Bank of Japan rate hike by September. Treasury Secretary Bessent has encouraged Japan to utilize the Fed's FIMA Repo facility to extend intervention firepower — an approach some former officials regard as an unusual intrusion into Fed operational territory. Whether the yen can hold without a credible shift in Japan's rate path remains genuinely uncertain.

Germany and Economic Momentum

German industrial production expanded for a third straight month, offering tentative evidence that Europe's largest economy is holding up despite the economic disruption associated with the US-Iran conflict and elevated energy prices. The resilience is meaningful given persistent concerns about European competitiveness, though analysts flag that declining Rhine River water levels — a recurring climate-related constraint on inland shipping — represent a near-term downside risk to industrial activity. Broader eurozone data flow remains sparse in this period, leaving Germany's industrial print as the primary macro signal from the region.

ECB and Currency Diplomacy

The revelation that the US Treasury sold euros to finance its yen intervention without informing the ECB until after execution has created institutional friction within the G7 monetary framework. ECB President Lagarde spoke with Treasury Secretary Bessent after the July 31 transaction had been completed. Senior ECB officials characterized the use of euros — rather than dollars — as an unprecedented violation of longstanding conventions on cooperation between Western monetary authorities. The episode raises broader questions about the durability of multilateral currency coordination norms under the current US administration. ---

Colombia's New Government

Colombia's inauguration of President De la Espriella marks the region's latest rightward turn, but the governing environment he inherits is structurally challenging. A widening fiscal deficit, persistent inflation, a deteriorating security situation, and a congress outside his coalition's control constrain his room for maneuver from the outset. His agenda draws explicitly from Milei's austerity playbook and Bukele's security model, yet the regional experience — including in Argentina, Peru, and Chile — suggests that electoral mandates for change do not automatically translate into governing capacity. Critically, proximity to Washington has provided less material support than anticipated; the White House has demonstrated limited appetite for underwriting allied governments' economic or security challenges, leaving De la Espriella largely without an external backstop.

Regional Political Economy

China alleged US interference in Argentina after American embassy officials opposed a Huawei telecoms contract expansion — an episode illustrating the intensifying US-China competition for influence in Latin America. The Trump administration's continued pressure on Cuba via targeted sanctions reflects ongoing use of economic tools in the region. These developments collectively reinforce a picture of Latin America navigating competing external pressures — US ideological alignment on one side, Chinese economic engagement on the other — with limited leverage to extract concrete benefits from either. ---

Strait of Hormuz

The Hormuz situation remains unresolved and the dominant macro risk for global energy markets. A draft bill under review by Iran's parliament would bar American and Israeli vessels, demand compensation from "hostile countries," prohibit Israel-linked cargo, and impose fee structures for transit services. While President Trump described negotiations with Oman as progressing, the parliamentary conditions — if adopted — would substantially complicate any reopening agreement. The Houthis' "large-scale" attack on Saudi-backed Yemeni forces, with strikes reaching the Najran region, raises the risk of geographic escalation. Energy markets are pricing the uncertainty: Brent crude's advance reflects fading confidence in a near-term resolution. The conflict is simultaneously sustaining demand for military technology, including chips and missile-related hardware, which partly explains the strength of Chinese tech exports.

Global Monetary Coordination

The US-Japan yen intervention's diplomatic fallout illustrates the strains on multilateral financial governance. The ECB's exclusion from pre-coordination on a transaction that directly deployed euros as the funding currency is a structurally significant precedent. Treasury Secretary Bessent's "buy-side" approach — leveraging unconventional tools including the Fed's FIMA Repo facility and signaling three-times leverage in currency defense — represents a meaningful departure from the probabilistic, consensus-driven style of prior Treasury secretaries. Whether this approach proves effective over a sustained period, or whether it undermines trust in G7 coordination mechanisms, remains to be seen.
