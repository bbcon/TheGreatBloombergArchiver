**Energy Markets and Hormuz Risk**
The Strait of Hormuz remains the central node of global energy risk. Brent at $114 reflects both the sustained US-Iran military conflict — which began in February and has persisted well beyond initial Washington and Jerusalem expectations — and the escalating Houthi campaign against Saudi infrastructure, including the evacuation of Aramco's Dhahran headquarters. The fragmentation of oil markets is producing sharp regional price divergences: Europe and East Asia are paying $120–$160 per barrel while North America benefits from prices below $100. Physical commercial cargo crossings through the strait remain severely depressed. Trump has signaled additional strikes are possible after the November 3 midterms; Netanyahu has explicitly framed the goal as regime change in Tehran; Israeli elections on October 27 add a further variable with polls suggesting no parliamentary majority for either bloc.

**Cross-Asset Risk and Monetary Divergence**
The week's most consequential cross-asset development was the simultaneous selloff in AI-exposed equities and rally in sovereign bonds — suggesting investors are beginning to question whether AI capital expenditure will generate commensurate returns. Global monetary policy is diverging in meaningful ways: the BOJ appears to be moving toward December tightening; the PBOC is easing through structural reform; the Philippines delivered a surprise 50 basis point hike; Turkey cut for the first time since the Iran war began; and the Fed remains on hold with markets pricing three additional hikes by mid-2027. Gulf AI infrastructure faces a compounding risk dimension that was not initially priced — physical vulnerability to military strikes raises data center construction costs by an estimated 5%–7%. Separately, Berkeley Earth now expects 2026 to be the hottest year on record as the most extreme El Niño cycle on record intensifies, with food commodity prices rising and institutional investors deploying bespoke emerging-market hedging strategies.
