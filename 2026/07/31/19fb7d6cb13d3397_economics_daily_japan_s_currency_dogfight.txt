Yen Dogfight

The yen in recent weeks has been trading near its weakest level since 1986 — 
back whenTop Gun was top of the Box Office. Now, Japanese authorities are 
locked in adogfight with markets 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/yen-surges-after-japan-intervened-in-market-again-ahead-of-boj?cmpid=BBD073126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7098>
, trying to shore up the currency through a surprise intervention that appears 
to have had US backing.

The weak yen is a problem for nationalist Prime Minister and monetary policy 
dove Sanae Takaichi because it drives up import costs in the energy importing 
nation, adding to cost-of-living pressures that are eroding her popularity. An 
accompanying rise in bond yields also make it pricier for Japan to roll over 
its massive pile of government debt and may crimp Takaichi’s bold bid to 
transform an economy mired by decades of drift, as we explore in today’sBig Take
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-30/japan-pm-takaichi-faces-biggest-test-yet-as-yen-sinks-approval-ratings-drop?cmpid=BBD073126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7098>
.
Sanae Takaichi, Japan’s prime minister, center, departs following a meeting 
with the Liberal Democratic Party executives at the party’s headquarters in 
Tokyo on Thursday, July 30.
Takaichi is aiming to ramp up military spending, boost strategic industries 
like shipbuilding and has just announced a plan to temporarily cut thesales tax 
on food 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/japan-s-takaichi-calls-for-food-sales-tax-cut-to-1-nhk-says?cmpid=BBD073126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7098>
 to 1% from April next year. Problem is, investors are skeptical she’ll be able 
to pay for all her promises.

Combine that with a yawning gap between Japan’s benchmark rate — held at 1% 
Friday — and the benchmark USfederal funds rate 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-29/fed-holds-rates-steady-three-officials-dissent-favoring-a-hike?cmpid=BBD073126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7098>
 of 3.5% to 3.75% and the upshot is a floundering local currency.

Echoing US President Donald Trump, Takaichi in the past has signaled a 
preference for lower rates to keep growth humming.

Bank of Japan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-31/boj-holds-rate-steady-with-upward-revision-to-economic-outlook?cmpid=BBD073126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7098>
 Governor Kazuo Ueda, by contrast, wants to keep normalizing policy, but he’s 
reluctant to push too fast and snap what’s shaping up as one of the longest 
growth streaks since WWII. That left Ueda sounding somewhat hawkish on Friday, 
but not quite enough to sustain all the yen’s overnight gains.

Balancing the yen’s fragility with policy goals will be no easy feat for 
either Ueda or Takaichi in the months ahead.

Need-to-Know Research

Discussions of international trade typically focus on goods, with services — 
typically including tourists and students heading from one nation to another — 
getting short shrift. As the world digitizes, however, services trade is likely 
to expand, HSBC economists say.

In fact, “some estimates suggest that global services trade could already be 
bigger than goods trade,” HSBC economists led by James Pomeroy wrote in a note 
this week. That’s if you incorporate things like companies delivering legal, 
financial and software services to customers from a physical location abroad. 
Measurement difficulties mean some economies may be growing faster than they 
seem — or their data will be more prone to revision, they wrote.

“For anyone following GDP data on a quarter-to-quarter basis for economies 
with a large services trade component, expect more revisions, potentially large 
ones, as more parts of economic activity get captured,” the HSBC team wrote. 
They also noted that robust cross-border services growth means that “the 
oft-quoted global trade share in GDP could be creeping higher” and that “many 
fears around deglobalization could be overblown.”
