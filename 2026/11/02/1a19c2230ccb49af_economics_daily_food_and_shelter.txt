Essential Spending

A dive into the latest consumer expenditure survey from the US Bureau of Labor 
Statistics offers some perspective as to why Americans are sounhappy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/why-the-us-economy-looks-strong-but-feels-bad-to-americans?cmpid=BBD110226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10829>
 about an economy that by many metrics isroaring 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/us-economy-grows-a-faster-than-expected-3-4-most-in-a-year?cmpid=BBD110226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10829>
.

Last year, US families on average spent $1,988 more on housing and food 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-30/housing-food-costs-make-up-half-of-americans-spending-most-in-15-years?cmpid=BBD110226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10829>
 than in 2024. The two categories together made up almost half of what 
households spent in total that year, the most since 2010.

So while incomes rose faster than inflation, families dedicated a bigger share 
of their budgets to covering basic necessities.

After a decades-long decline in the portion of food budgets in household 
expenses, the share started rising again after the Covid-19 pandemic and 
reached a 14-year high of 13% last year.

That’s mostly because of the protein category. Americans, among the largest 
per-capita meat consumers in the world, spent $1,579 on meat, poultry, fish and 
eggs last year, twice as much as in 2010.

This rise of the past couple of years was fueled first by an avian flu 
outbreak that sentegg prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-02-12/eggs-soar-15-in-a-month-with-grocery-shelves-empty-across-us?cmpid=BBD110226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10829>
 surging, followed by soaringbeef costs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-02-14/beef-prices-are-rising-more-than-inflation-this-year-with-no-relief-seen?cmpid=BBD110226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10829>
.

Back in 1984, Americans spent about 15% of their total expenditures on food 
and 30% on housing. Over the following two decades, as the former shrank, the 
latter expanded. More recently, Covid and the post-pandemic era disrupted 
patterns for both, with the two categories rising in sync since 2023.

The burden of essential spending is set to climb further after the war in Iran 
sent grocery and utilities costs soaring. Additional expenses under the housing 
umbrella, such as insurance, home repair and maintenance, also got costlier. 
And gas expenses soared after falling in the 2025 survey.

Need-to-Know Research

Just in time for the northern hemisphere’s cold-season heating bills, Morgan 
Stanley has come out with a deep-dive look at global power demand, with some 
eye-popping figures that economists may want to consider as they pencil in 
inflation and interest-rate forecasts for 2027 and beyond.

“The world of electricity is transforming at an unprecedented pace,” equity 
analysts led by Mayank Maheshwari wrote in a report last week. Not only is US 
power consumption “inflecting” thanks to data-center construction, but “Europe 
and Japan are seeing steady growth in power consumption” after years of decline.

In Japan’s case, that’s being driven by the semiconductor and AI cycle 
propelling baseload demand, while in Europe, hotter weather means more air 
conditioning is needed – hence more power. In fact, the power needed for 
cooling will match that for data centers by 2030, Morgan Stanley says. “As 
happened in the 1990s, power prices look set to rise further,” after a 20% 
surge this year, the team wrote.
