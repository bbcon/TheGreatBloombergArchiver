‘Jawboning’ Evolves

Global traders have for decades been used to Japanese Finance Ministry 
officials trying tojawbone the yen 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-20/a-trader-s-guide-to-japanese-policymakers-language-on-the-yen?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 — using verbal warnings to get it moving the way they want. Recently, 
policymakers have been eyeing a different market: government debt.

Soaring yields have made it costlier for Japan to service the world’s largest 
public debt load, giving the government every interest in boosting demand. So 
when Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama earlier this monthcalled for 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-10/japan-seeks-more-domestic-investment-by-gpif-pension-funds?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 Japan’s large pension funds, including the giant Government Pension Investment 
Fund, to increase investments in domestic assets, that caughtinvestors’ 
attention 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-14/japan-s-20-year-bond-sale-demand-stronger-than-12-month-average?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
.

Katayama’s comment wasn’t a one-off. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-27/takaichi-s-support-ratings-tumble-as-inflation-concern-lingers?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 days later said “it is important topursue measures 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-17/japan-s-takaichi-says-gpif-investing-in-japan-assets-is-key?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 that encourage households and pension funds” including the GPIF to “further 
increase their investment in Japanese financial assets.”

Japan, of course, isn’t alone in having a giant debt load. The US debt-to-GDP 
ratio is set to exceed its1940s record high 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-11/cbo-boosts-us-deficit-call-by-1-4-trillion-on-trump-policies?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 in several years. Raising taxes and slashing spending to shrink borrowing is 
politically difficult, if not impossible. So that’s made “financial repression” 
of some sort a more likely option.

One scenario is to let inflation shrink the relative burden, but that may also 
be politically challenging, given voters’ anger over the cost-of-living surge 
since Covid. Another scenario is governments mobilizing investment into the 
bond market.

Government-run pension funds like the GPIF may be a relatively easy option. In 
the US, some analysts havemused 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-02-20/new-us-debt-projections-put-spotlight-on-the-doom-loop-scenario?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 about legislative changes to force tax-advantaged savings accounts to allocate 
a certain portion to Treasuries.

While no American officials have broached such a concept, they’ve been 
comfortable with the idea of using suasion in other financial market contexts. 
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent last week said one motivation behind the US 
taking stakes in a number of technology and resource companies is about giving 
direction to private money.

“What we’re trying to do is create market signals,” Bessent said 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/bessent-says-us-will-scrutinize-chinese-ai-models-for-ip-theft?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 on Fox Business. “In essence, trying to tell investors, OK, here’s where the 
puck’s going to be. Skate to it quickly.”

The broad idea of some sort of government pressure coming to bear on investors 
down the line is something on the radar screen for Lori Heinel, who helps 
oversee $6 trillion as chief investment officer at State Street Investment 
Management. “We do have some of these conversations as a team,” she said in an
interview 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-07-24/masters-in-business-with-lori-heinel-video?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 last week.
Lori HeinelPhotographer: Dylan Hollingsworth/Bloomberg
Heinel believes a “separation of powers” will persist. “There could be 
scenarios where there’s exertion of political will and influence, but that 
doesn’t necessarily mean that the actor on the receiving end of that has to 
respond.”

Nonetheless, recent policymaker remarks make clear that official jawboning is 
now something to consider.

Need-to-Know Research

While the leader of Europe’s largest economy — German Chancellor Friedrich 
Merz — has lately been makingstrident comments 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-13/merz-urges-currency-dialogue-with-china-as-trade-worries-rise?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 about China’s exchange rate, Goldman Sachs analysis of a new US Treasury 
report suggests Washington has headed in the other direction.

The Treasury’s semiannual foreign exchange report 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-23/us-treasury-calls-out-china-for-lack-of-transparency-on-currency?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 “relaxed its language on China somewhat,” Goldman strategists Lexi Kanter and 
Michael Cahill wrote in a note Friday. Languagein January 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-29/us-calls-on-china-to-strengthen-substantially-undervalued-yuan?cmpid=BBD072726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6986>
 urging Beijing to let the yuan “strengthen in a timely and orderly manner” was 
gone. Instead, the Treasury said China had “shifted to allowing a gradual and 
managed pace” of appreciation against the dollar in the latter part of 2025 “as 
trade tensions abated.”

Also gone, the Goldman duo noted: language about China having enacted policies 
that “suppressed imports.” They concluded: “Overall, we read the Treasury’s 
assessment as moderately more constructive, including by acknowledging recent 
measures to allow RMB appreciation, while still encouraging authorities to go 
further.” (RMB refers to renminbi, the official name of China’s currency.)
