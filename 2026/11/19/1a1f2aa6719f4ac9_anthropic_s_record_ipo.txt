Deep Dive: The Arctic’s Energy Problem
An Offshore Tug and Supply Ship (Viking Avant, Norway) in Hammerfest, Norway.
Photographer: Dana Ullman/Bloomberg
As the first storm of the oncoming winter gathered force outside, the morning 
shift of energy industry workers emerged downstairs in the hotel for cooked 
breakfasts, sweet pastries and mugs of coffee. The hotel’s bustle in the gloom 
of 5:30 a.m. hinted at Hammerfest’s ambitions as the next frontier in Norway’s 
fossil fuels industry. Withdemand surging 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-19/norway-expands-arctic-oil-and-gas-exploration-defense-near-russia-border?cmpid=111926_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11606>
 for the kind of energy security that Norway can provide, the Arctic hub of 
around 10,000 people is looking to reap similar rewards.

 * Disruption to supplies due to the US war with Iran has cast doubt over the 
Gulf’s reliability and triggered a search for possible alternative energy 
sources. That’s adding impetus to European efforts that were already underway 
to diversify following Russia’s war on Ukraine.
 * Norway has more than half a century of expertise in extracting oil and gas 
from the North Sea. Now the energy industry is moving into the still more 
unhospitable territory of the Arctic, benefitting Hammerfest as the onshore 
base for exploration and production in the region.
 * Environmental groups and the country’s biggest pension fund say the 
industry risks damaging an extremely vulnerable part of the world while stoking 
security threats with Russia next door. Yet geopolitical factors have come 
together to make this a propitious moment for advocates of the push into the 
Arctic, Norway’s government among them.
