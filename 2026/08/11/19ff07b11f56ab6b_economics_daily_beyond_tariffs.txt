Beyond Tariffs

Trump used to like saying that “tariff” was his favorite word. No country was 
a bigger target than China, as he took US levies on the geostrategic rival up 
past 100% last year. Lately, however, the administration’s efforts to reset the 
two nations’ economic relationship has focused on a different set of tools.

Across a range of different products, US authorities have been unveiling a mix 
of subsidies and regulatory revamps aimed at kindling the formation of domestic 
alternatives to Chinese imports. The pattern suggests a new level of 
sophistication, and perhaps a recognition in Washington that high import duties 
on their own may not achieve desired goals.

A slew of recent actions also indicates the White House views the measures as 
so critically important that their timing couldn’t be held off until after the 
much-awaited visit of Chinese President Xi Jinping to the US, expected next 
month.

On Friday, Trump touted $3 billion in US investments in critical minerals 
projects 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-07/trump-touts-3-billion-in-minerals-projects-to-counter-china?cmpid=BBD081126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260811&utm_content=7584>
. Some are aimed at strengthening the Defense Department’s procurement of key 
inputs, while others are designed to encourage output of battery products in 
the US or allied locations.

Need-to-Know Research

Policymakers looking to spur the euro-zone economy might want to read a new 
study by theGerman Institute for Economic Research <https://www.diw.de/en>, 
which looks at how fiscal tools in the bloc should be designed to get the most 
bang for the buck.

The research by Goekhan Ider and Malte Rieth concludes that public investments 
and government consumption have much stronger effects in the short term than 
tax cuts. While every euro in state spending increases gross domestic product 
by as much as €1.30 in the first year, a tax reduction of the same size only 
offers an upside of as much as €0.40.

A key reason for the discrepancy is the euro area’s common monetary policy. 
“Because the European Central Bank generally does not react to fiscal measures 
taken by individual member states, national spending programs are not curbed by 
interest-rate hikes,” said Ider. At the same time, tax cuts — which tend to 
have a deflationary effect — do not benefit from potential monetary easing, 
which would further amplify their impact.
