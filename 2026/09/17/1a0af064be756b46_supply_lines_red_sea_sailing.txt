Container Ships Return to Red Sea Slowly Despite Houthi-Saudi Fighting
Container shipping through the Suez Canal is creeping up, with eastbound 
traffic leading the charge ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌

Just over a quarter of container ship capacity deployed in both directions 
between Europe and Asia is transiting the Suez Canal, according to a new 
analysis, nearly three years since attacks on ships in the Red Sea diverted 
vessels the long way around Africa.

That’s edged up from 17% on Aug. 3, as carriers slowly return to the shortcut 
sitting in the middle of three continents. But as the conflict heats up between 
Houthis and the Saudis, some cargo owners aren’t convinced the more efficient 
route is worth the risk.

On Monday, Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd announced plans to shift four service lines 
through the Red Sea instead of sailing around the Cape of Good Hope, joining 
two other Gemini alliance services already routed through the Egyptian canal.

The companies said they “will continue to monitor the security situation in 
the Middle East region very closely, and any alteration to services within the 
Gemini Cooperation will remain dependent on the ongoing stability in the Red 
Sea area and theabsence of any escalation 
<https://www.maersk.com/news/articles/2026/09/14/structural-changes-ae5-ae11-ae12-me2-gemini-services>
 in conflicts in the region.”

 last month, including Asia-Mediterranean, Asia-North Europe and 
India-Mediterranean. It announcedanother step

 this week involving a westbound service to the US East Coast.

Back to Asia

Alan Murphy, chief executive of maritime data company Sea-Intelligence, 
indicated in a report this week that the share of eastbound traffic through 
Suez between Europe and Asia has picked up since early August. The westbound 
capacity — called the head-haul because it’s usually more profitable — service 
has remained stable.

“This back-haul focus implies that shipping lines are prioritizing 
repositioning vessel capacity back to Asia to address operational gaps from the 
recent port congestion and delays in Asian hubs,” Murphy wrote inthe report 
<https://www.sea-intelligence.com/press-room/408-read-sea-crisis-is-27-normalized>
. “By focusing on vessels from the Mediterranean, they are targeting the 
vessels with the shortest return time to Asia.”


Iran-backed Houthi militants opened a second front 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/mbs-and-saudi-arabia-face-crisis-with-key-oil-pipeline-shut-for-weeks?cmpid=BBD091726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260917&utm_content=8849>
 in the Iran war in July by threatening ships calling at Saudi ports. But 
beyond a few attacks on the kingdom that month, they largely avoided 
hostilities until around 10 days ago, when they ramped up strikes on energy 
sites and western towns.

Houthis have also captured more territory in Yemen, tightening their control 
over Bab el-Mandeb, anotherstrait vital for shipping 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-25/houthi-claim-missile-strikes-on-southern-saudi-arabia?cmpid=BBD091726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260917&utm_content=8849>
 and energy markets.

According to an industry executive, the Houthis have nothing against shipping 
lines, at least for the time being. Communicating with the Houthis is a 
challenge and there are no guarantees, the executive said, but so far there 
have not been problems crossing the Suez for container lines.

Meanwhile, the head of the Global Shippers Forum said the return to Suez 
“seems areckless gamble 
<https://mykn.kuehne-nagel.com/news/article/maersk-and-hapag-lloyd-expand-return-to-suez>
.”

The UK-based group, which represents cargo owners, warned that while Red Sea 
navigation may be presently safe for vessels other than Saudi ships, it’s 
unclear whether the Houthis will continue to hold off on attacking container 
vessels.

That prospect is a concern because it’s currently peak demand season for 
seaborne trade, with a lot of time-sensitive merchandise in transit. Shippers 
welcome the reduced sailing times but worry about the safety of seafarers, the 
consequences of re-diversion and potential for damage to freight, said James 
Hookham, director of the forum.

“If shipping lines have been assured of safe transit, they could be a bit more 
forthcoming with their customers,” he said in an email to Bloomberg. “Otherwise 
it does feel they are crossing the road against the red light.”
