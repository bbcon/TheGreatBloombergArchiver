Can the European Union extend further into the Atlantic? That’s a question 
Icelanders might be about to help answer as they vote on whether to restart 
talks to join.

For the 27-member bloc, the windswept volcanic island in the middle of its 
neighboring ocean might seem small fry. Iceland’s economy is tiny, and its 
400,000 or so inhabitants amount to less than a midsized city in a massive 
regional club whose population exceeds 450 million.

But the stakes for Brussels in Saturday’s referendum aren’t so low. One of 
Europe’s richest nations on a per capita basis, Iceland stands out from other 
possible applicants as a prestige project for the EU. And enlargement, focused 
on the poorer east,has halted 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-05/eu-pushes-to-end-expansion-deadlock-to-curb-russia-and-china?cmpid=BBD082726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8122>
 since the last expansion to Croatia 13 years ago.

Meanwhile, Iceland’s strategic position and traditionally stronger American 
cultural ties underscore the sensitivity of its status, not least after a 
diplomatic crisis this year over US President Donald Trump’s obsession with 
Greenland, a semi-autonomous part of the Kingdom of Denmark.

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/iceland-s-pro-and-anti-eu-camps-tie-in-poll-ahead-of-referendum?cmpid=BBD082726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8122>

The key question for voters is fishing, with Iceland’s catches far exceeding 
those of any member of the bloc. That issue and agriculture are likely to be 
the trickiest parts of any accession talks, according to Prime Minister 
Kristrún Frostadóttir.

There’s also the matter of the krona, because joining could eventually require 
the country to ditch its currency and adopt the euro. That step that would 
necessitate “deep reforms” of its labor market, according to its central bank.

Voters might wonder if it’s worth the bother, since Iceland already has access 
to the EU single market as part of its membership of the European Economic 
Area. The bloc takes two-thirds of its exports, while the share of US is at 9%, 
according to WTOdata <https://ttd.wto.org/en/profiles/iceland>.

Opinion surveys point to a very close race 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/icelandic-backers-of-eu-talks-may-score-narrow-win-in-referendum?cmpid=BBD082726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8122>
. The latest one, published Wednesday and conducted by pollster Maskina, shows 
the lead of the “yes” camp at just 2.6 percentage points. The business lobby’s 
own polling shows 66% of member companies are against joining the EU.

Dreamers of a much bigger bloc will be watching the outcome closely: After 
all, if Brussels can extend its reach to the mid-Atlantic, why stop there?

Sweden’s premier, Ulf Kristersson, joked earlier this year about the prospect 
of Canada joining, while Finnish President Alexander Stubb even proposed 
allowing Ottawa to become a potential accession candidate.

That’s not something Canada is looking to do, according to Prime Minister Mark 
Carney. But next month, he will attend European Commission President Ursula von 
der Leyen’s State of the EU speech as a guest of honor, before addressing the 
European Parliament too.

With Canada embroiled in a bitter trade war with Trump 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/canada-us-relations-set-to-get-rockier-former-trade-negotiator-verhuel-warns?cmpid=BBD082726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8122>
, it’s hard not to notice a warming relationship with Brussels there, too.
