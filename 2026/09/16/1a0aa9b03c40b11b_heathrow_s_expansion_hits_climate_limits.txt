The UK’s impossible balancing act

By Jess Shankleman 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASoVYP9ojQ0/jess-shankleman?cmpid=BBD091626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8961>
 andOlivia Rudgard 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWBnLoYN_tg/olivia-rudgard?cmpid=BBD091626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8961>

Heathrow Airport embodies the difficulties of cutting emissions from flying 
just as authorities are looking to add a third runway that will allow the UK’s 
largest airport to meet increasing demand for air travel.

One option is to make airlines pay a government levy to cover the cost of 
cleaner fuels and carbon capture, the UK’s Climate Change Committee said today 
as it released a report on the issue. But the levy would significantly raise 
the price of plane tickets. What’s more, both sustainable aviation fuels and 
carbon capture face significant challenges and are largely unproven at 
commercial scale.

Adding a third runway to Britain’s largest airport “is not currently 
compatible with the UK’s net zero target,” Nigel Topping, chair of the CCC, 
said in a statement. “Government needs to ensure that the aviation industry 
takes responsibility for the emissions it creates.”

Air travel represents a growing share of global emissions and decarbonizing 
the sector is one of the most difficult challenges facing the energy 
transition. Airplanes are hard to electrify and lower-carbon alternatives to 
jet fuel, made from crops or waste, are expensive and insufficient to meet 
global demand.

That makes adding more flights and more emissions irreconcilable with climate 
goals, unless the industry is forced to invest heavily in engineered removals 
and lower-carbon fuels, driving up its costs and the cost of flying.

While the UK has succeeded in cutting emissions over the past 30 years, 
aviation emissions have continued to rise. Even without Heathrow’s expansion, 
emissions from aviation are forecast to continue growing between now and 2050.

The UK is already at risk of missing its goal of net zero emissions by 2050, 
in the absence of industry reform. Taken in isolation, expanding Heathrow would 
increase emissions by almost 7% by midcentury.

Under the CCC’s proposed Net Zero Aviation Pathway, the cost of a return 
ticket to New York would increase by about £400 ($539) by 2050 as airlines 
passed on costs to passengers, the CCC estimated — that’s about 6 pence to 9 
pence per mile traveled. A round trip to Alicante, Spain, would increase by 
about £150. The full costs of decarbonization should be phased in gradually, 
starting “soon,” the CCC said.

Flight prices are likely to rise over that period if capacity remains 
constrained, the CCC said, but its proposal would ensure some of those higher 
costs pay for lowering emissions rather than boosting airline profits.

The CCC also urged the government to consider a frequent-flier tax, noting 
that public panels had strongly backed one and that almost 40% of international 
flights in 2024 were taken by people in the highest-income quintile.

Europe’s summer of truth

By John Ainger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD091626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8961>
 andLaura Millan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD091626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8961>

Europe has just been through a “summer of truth” on climate change, said the 
European Commission’s President Ursula von der Leyen in her annual state of the 
European Union speech, which marks the start of the political year in the 
region.

Almost no part of the continent was spared, Von der Leyen said. The bloc’s 
economy and security largely depend on how it manages what she called “the 
tipping points of our times” — namely, climate change and artificial 
intelligence.
Ursula von der Leyen.Photographer: Jean-Christophe Verhaegen/AFP/Getty Images
“We need to step up dramatically on preparedness,” she said. “We need better 
early warning systems and better health preparedness, we need urban adaptation 
and cooling and we need to support the most vulnerable.”

The EU will set up a “climate insurance alliance” to help cover losses as 
global warming increases the frequency and intensity of droughts, floods and 
wildfires, she said. Only a quarter of catastrophe losses are privately 
insured, with the rest of the coverage therefore needing to come from national 
budgets. A climate resilience framework, due next month, will map 100 of the 
most vulnerable regions in the bloc, she added.
