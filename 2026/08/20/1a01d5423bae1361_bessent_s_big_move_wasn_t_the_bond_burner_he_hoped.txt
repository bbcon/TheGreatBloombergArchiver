Bessent’s Bond-Burner

Fiscal dominance is here. Wednesday gave us the perfect test, and it turns out 
that the US Treasury (guardian of fiscal policy) had far more power to move 
markets than the Federal Reserve (architect of monetary policy), which published
minutes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/fed-minutes-show-many-officials-said-rate-hikes-may-be-needed?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
 revealing governors are deeply divided over whether to hike rates that 
elicited minimal market response. The problem, after Treasury Secretary Scott 
Bessent made asmall but hugely symbolic 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/bessent-becomes-most-interventionist-treasury-chief-in-decades?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
 move to stop the steady rise in longer bond yields, is that even his power 
appears to be limited. To move the bond market, he will need to go beyond 
signaling and symbols and — it appears — do something drastic to change fiscal 
policy.

Bessent’s key intervention 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/long-dated-treasuries-rally-as-treasury-boosts-bond-buybacks?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
, announced as Wall Street opened, was to double the amount of 10-year and 
longer bonds that the Treasury can buy back. If you think this sounds like
something the Fed would do 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/treasury-bond-buybacks-evoke-memory-of-fed-s-operation-twist?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
, you would be right. Monetary policy ever since the Global Financial Crisis 
has revolved around quantitative easing (QE) — buying bonds to push down their 
yields.

Kevin Warsh, the new Fed chairman, wants to move away from that and shrink the 
central bank’s enormous holdings of bonds. He also wants to preserve theFed’s 
independence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2025-07-03/independence-is-the-worst-form-of-central-banking?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
. And now Bessent is using fiscal policy to achieve the same ends; the Fed may 
well be independent, but it might becomeirrelevant 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-04-24/fed-treasury-the-shape-of-a-deal-to-come?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
.

To be clear, the sums involved are far smaller. Bessent is doubling the amount 
of bonds he can purchase in a buyback under the Treasury’s program to help 
liquidity in the market (it’s not meant to be a vehicle of macroeconomic 
policy) to $4 billion from $2 billion. At peak QE after the pandemic, the Fed 
was buying $120 billion per month. This is a symbolic gesture.

But symbols can be vital declarations of intent. Bessent was telling the world 
he saw yields rising, didn’t like it, and thought it necessary to do something 
about it. The bond market reacted accordingly, with the 10-year yield dropping 
about seven basis points. That’s a strong day, but nothing exceptional — there 
have been plenty of bigger daily falls since the 2024 election:


The 30-year yield — at its highest since the eve of the crisis 
<https://www.ft.com/content/8ea6e6f2-1cfb-11dc-9b58-000b5df10621> in 2007 
—dropped eight basis points. But the upward trend remains intact. Investors 
heeded Bessent’s warning flare, but told him he would need to do much more to 
ease conditions substantially:

The blockbuster did little to move the equity market, with the S&P 500 gaining 
an unremarkable 0.21%. Stocks have continued to set new records even as bond 
yields have risen; it’s not a key driver of sentiment at present. “The big 
picture is that Treasuries have played second fiddle to AI in influencing the 
S&P 500 in recent years. And that’s likely to remain the case for a while,” 
said John Higgins of Capital Economics.

But the maneuver did have a dramatic impact on the dollar, which had its worst 
day in months and dropped below its 200-day moving average:

Gold was the clearest tell 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-18/gold-holds-losses-as-bond-selloff-hormuz-limbo-weigh-on-outlook?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
 that investors are concerned by Bessent’s move. After gaining for several 
years as part of the so-called “debasement 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2025-10-22/gold-dinger-debasement-with-a-midas-touch?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
trade 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2025-10-24/the-decline-and-fall-of-the-debasement-trade?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
” — treating it as protection against feckless monetary policy and inflation — 
gold hit what looked like a blow-off top in January and appeared to be settled 
into a decline. Wednesday’s intervention drove the metal’s best day in six 
months, bringing it back up to the 200-day moving average:

Why Bessent Is Boosting Treasury Buy-Backs
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/audio/2026-08-19/big-take-why-bessent-is-boosting-treasury-buy-backs-podcast?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
Why so damaging to the currency? Robin Brooks of the Brookings Institute drew 
a parallel toJapan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-03/the-us-steps-in-to-back-the-yen-in-rare-joint-intervention?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
, where attempts to put a cap on rising yields have seen investors punish the 
yen as the only way to register their concern about its fiscal problems:
If you artificially suppress risk premia in long-term government bond yields, 
that obviously morphs into currency depreciation, which is exactly where Japan 
has ended up.The key signal Bessent sent is that the rise in long yields is 
unwelcome, but the US is unwilling to address the underlying problem, which is 
rising debt and a very large deficit.
That feeds into a lack of confidence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/jpmorgan-team-sees-credibility-risk-in-treasury-s-bond-buybacks?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
. As Jean Ergas of Tigress Financial Partners puts it, the move “smacked of 
desperation,” while the market response was “a vote of no-confidence in the 
Treasury and the administration.”

The US controversially joined with Japan recently in an attempt to prop up the 
yen, and did so by selling reserves of euros. To support the yen against the 
dollar, Japan needs to sell Treasuries, and thus many traders believe that the
US got involved 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-08-03/how-safe-is-the-yen-under-the-us-umbrella?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
 not to help Tokyo but to avert more bond-selling. George Saravelos, foreign 
exchange strategist at Deutsche Bank AG, offers a list of reasons why this move 
hit the dollar:
First, we see this, as well as the discouragement of intervention 
<https://research.db.com/research/Article?rid=01M08727MWKPQH6X0SKYZT6D1T&kid=RP0001&documentType=R&wt_cc1=IND-1823-4112>
 from Japan earlier this month, as signs of increasing administration unease on 
the ongoing rise in long-end US yields.Second, we see the buyback... as 
soft-form financial repression aimed at containing the long-end of the US yield 
curve.Third, we see both developments as negative for the dollar. If the market 
price of USTs is not “allowed” to adjust down, the foreign exchange price of 
UST owned by foreign investors has to adjust via a weakening in the dollar.
Fourth...to the extent that this eases financial conditions, it would arguably 
necessitate an offsetting tightening from the Federal Reserve.
More maneuvers like this will likely weaken the dollar further, and increase 
the pressure on a central bank whose independence is currently in question. It 
remains hard to see what gets bond yields down other than an unequivocal move 
to reduce the deficit. The more it hurts politically, the more seriously it 
will be taken. Without any pain, it appears, yields aren’t coming down.

Moderna Life

Healthcare equities have lacked a certain je ne sais quoi for a while now. The 
momentum inspired by the 2020 breakthrough in Covid vaccines and the later boom 
in GLP-1 drugs eased, and then went into reverse after ChatGPT produced another 
set of star stocks to follow. Those blockbuster medications, especially the 
weight-loss drugs available as injectables and then pills, have had a great 
effect on public health — but not on healthcare share prices.

The relative performance of the MSCI All-World Health Care Index against the 
broader benchmark shows just how much the luster has been dulled. Since ChatGPT 
arrived in 2022, healthcare’s slide has been inexorable:

But investors never abandoned the quest for the next blockbuster, and 
Wednesday’s reaction to favorable results from Moderna Inc.’s cancer vaccine 
trial suggested that they truly believe they’ve found it. The S&P 500 Health 
Care index was up over 4% to fresh highs on the day, while Nasdaq’s 
Biotechnology gauge gained as much as 5.6%:

Moderna itself was something else 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/moderna-merck-mrna-shot-helped-cut-melanoma-recurrence-in-big-trial?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
. Its share price soared as much as 176% on the news, more than triple its 
biggest move even in the days when it looked as though it might be the company 
to save the world from Covid. Investors have a stronger understanding of 
cancer, a risk that will never go away. The importance of the trial of the 
personalized cancer vaccine developed with Merck & Co. that helped reduce 
melanoma recurrence is hard to overstate.

It was obviously hard to spot coming, because it caught many investors 
unawares. Moderna’s rallygenerated paper losses of about $5 billion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/moderna-s-160-surge-burns-shorts-in-painful-5-billion-loss?cmpid=BBD082026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7907>
 for short sellers, bringing their mark-to-market decline to more than $7 
billion this year, according to data from S3 Partners LLC.

Wednesday’s rally came after a 110% gain for this year even before the 
melanoma trial results, which had been driven by hopes that the company’s flu 
vaccine would diversify it away from the declining Covid immunization business. 
The latest milestone for individualized cancer therapy, however, shows that 
treatments engineered around a patient’s specific tumor profile can outperform 
standard-of-care immunotherapy.

We still don’t know if it will be truly the next big thing, in either the 
battle against cancer or — far less importantly — in the stock market, but the 
signals are encouraging so far.

Myles Minter of William Blair argues that Moderna “has a clear line of sight 
to revenue diversification from the Covid business.” After suffering four 
straight years of losses – plunging 94% below its 2021 peak as demand for its 
Covid vaccine waned — it seems to be back. The trial’s success coincides with a 
rally in the Tema Oncology exchange-traded fund:

This may be based on the hope that the trial’s success could speed up 
development across a broad oncology pipeline, particularly for Moderna’s 
rivals, such as BioNTech SE, who are also working on personalized cancer 
vaccines. But it’s not just about the specifics of product development. The 
sector was already in strong shape, becoming a key beneficiary of the rotation 
out of semiconductors since June 1, as Longview Economics illustrates:

Investors have also rewarded second-quarter earnings, with 53 of the 55 
healthcare stocks in the S&P 500 beating expectations. StoneX’s Vincent Deluard 
expects that outperformance to continue:
The healthcare sector should benefit from political gridlock in the next two 
years. Neither party has the appetite or support to launch major reforms; the 
two parties will happily blame high healthcare costs on the other, while doing 
nothing to reduce them.
Bloomberg Intelligence’s Duane Wright sees additional tailwinds from greater 
clarity around drug pricing, which he argues has not been as adverse as 
previously feared, as well as signs that healthcare utilization among insurers 
is stabilizing, even if only in the short term.

On the other side of the ledger remain the Trump administration’s tighter 
Medicaid work requirements, enacted under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. The
rules cleared a legal hurdle 
<https://www.ajmc.com/view/judge-denies-preliminary-injunction-in-medicaid-work-requirements-case>
 last month after a federal judge denied states’ request for a preliminary 
injunction, raising enrollment risks for Medicaid-heavy managed-care plans and 
leaving a lingering overhang for companies with significant Medicaid exposure.

—Richard Abbey
