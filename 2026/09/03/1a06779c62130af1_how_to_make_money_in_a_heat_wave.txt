The rise of home electricity traders

By Akshat Rathi 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUdOobfNzkQ/akshat-rathi?cmpid=BBD090326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8493>
 andOlivia Rudgard 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWBnLoYN_tg/olivia-rudgard?cmpid=BBD090326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8493>

For many Europeans, this summer’s heat was a powerful reminder of the damage 
climate change has wrought — from illness and devastated crops to high energy 
bills. For Andrew Austin, a 34-year-old homeowner in Shropshire, England, the 
stubbornly high temperatures were achance to make some money 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-03/consumers-profit-off-solar-batteries-selling-excess-energy-back-to-grid?cmpid=BBD090326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8493>
.

Power demand was rising as London crossed 34C (93F) on June 24. Energy prices 
spiked as the sun set and solar panels across the UK stopped generating 
electricity. That’s when Austin knew he could take advantage of his 200 
kilowatt-hour home battery, which he had charged up using cheap power from the 
grid during the daytime. He sold the power back to the grid for £60 ($82) from 
4 p.m. to 9 p.m. In just a few hours, Austin had earned about as much as an 
apartment dweller would typically pay for electricity every month.
Mark Purcell, an electrical engineer, next to his electric car and 40kWh solar 
battery in Queensland, Australia. He buys energy when prices are low, stores 
it, then sells it back to his utility for a profit.Photographer: Dan 
Peled/Bloomberg
All this happened without Austin having to press a single button. He has set 
up a computer to conduct this energy trading while he goes about his life as 
normal. The computer downloads electricity prices for every 30-minute period 
from his utility Octopus Energy, which publishes them at 4 p.m. each day. That 
allows him to know when electricity will be cheap to charge his batteries, and 
when power prices on the grid are high, the computer sells it back to the grid.

Many parts of the world, especially Europe, are struggling with high 
electricity bills after the wars in Ukraine and the Middle East caused oil and 
natural-gas prices to soar. At the same time, the cost of electricity 
technologies manufactured mainly in China is falling. It’s why tens of millions 
of people areswitching to electric options 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-energy-transition-iran-war/?srnd=undefined&sref=jjXJRDFv&cmpid=BBD090326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8493>
 like solar panels and batteries, regardless of whether they worry about 
climate change.

Austin’s going one step further — not just offsetting his energy bills, but 
making money. He’s taking advantage of an opportunity that used to be available 
only to professional traders: arbitrage on volatile electricity markets. He 
views the current market volatility very differently than many consumers. “To 
me, it’s not a crisis, it’s an opportunity,” he says.


The history of retail energy markets can be summed up as one transaction 
repeated trillions of times: Companies sell consumers fuel to burn. That basic 
relationship is now changing for the first time, as millions of people use 
their solar panels and batteries to generate energy and sell excess back to the 
grid.

For an increasing fraction of those millions, it means buying electricity from 
the grid when it’s cheap or generating it using solar panels for free, then
storing it in batteries 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-04-19/cheap-batteries-are-taking-over-the-world-s-power-grids?cmpid=BBD090326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8493>
 for a few hours. When power prices are high, typically after the sun goes 
down, it means discharging the battery and selling the power back to the grid — 
making a tidy sum in return. Europe, the US and Australia are just a few places 
where consumers are making this shift.

China’s waning appetite for oil

By Lili Pike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXt16hTqABg/lili-pike?cmpid=BBD090326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8493>

The millions of Chinese drivers who turned to charging stations over gas pumps 
as the Iran War drove up fuel prices have helped shift the country’s climate 
trajectory.

China’s oil consumption fell 9% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, 
driven by a 16% slump in the transport sector, according to a report from the 
Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air. Crude oil processing dropped 11%. 
Electric vehicles were a major beneficiary as gasoline and diesel became more 
expensive.

Although coal use at power stations continued to rise, the decline in oil 
demand helped trim total emissions by 1% — the first time that oil rather than 
coal has been responsible for a drop in China, CREA said.

The shift is significant. If the marginal drop 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-12/china-s-emissions-fell-last-year-in-first-decline-since-2022?cmpid=BBD090326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8493>
 in emissions last year can be sustained, it would put Beijing well ahead of 
its commitment to peak carbon before 2030.
