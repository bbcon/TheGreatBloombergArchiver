Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Mitchell Ferman looks at the next big test for Shell’s CEO after 
bumper earnings.

Shell Plc has shown it knows how to make money when energy markets go haywire. 
Chief Executive Officer Wael Sawan’s next act is turning that cash into 
sustainable growth.

The CEO spent his first three years in the job tightening spending, cutting 
costs and strengthening the balance sheet while returning billions of dollars 
to investors. As a result, Shell has outperformed its biggest peers on total 
shareholder returns since he took over.

Now, war and supply disruptions are supercharging Shell. The company just 
posted itssecond-highest quarterly profit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/shell-reports-second-highest-profit-ever-as-refining-hits-record?cmpid=BBBXT102926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10731>
 ever, allowing it to lift its share buyback to $5 billion, on par with 
industry leader ExxonMobil Holdings Corp.

With cash pouring in and net debt falling, Sawan has more financial firepower 
than ever to pursue what has become a key challenge for Shell — growth. For all 
its recent gains, the company still trades at a hefty valuation discount to 
faster-growing US rivals Chevron Corp. and ExxonMobil.

Some large investors have told Bloomberg they want Sawan to drive sustainable 
growth, but not chase extra barrels just for the sake of it. Financial 
discipline should be paired with a clearer plan for the next phase of capital 
deployment and long-term expansion, they said.

Investors should get a clearer idea of the CEO’s plan when Shell holds its 
first capital markets day update for two years in February.

Shell has laid some groundwork for growth and shown an appetite to spend for 
it. The company’s $13.6 billionpurchase of ARC Resources Ltd. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-27/shell-agrees-to-buy-canada-s-arc-resources-for-13-6-billion?cmpid=BBBXT102926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10731>
 adds about 370,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day of production, while it 
recently approved with partners a$23 billion expansion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-29/shell-s-lng-canada-to-proceed-with-multibillion-dollar-expansion?cmpid=BBBXT102926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10731>
 of a liquefied natural gas project in Canada.

But the purse strings aren’t loosening too much.

Shell wants normalized free cash flow per share — a measure of the cash its 
business generates after spending, adjusted for commodity-price swings and 
other short-term factors — to grow by more than 10% through 2030. It also wants 
to keep annual investment around $20 billion to $22 billion for the next two 
years.

The question is how much further Sawan can deliver growth and narrow the gap 
with American rivals without losing the spending discipline investors have 
rewarded.

—Mitchell Ferman, Bloomberg News
