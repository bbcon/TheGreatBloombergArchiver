When Good News on Inflation Is Bad

To cut a long story short <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JE2sCISQmpE>, the 
fed funds rate will rise this week. Following Friday’s inflation data for 
August, there can be no sensible doubt about this.

After surprisingly strong August employment data, and new Federal Reserve 
Chair Kevin Warsh’s strong words at Jackson Hole last month that inflation was 
still too high, the prices data have settled the issue. They’ve also completed 
a startling transformation in Fed perceptions since the eve of that speech:

This is true because Warsh has loaded the scales toward a hike, needing to see 
clear improvement in inflation to avoid one. That hasn’t happened, and with the 
bond market now in a turbulent mood, not hiking could leave him looking silly. 
Credibility is easily lost, and he needs to go through with a hike to maintain 
it, particularly in current conditions.

Warsh has set out a stall against giving forward guidance. But ironically, 
August’s numbers might justify keeping rates where they are, if only he’d been 
less explicit at Jackson Hole. This is our usual breakdown of inflation into 
its four main components. Core inflation is up, but only very slightly, core 
goods inflation (where we would expect to see tariffs show up) is tiny, and the 
overall rate of inflation is barely changed. So no worsening, but a clear 
improvement it is not:

The energy crisis, with the diesel price now at an all-time record, has much 
to do with this. Various measures of underlying inflation suggest that August 
saw the ongoing improvement that might have justified leaving rates on hold. 
Trimmed mean CPI, compiled by the Cleveland Fed, which excludes outliers in 
both directions, is its lowest since early 2021, while sticky prices (which are 
costly to move and rarely decrease) as measured by the Atlanta Fed, also 
slowed. The same is true of the median. The Fed’s “supercore” measure of 
services excluding housing did rise slightly, back to the upper bound of 3%. 
But steady, if painfully slow, disinflation continues:

There’s also one important price series that can explain August’s 
disappointing result. Wireless telephony charges are in long-term decline. Last 
month, they inflated the most since 2021:

According to Omair Sharif of Inflation Insights LLC, this on its own added 10 
basis points to core inflation. It’s chiefly responsible for the rise in the 
supercore:
To be sure, the core rate would have been far more modest without this 
extraordinary rise. That said, I am not sure the Fed can play that game right 
now with odds of a hike pushing to the 90%-ish zone.
A further illustration that these numbers aren’t that bad is to see how many 
CPI components exceeded the 3% upper bound target. The exercise, carried out by 
Robin Brooks of the Brookings Institute below, shows that the proportion with 
strongly above-target inflation dropped sharply last month. It’s a bit above 
lows from the turn of the year, but shows, again, that August was a month of 
progress on inflation:

If the Fed’s response now appears clear, it’s because of the overriding 
importance of the bond market, which is threatening ariot 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hamKl-su8PE>. Treasury Secretary Scott 
Bessent’s gambit to deploy increased bond buybacks to keep longer-term yields 
in check appeared last week to have failed. The 10-year yield leapt after the 
long-heraldeddetails of the buyback were announced on Wednesday 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-09/treasuries-weaken-as-soaring-oil-prices-revive-rate-hike-worries?cmpid=BBD091426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8818>
 — and weren’t as great as some had expected — and again on Thursday when it 
emerged that theTreasury hadn’t bought all the bonds that it could have 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-10/treasury-buys-less-than-maximum-in-longer-debt-buyback-operation?cmpid=BBD091426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8818>
 under its new mandate. Long yields matter, and the overriding purpose of the 
Fed must now be to avoid pushing them higher, for the sake of the housing 
market above all. The chart below shows the relationship between shares of 
homebuilders, a direct expression of housing market perceptions, and the 
30-year mortgage benchmark bond yield:

The good news for housing, and for Bessent, is that in the immediate aftermath 
of Friday’s data, the 10-year Treasury yield fell while stocks had a good day. 
Inflation is bad for bonds; but it was seen as initially encouraging that the 
data more or less ensured that the central bank would be hiking. Any hikes now 
improve the chances of averting inflation in the longer term, and nibble away 
at the “debasement trade” — the bet that feckless government policies will 
erode the value of the dollar.

The positive reception didn’t last, however, and the 10-year yield ended the 
week at a three-year high, and a whisker away from returning to territory it 
last saw in 2007.As detailed last week 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-09-10/bessent-dares-bond-traders-to-burn-down-the-house?cmpid=BBD091426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8818>
, what happens when and if it goes through that watershed could be crucial:

Komal Sri-Kumar of the SriKonomics newsletter 
<https://srikonomics.substack.com/> argued that bond markets had called 
Bessent’s bluff:
Investors reasoned that buying back a modest quantity of older securities 
while issuing vastly larger sums of new debt would change the maturity 
composition of the debt but not make the financing burden disappear. The 
federal deficit is still running at close to $2 trillion per year, and the 
tariff campaign is expanding.
He argues for the Fed to consider a “jumbo” hike of 50 basis points as a mere 
25 basis points might look more of “an acknowledgement of the problem rather 
than a determination to solve it.”

He’s not alone. Steven Blitz of TS Lombard argues: “Given Warsh’s disdain for 
the Bernanke slow-walk pace of hiking, why not surprise the market with a 50bps 
jump – leaving aside, for the moment, the resultingfracture of his relationship 
with Trump.” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-13/trump-pushes-fed-to-lower-rates-despite-pressure-to-raise-them?cmpid=BBD091426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8818>

The reason for not doing so is counterintuitive. To rein in inflation caused 
by strong federal spending, Blitz argues, the need is for lower equity prices. 
That makes capital harder to come by, slows the buildout of artificial 
intelligence, and removes inflationary heat. The way to do that, even if 
Bessent will not say so, is to allow the 10-year yield to rise enough to choke 
off the stock market. That’s more likely if the Fed doesn’t hike aggressively 
this week.

These are perilous times. Even though August’s inflation data weren’t in fact 
that bad.

Collateral Damage

The Treasury yield commands attention, but the global shift in yields could 
have its greatest impact in other jurisdictions where investors worry that 
government debt is out of control. The most worrying example is France.

French politics has been logjammed for two years, since snap elections left 
the legislature roughly equally divided between three political blocs. That 
gridlock won’t end before next spring’s presidential election, which might well 
at last give Marine Le Pen’s far-right Rassemblement National a turn in power.

The issue is whether the fiscal concerns boil over into a market crisis before 
or after the election. The spread of French 10-year OATS over equivalent German 
bunds is close to 1%, higher than during other political upheavals of the last 
10 years. In absolute terms, the cost of financing French government debt is 
its highest since the crisis of 2008:

This has the hallmarks of an ungodly mess in the making. If Bessent doesn’t 
tame the Treasury market, the worst effects may well be felt in France.

A Bond Funfair

The flood of artificial intelligence debt is pressuring spreads, with 
AI-related spending helping push totalUS convertible bond issuance 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TL5BNQT9NJLU?cmpid=BBD091426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8818>
 to a record $131 billion in 2026. So far this year, tech convertible bonds, 
which give investors the option to swap their debt for equity, have returned 
53%, but the strain is growing visible in other corners of the debt market. 
Spreads for non-investment-grade bonds have begun to widen:

Six months ago, high-yield spreads peaked before compressing to about 270 
basis points. Goldman Sachs’ Miriam Wheeler told Bloomberg: “Most yields are 
trading below their new issue price, and that is a combination of heavy supply 
and the rates having some impact.”

By some estimates, US high-grade bond supply should approach $2 trillion this 
year. Barclays says this would top the 2020 record of $1.66 trillion. So far, 
the heavy supply has yet to cause a sustained widening in spreads. However, 
stress indicators are mounting. Within the IG universe, tech bonds generally 
have wider spreads as hyperscalers sort out how to monetize the new technology.

Until now, spread dislocations caused by the heavy issuance have tended to 
last only a couple of days before spreads narrow again thanks to continuing 
demand — which also adds to the pressure on Treasuries to offer a more 
competitive yield. To quote Maria Giraldo of Guggenheim Partners:
That confidence isn’t just a mood. It’s really supported by fundamentals. 
Given how strong corporate earnings growth has been, US companies seem to have 
weathered a war, inflation, AI disruption, and mixed trends across consumer 
cohorts.
The yield surge following the Treasury buyback raises fresh questions. Is AI 
corporate issuance “almost yield-agnostic” — meaning borrowers think the 
returns justify whatever they pay, asBessent argued after 
<https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/20/cnbc-exclusive-transcript-us-treasury-secretary-scott-bessent-speaks-with-cnbcs-sara-eisen-on-squawk-on-the-street-today.html>
 the buyback announcement? Probably. Even as Treasury yields have climbed to 
multi-year highs, AI issuance powers on.

The supply is premised on the logic that the return on investment in AI should 
be so high that the cost of debt is essentially irrelevant. That assumption can 
only hold to an extent, and it is increasingly hitting its limits as investor 
fatigue sets in and new-issue concessions creep higher. Growing expectations of 
tighter policy are another headwind. That’s the part the yield-agnostic thesis 
never really answers: Issuers may not care what they pay, but someone on the 
other side has to want the paper at that price.

—Richard Abbey
