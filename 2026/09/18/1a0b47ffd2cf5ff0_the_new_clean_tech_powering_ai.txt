Hyperscalers back ultra-long storage

By Coco Liu 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AU-LNn94_HY/coco-liu?cmpid=BBD091826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=9067>

Soaring energy demand for artificial intelligence has spurred billions of 
dollars in clean energy investments 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-18/meta-google-invest-in-long-duration-batteries-to-power-ai?cmpid=BBD091826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=9067>
, such as in next-generation geothermal and advanced nuclear reactors. Now, 
hyperscalers are boosting yet another clean technology: long-lasting batteries.

Developers are starting to add these batteries, known as long-duration energy 
storage, to data centers. As they do that, there’s a real chance to scale them 
and unlock more renewable energy, experts say.

“Long-duration storage is an important addition to a growing toolkit that can 
help us build AI infrastructure at unprecedented scale,” said Cully Cavness, 
cofounder and president at Crusoe, which builds and operates AI data centers 
for tech giants.
A rendering of Energy Dome’s CO2 Battery project near Rhode, County Offaly, 
Ireland.Source: Business Wire
Long-duration energy storage is an umbrella term for technologies capable of 
storing and releasing electricity for many hours or even days. Conventional 
lithium-ion batteries, in contrast, only hold power for a few hours.

Historically, facilities have relied on a mix of diesel generators, natural 
gas turbines and conventional batteries to keep operations running during grid 
failures or when renewable power lulls. But adding new fossil-fuel capacity 
threatens tech companies’ pledges to cut carbon emissions at a time when AI’s
climate toll 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-01/big-tech-s-carbon-emissions-spike-with-runaway-growth-of-ai?cmpid=BBD091826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=9067>
 is growing. The installation of gas turbines is also bogged down by persistent 
supply chain bottlenecks, leaving developers with multiyear wait times for new 
orders.

Truck tampering — fellons or victims?

By Leslie Kaufman 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUZ8izNmriE/leslie-kaufman?cmpid=BBD091826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=9067>

Matthew Geouge built a lucrative business selling technology that, among other 
things, allowed owners of Ford diesel pickups to boost their horsepower while 
disabling the pollution controls required under the Clean Air Act. The federal 
government pursued his company, Spartan Diesel Technologies, in a civil action, 
ultimately winning a $4.15 million default judgment in 2018. But it didn’t stop 
there.

US prosecutors later accused Geouge of continuing to tune engines illegally at 
another company. He pleaded guilty in 2021 to conspiring to violate the Clean 
Air Act and defraud the US, as well as to tax evasion, and was sentenced to a 
year and a day in prison. He served four-and-a-half months in 2022.

On July 3, President Donald Trump pardoned him 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-18/trump-pardons-people-convicted-of-diesel-emissions-tampering?cmpid=BBD091826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260918&utm_content=9067>
.
Matthew Geouge.Source: Spartan Truckworks
Geouge was among nine people granted clemency that day for Clean Air Act 
convictions involving vehicle emissions. Trump pardoned additional defendants 
in similar cases in September, marking a sharp reversal in the federal 
government’s treatment of an aftermarket industry built around disabling 
pollution controls. Months earlier, Trump’s Department of Justice had told 
prosecutors to stop bringing criminal cases involving so-called “defeat 
devices.” The president cast the pardons as relief for people prosecuted for 
simply “fixing their car.”

As US air pollution rules have gotten stricter, automakers have installed 
equipment in diesel trucks to stifle their emissions. But an illegal 
aftermarket to undo these alterations quickly sprung up and spread. Software 
developers wrote new engine calibrations, and manufacturers produced hardware. 
Online distributors sold defeat devices, and repair shops installed them.

Some truck owners wanted more horsepower; others were looking for a way around 
early pollution-control systems that could be unreliable and expensive to 
repair; still others felt government regulation infringed on their personal 
freedoms. Regardless of the motivation, the upshot is dirtier air: The 
Environmental Protection Agency estimated in 2020 that emissions controls had 
been fully removed from more than 550,000 diesel pickups since 2009 — about 15% 
of the trucks originally certified with the equipment.

Defeat devices sold between 2009 and 2020 for certain vehicle models would 
lead to them releasing an estimated 570,000 additional tons of nitrogen oxides 
and 5,000 more tons of particulate matter over the vehicles’ lifetimes, the EPA 
calculated — the pollution equivalent of adding 9 million compliant diesel 
trucks to US roads. Nitrogen oxides contribute to smog and can aggravate asthma 
and other respiratory illnesses, while fine particulate pollution is linked to 
cardiovascular and respiratory disease and premature death.

White House spokesperson Lauren Bis, in response to a request for comment, 
pointed to a social media post by Trump describing the pardonees as having been 
“persecuted” by the Biden administration. “I AM SETTING THEM ALL FREE, RIGHT 
NOW!” Trump wrote.
