Hi, this is Paul Abelsky in Hong Kong.

China’s government this week finally opened the stimulus taps slightly, 
rolling out a plan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/china-studies-policies-to-boost-economy-steady-housing-market?cmpid=BBD100226_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9371>
 to restore momentum to the flailing economy.

Beijing’s biggest stimulus effort since September 2024 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-09-27/xi-s-big-stimulus-week-aims-to-draw-a-line-under-china-slowdown?cmpid=BBD100226_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9371>
 includes mortgage subsidies as well as expanded central bank lending for 
infrastructure, technology and other targeted sectors. The Finance Ministry has 
also pledged a more targeted fiscal boost, such as more interest-rate subsidies 
to help consumption, and deploying unused local government bond quotas to fund 
investment.

The stimulus package appears designed to reach China’s economic growth target 
of 4.5%-5% this year, rather than deliver a broad revival, leaving investors 
waiting for more aid to address the country’s underlying demand weakness. It’s 
“a nudge, not a game changer,” Barclays analysts led by Yingke Zhou said in a 
report. “While the policies selectively lower financing costs for the real 
economy, China’s key macro challenge is not the cost of credit – it is the 
demand for credit.”
Chinese President Xi Jinping on the eve of the 77th anniversary of the 
founding of the People’s Republic of China at the Great Hall of the People in 
Beijing on Sept 30.(Photo by Xie Huanchi/Xinhua via Getty Images)
The government had effectively been in austerity mode, with the economy at risk
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/china-s-consumption-and-investment-wilt-as-factories-power-ahead?cmpid=BBD100226_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9371>
 of growing more slowly than the full-year target for the second straight 
quarter. A broad measure of government expenditure had fallen 3.5% in the first 
eight months, helping explain the historic fall in fixed-asset investment that 
began in mid-2025 and intensified in recent months.

While the government scrutinizes spending and looks to rein in local debts, 
years of slumping property prices have also depleted revenue from land sales. 
Desperate to find other sources of funds, officials this year have gone into 
overdrive toextract more taxes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-25/xi-s-unpredecented-tax-clawback-hammers-chinese-listed-companies?cmpid=BBD100226_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9371>
 from billionaires and companies.

They’ve rewritten the rules on offshore trusts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-24/china-targets-offshore-trusts-in-sweeping-tax-clampdown?cmpid=BBD100226_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9371>
, launched the biggest raid in years for back taxes on local firms and set 
their sights on the more than $1.7 trillion of wealth that Chinese residents 
hold in Hong Kong alone. And they’ve also formeda series of special squads 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-27/china-s-tax-crackdown-squeezes-billionaires-harder-than-ever?cmpid=BBD100226_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9371>
 dedicated to tracking the rich and boosting tax receipts.

The country’s wealthiest have been given until late October to pay bills on 
their overseas trusts without facing extra charges, leaving them scrambling to 
figure out how much they owe.

Unsurprisingly, such efforts have paid off. In the first eight months of the 
year, tax revenue is up 6.6% from 2025 — more than double the increase the 
government projected for all of 2026. Helped by higher energy prices and a 
rebound in producer inflation, fiscal income under the general public budget — 
the largest among the government’s four books — has rebounded after a 
contraction last year, also coming in well ahead of the plan.

On the other side of the ledger, officials are still trying to figure out how 
they can spend every penny more effectively. Consumer spendingremains lackluster
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-27/china-s-consumer-stocks-face-lost-decade-as-ai-steals-spotlight?cmpid=BBD100226_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9371>
 despite a temporary boost from government subsidies, hurting growth and 
shares. While gross domestic product notched a 4.7% gain in the first half — 
comfortably within the official target for the year — the expansion dropped off 
sharply in the second quarter. Officials earliervowed to study fresh measures 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/china-studies-policies-to-boost-economy-steady-housing-market?cmpid=BBD100226_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9371>
 to promote employment, boost household income and stabilize the property 
market, which is mired in a yearslong slump.

With exports booming so much that they are masking the slowdown at home — and 
major central banks entering the hiking cycle — most economists no longer 
expect to see an interest rate cut this year.

In just over two weeks, China will report its GDP data for the third quarter. 
That will give investors another window to see whether Beijing’s modest 
stimulus so far have been effective, or such policy prudence has left officials 
behind the curve.

Tariff Cuts Expose Limits to Non-Sensitive Trade

From American coal and corn to Chinese toys and kitchenware, Beijing and 
Washington have drawn up a plan to cut tariffs on about $30 billion worth of 
imports from one another.

Negotiated under the newly operationalized Board of Trade, the reciprocal 
tariff reductions are among the most tangible outcomes of last week’s 
pageantry-filled summit between Donald Trump and Xi Jinping in Washington.

Yet even this modest breakthrough underscores the constraints the superpower 
rivals face in carving out areas of non-sensitive commerce from a relationship 
increasingly defined by economic and technological competition.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-09-26/the-china-trade-problem-that-tariffs-alone-can-t-fix-video?cmpid=BBD100226_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9371>
Michael Froman, former US Trade Representative in the Obama administration and 
now president of the Council on Foreign Relations, tells us why Xi Jinping sees 
a consumer-led economy as a political threat.
For a start, the roughly $60 billion in two-way trade expected to benefit is a 
small share of the $415 billion in total goods exchanged between the world’s 
two largest economies last year.

“While these goods will receive favorable tariff treatment — a clear signal of 
diplomatic goodwill — the macroeconomic scale is negligible,” said Chang Shu, 
chief Asia economist at Bloomberg Economics. “The composition of 
‘non-sensitive’ goods is heavily ring-fenced, confined to mostly agricultural 
products and medical devices for US exports, and low-end consumer goods for 
Chinese exports.”

A closer look at the product lists proposed by both sides further illustrate 
those limits.

The US list covers just 77 product entries, including fireworks, household 
goods, sporting equipment. China’s side is broader, spanning 1,619 items 
including meat, seafood, dairy products, grains, coal, timber and medical 
equipment. But many of the American products on China’s list barely register in 
bilateral trade — nearly 60% recorded no sales to China in 2025 or 2024.

Most telling is what the list leaves out: soybeans, the single largest 
American agricultural export to China.

Beijing has previously committed to buying 25 million metric tons from the US 
annually through 2028. But that purchasing push is being spearheaded by China’s 
giant state-owned agricultural traders, while the private sector has largely 
stayed on the sidelines because higher tariffs have made the trade commercially 
unattractive.

Keeping soybean purchases on a separate track outside the Board of Trade could 
allow Beijing to preserve an important source of leverage over Trump, as the US 
heads toward November’s midterm elections.

“Beijing is well aware that soybean purchase will have a direct impact on 
agricultural states in the US, which are Trump’s voter base,” said Feng 
Chucheng, founding partner of Beijing-based Hutong Research. “So despite 
soybean being a non-sensitive item in trade, the political significance of 
China’s soybean purchase is enormous and carries major political implications.”

Neither side has said when the tariff cuts will take effect, with both still 
needing to complete procedures required under their domestic laws. Still, the 
agreement represents one of the few concrete — if limited — steps toward 
stabilizing a trade truce that has just been extended for another two months.

Behind the Great Firewall

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

Japan’s victory over China in men’s team table tennis 
<https://english.news.cn/20260924/f3596b479faf48c9a8399e9f2995f4e3/c.html> at 
the Asian Games last week ended a winning streak that had lasted more than 
three decades.

The result sparked soul-searching among Chinese fans, with reactions ranging 
from disappointment and anger to calls for greater respect toward players.

Many fans pointed to what they saw as a weakening talent pipeline, with one 
user on Instagram-like Xiaohongshu concluding that “each generation just isn’t 
as good as the last, we must face reality.”

Japanese player Tomokazu Harimoto’s post-match remarks added fuel to the 
debate. The Japanese player said the victory showed Japan had overcome its 
inferiority complex.

“That we beat them was proof that we had lacked confidence before,” said 
Harimoto, according to The Japan Times 
<https://www.japantimes.co.jp/sports/2026/09/25/asian-games/japan-china-table-tennis/>
. “China used to have six or seven strong players, that’s why their era lasted 
10 or 20 years,” said Harimoto.

China’s team featured Wang Chuqin, ranked number one 
<https://www.worldtabletennis.com/rankings> in the world, along with teammates 
who are ranked eighth and 15th. Japan had two top-five players compete.

The upset also highlighted the impact of fan culture and online scrutiny on 
athletes. Former Chinese table tennis playerCheng Jingqi said 
<https://k.sina.com.cn/article_7879923909_m1d5ae18c503301nydk.html> Wang Chuqin 
faces immense pressure from criticism.

“There is an atmosphere created where he cannot lose, and wins are taken for 
granted. These kinds of public opinion is not friendly toward him,” Cheng said 
during a television program. Cheng warned that similar public pressure has hurt 
Chinese athletes in the past and urged fans to show greater kindness online.
