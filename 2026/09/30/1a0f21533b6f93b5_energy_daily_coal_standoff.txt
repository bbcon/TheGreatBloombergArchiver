Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Indonesia-based reporter Eddie Spence looks at an impasse in the seaborne coal 
market.

Coal markets are caught in a standoff.

Benchmark Chinese thermal coal prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/china-coal-prices-have-jumped-by-almost-a-quarter-since-mid-july?cmpid=BBBXT093026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9517>
 are at a three-year high after climbing for 11 weeks in a row. Asian buyers 
are balking at the cost, even as power-generation alternatives such asliquefied 
natural gas 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-13/a-7-billion-gas-bill-sees-developing-asian-nations-sour-on-lng?cmpid=BBBXT093026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9517>
 have also gotten more expensive due to the US-Iran war.

Coal has been buoyed by strict mine safety checks in China following a fatal 
accident andcuts to production quotas 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-04/indonesia-s-largest-coal-miners-spared-from-major-output-cuts?cmpid=BBBXT093026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9517>
 in Indonesia, the world’s biggest exporter. Astrong El Niño 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/will-a-super-el-nino-impact-the-weather-and-food-production-in-2026?cmpid=BBBXT093026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9517>
, which has reduced hydropower output and dried up rivers — making barging more 
difficult — has further boosted the fuel.

The result is a market impasse, with coal and competing commodities all too 
high for many buyers.
A barge transports coal on the Barito River in South Kalimantan, Indonesia.
Photographer: Dimas Ardian/Bloomberg
Typically, a surge in the cost of LNG 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/qatar-extends-lng-force-majeure-as-hormuz-disruptions-drag-on?cmpid=BBBXT093026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9517>
 — a key power-plant feedstock for many Asian countries — would stoke demand 
for Indonesian coal, as seen following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

That hasn’t happened, with supply from the Southeast Asian nation down 
dramatically after the government purposefully throttled production this year 
in a bid to boost prices.

Jakarta is now working to increase output 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/world-s-top-coal-exporter-strains-to-lift-output-as-prices-soar?cmpid=BBBXT093026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9517>
 with midyear revisions to mining quotas, though new tonnage may take a while 
to reach the market. Many producers had to stand down contractors after 
exhausting their allocation, creating a more protracted restart process.

There’s potential for the deadlock to be broken in the coming months. Winter 
is approaching in China — the biggest importer of Indonesian coal — and that 
should result in a seasonal jump in demand. India is alsoburning rapidly 
through stockpiles 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/india-burns-through-coal-stockpiles-in-rush-to-ease-power-gap?cmpid=BBBXT093026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9517>
 after drought eroded hydropower.

But a strong return by buyers to a seaborne coal market with very little slack 
could send prices even higher. And that would mean higher power costs for Asian 
countries whose electricity supply is underpinned by the fuel.

—Eddie Spence, Bloomberg News
