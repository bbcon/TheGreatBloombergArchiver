While some of the key talks happened at a resort nestled in the green hills of

claim to a bigger role on the global stage, one they hope will grant them even 
more say in how the world is run.

Since it has been such a bewilderingly busy week, here are three key takeaways 
to help make sense of what happened.

The Yalta-esque trilateral meeting yielded little in the way of concrete 
outcomes but the G2 is on firmer ground.

When China and the US started making plans for their leaders to meet four

during bouts of friction. This APEC couldn’t have been better timed for that.

But, the two sides managed to get through it fine, partly because tech doesn’t

Of course, China may still retaliate for those latest curbs. Some have 
wondered if it may again use its advantage in rare earths to get its way, 
possibly as soon as this month. Before APEC, US officials were already 
complaining about diminished flows of the materials key to manufacturers.

Yet for now, the China-US trade truce remains intact, paving the way for the 
two sides to extend it when Xi and Trump meet, as expected, at a Group of 20 
summit in Miami in mid-December.

Counting the Costs

And hello, this is Allen Wan in Shenzhen, where the gathering of global 
leaders actually had competition for attention. As much as people were chatting 
about APEC, they were also discussing the US moving tocurb China’s remote access
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/us-moves-to-limit-remote-access-to-advanced-ai-chips?cmpid=BBD112026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11503>
 to Nvidia’s AI semiconductors.

The ramifications of the move could be huge, ranging from impairing China’s AI 
competitiveness to casting doubt on the sustainability ofthe data center 
buildout in Southeast Asia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/asia-s-data-center-hotbed-hit-by-us-chip-curbs-as-trump-visits?cmpid=BBD112026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11503>
.

The ultimate target for Washington may have been Beijing, but there will be 
more casualties than that, including dealmakers, tech companies and countries 
that have benefited from helping China compete with the US.

Washington’s move came after Jensen Huang’s admission of an affair 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-17/nvidia-delivers-solid-outlook-in-first-post-huang-earnings?cmpid=BBD112026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11503>
 with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed whom the US 
suspects of being a Chinese spy. (Beijing has denied the allegation, and Jensen 
Huang and Alice Huang are not related.)

Just after his ouster, the US Commerce Department — which had come under 
criticism for allowing Nvidia to ship chips to Megaspeed — published interim 
rules that would require data center operators in Singapore, Malaysia, 
Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil to seek a US government license to buy advanced 
AI chips from Nvidia and other American manufacturers.
Jensen Huang, the former CEO of Nvidia, in Berlin last month.Photographer: 
Alex Kraus/Bloomberg
Southeast Asia attracted hundreds of billions of dollars 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-17/clampdown-on-china-chip-loophole-puts-billions-in-loans-at-risk?cmpid=BBD112026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11503>
 in data center investment and generated substantial chip-related revenues from 
supplying Chinese firms like Alibaba and ByteDance, whose access to the most 
advanced chips Washington wants to block.

Before the latest regulation, nearly a third of China’s advanced compute had 
been forecast to come from renting overseas data centers by the end of 2027, 
while another 7% would come from chip smuggling, according to estimates by the 
Institute for Progress, a Washington-based think tank.

So the new US rule presents a major challenge for both Chinese cloud providers 
and AI model developers such as MiniMax and Z.AI, whose shares slumped this 
week.

They allegedly rely on Nvidia processors to train cutting-edge models while 
domestic chipmakers like Huawei and SMIC play catch-up. The proposed rules 
could raise the cost of accessing advanced compute.

Alibaba’s Hong Kong-listed shares have also tumbled, cutting the company’s 
market value 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-19/us-approved-ai-chip-sales-to-china-s-tencent-alibaba-data-show?cmpid=BBD112026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11503>
 to about $211 billion, less than half its level just over a year ago when 
investors were betting on it to lead China’s AI industry. Part of Alibaba’s 
ambition to build 20 gigawatts of data center capacity before 2032 depends on 
expansion across regions like Southeast Asia, though this week executives said 
that target may have to be lowered.

ByteDance, the tech giant behind TikTok and Douyin, also stands to lose from 
the new regulations 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-19/nscale-s-bytedance-deal-faces-scrutiny-under-us-chip-curbs?cmpid=BBD112026_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261120&utm_content=11503>
, along with the dealmakers who have facilitated its massive expansion into AI. 
Bankers have reportedly frozen work on a $480 million loan that would have 
funded advanced Nvidia chips for use by ByteDance.

Data center companies based in Asia-Pacific have raised about $49 billion in 
loans since 2023, according to figures compiled by Bloomberg. Across the 
region, lenders and private credit funds are scrambling to work out what the 
latest US directive would mean for billions in AI debt in the pipeline and 
deals that have already been clinched.
