COP’s grim climate finance outlook

By John Ainger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD102926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10347>

The European Union’s climate chief, Wopke Hoekstra, said talks on new climate 
finance commitments at this year’s United Nations COP31 summit are “dead in the 
water” after two years of stalled progress on cutting global emissions.

The 27-member bloc is already the biggest provider of climate finance 
globally, increasing its spending by €5 billion to €33 billion in 2025, 
according to figures from the European Commission. But EU officials 
increasingly feel that other countries are failing to match that financial 
support with more ambitious emissions-cutting contributions.
EU Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra.Photographer: Wiktor Dabkowski/Bloomberg
“Let’s not fall into the trap to have endless new rounds on financing,” 
Hoekstra said in an interview. “The problem needs something much more 
fundamental.”

His comments allude to a key area of tension as almost 200 nations assemble in 
Antalya, Turkey, on Nov. 9 for two-week discussions on how to tackle climate 
change. The summits frequently expose divisions between developed countries 
pushing for faster emissions cuts and developing nations seeking financial 
support to transition to cleaner economies and protect themselves against 
increasingly extreme weather.

The world is now all but certain to fly past the Paris Agreement’s landmark 
goal of limiting global warming below 1.5C above pre-industrial levels, 
according to the United Nations Environment Programme. To achieve even the 
“lowest possible” overshoot scenario, countries would have to slash emissions 
by 18% this decade. Even if all national climate plans are implemented, they’d 
only fall by 6% by 2035, according to a report this week.

Read More: EU to Push Global Emission-Cutting Ambitions at COP31 Summit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/eu-to-push-cop31-deal-to-boost-global-emission-cutting-ambitions?cmpid=BBD102926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10347>

Turkey, the host of COP31, has said a key goal of this year’s summit is to 
secure a global commitment to increase electricity’s share of final energy 
consumption to 35% by 2035. Discussions will also take place on extending a 
so-called mitigation work program to discuss how best to cut emissions. Brazil, 
last year’s host, is expected to present roadmaps for transitioning away from 
fossil fuels and curbing deforestation.

Two years ago, at COP29, developed countries agreed to boost public climate 
finance to $300 billion by 2035. Last year, countries struck a deal to triple 
adaptation finance by that date. With the US under President Donald Trump no 
longer participating in the COP process, much of that burden is set to fall on 
European nations.

“Any conversation on new fresh money is dead in the water,” Hoekstra said. “At 
least to the extent that we are the ones who should be providing it.”

That’s a thinly-veiled reference to the likes of China, Saudi Arabia and other 
Gulf states, which argue that responsibility for providing climate finance 
should rest with countries that industrialized first.

Hong Kong’s green rent premium

By Olivia Rudgard 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWBnLoYN_tg/olivia-rudgard?cmpid=BBD102926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10347>

Hong Kong landlords investing in green office refurbishments stand to pocket 
significantly bigger rent premiums than their counterparts in other major 
cities, according to a fresh analysis by property brokerSavills 
<bbg://securities/SVS%20LN%20Equity>.

Hong Kong offers average rent premiums of 23% for green upgrades, Savills 
calculations shared with Bloomberg show. The city is followed by Amsterdam and 
London, where rent premiums average 18% and 11%, respectively. In London, 
landlords investing in green improvements are also able to secure longer-term 
contracts with tenants, the analysis found.
Offices buildings in the Central district of Hong Kong.Photographer: Paul 
Yeung/Bloomberg
Investments in basic energy-efficiency measures such as better insulation have 
taken on greater importance against a backdrop of recurring energy crises and 
volatile oil and gas prices. At the same time, there’s growing evidence that 
tenants are willing to pay more to live in buildings that use environmentally 
friendly materials, the Savills analysis shows.

For investors, greener buildings can be cheaper to finance and are seen as a 
more reliable path to preserving value, said Georgios Askounis, a director at 
Savills Earth. For tenants, such buildings are often associated with greater 
comfort and prestige, he said.

Tenants “appreciate the positive impact of being in a sustainable building, a 
building that’s healthier, a building that has better quality, lower energy 
demand, that is less likely to overheat, possibly better access to amenities,” 
said Askounis.
