A Warsh Put?

Two months after he became Federal Reserve chair, Alan Greenspan was 
confronted with a stock-market plunge that drew parallels to the Great Crash of 
1929. He reacted by pumping liquidity into the banking system and lowering 
interest rates. When markets slumped in 1998 following a hedge fund collapse, 
he again oversaw an easing in policy.

Those responses generated the idea of a “Greenspan put 
<https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/econ_focus/2023/q1_federal_reserve>
,” borrowing the language of a contract that allows an investor to sell stock 
at a price that gives them some downside protection. Fast forward to today, and 
the latest plunge in equities gives rise to the question of whether Fed 
Chairman Kevin Warsh will similarly respond — this time by forgoing a December 
rate hike.

Perhaps predictably, given the Trump administration’s demonstrated record of 
advocating for lower rates, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent on Sunday 
attributed last week’s near-9% slide in the S&P 500 in part to “some very 
hawkish remarks from the Federal Reserve.”

“Many people believe that they may be making a policy mistake 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/bessent-warns-fed-risks-overtightening-as-us-markets-swoon?cmpid=BBD112326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11624>
 if they over-tighten — we’ve seen a substantial tightening in financial 
conditions over the past few months,” Bessent said on CBS’sFace The Nation.

Others see the risk of a policy mistake in the other direction if Warsh and 
his colleagues fail to follow through on a rate increase that investors had 
been fully pricing in until Friday’soutsize slump 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-20/us-stock-futures-dip-as-anthropic-premiere-disappoints-investors?cmpid=BBD112326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11624>
 on Wall Street.

“If the Fed backs off, it risks stoking the inflation issue,” Neil Dutta, head 
of economics at Renaissance Macro Research, wrote in a note Friday.
Alan GreenspanPhotographer: Chris Kleponis/Bloomberg News
The Warsh Fed has, though, indicated that it’s attentive to financial 
conditions, and how they may affect the AI investment boom, as this newsletter
highlighted 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-10-29/warsh-puts-a-spotlight-on-ai-financing-s-vulnerability-to-fed-policy?cmpid=BBD112326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11624>
 following the October policy meeting. Minutes of that gathering, released last 
week, showed that “a majority of participants noted a tightening in financial 
conditions since the September meeting.”

“Many pointed to lower AI-related equity valuations amid apparent 
reassessments of prospective returns and financing risks and judged that 
greater market discipline over highly leveraged projects could temper 
investment demand,” according to theminutes 
<https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcminutes20261028.htm>.

It’s not impossible to envision a scenario where Warsh and his colleagues fear 
adding to a tightening in financial conditions that then impedes the AI 
buildout that they think will sow the seeds for faster productivity growth. 
Dutta argues that would be the wrong move.

“The economy moves more slowly than markets,” he wrote. “Structures investment 
tends to be very slow moving and the AI build out has plenty of momentum.”

Goldman Sachs economists don’t see the Fed shifting its course for now, given 
high inflation and solid growth. But further tightening in financial conditions 
— “especially if it is led by a deterioration in risk sentiment or is connected 
to concerns about a pullback in AI spending — could displace rate hikes next 
year,” Goldman economist David Mericle wrote in a note Sunday.

Stocks could also come back before the Dec. 9 Fed decision. If not, investors 
will find out then if there’s a “Warsh put.”

The Week Ahead

Anxiety about inflation has yet to spoil the spending appetite of US consumers 
in the period leading up to the holiday-shopping season.

Economists estimate inflation-adjusted personal spending rose by 0.1% in 
October from a month earlier, based on the Bloomberg survey’s median estimate 
ahead of government figures due Wednesday. The modest increase builds on two 
robust quarters of consumer spending growth.

The report, the highlight of a Thanksgiving holiday-shortened US trading week, 
is also expected to show an acceleration in inflation. The personal consumption 
expenditures price index — the Fed’s preferred inflation metric — is projected 
to rise 3.8% from October of last year.

Elsewhere, a likely rate increase in South Korea and inflation in France and 
Spain will be among the highlights.

Need-to-Know Research

This newsletter began highlighting the cost each year of Thanksgiving dinner 
<http://fb.org/intel/markets/thanksgiving-dinner-cost-ticks-upward-in-2026> back
in 2022 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2022-11-21/what-s-happening-in-the-world-economy-the-us-holiday-season-is-getting-costly?cmpid=BBD112326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11624>
, when the American Farm Bureau Federation’s annual survey showed a surge in 
prices that reflected the broader cost-of-living crisis at the time. This year 
marks the first increase since then — again a reflection of the broader 
affordability concerns hitting US households.

The average cost of the all-American traditional holiday feast is up thanks to 
key ingredients including turkey, milk, rolls and cranberries are all getting 
pricier. Overall, the cost of a dinner for 10 people is set to rise 6% from a 
year ago to $5.86 per person. A 16-pound (7.3 kg) turkey, the centerpiece, will 
be 10% more expensive than in 2025.
Source: American Farm Bureau Federation
Spikes in prices throughout the supply chain, including fuel for processing 
and transportation, have made their way to consumers, the federation said. And 
farmers have been facing record diesel costs, elevated fertilizer prices, 
rising labor expenses and electricity bills. There’s good news on the dessert 
menu, however: frozen pie crusts and heavy whipping cream are down from last 
year.
