Food inflation

By Mark Niquette 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQVgYOcI2go/mark-niquette?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>
 andLauren Rosenthal 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXCV-bQx5Dk/lauren-rosenthal?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>

Americans are finally getting some relief at the gas pump, but a surge in food 
prices is only beginning — just in time for holiday meals.

That’s in part because there’s a lag for higher energy costs, which pushed up 
the price of diesel for farmers, fuel for transportation and plastic for 
packaging, to bleed into supermarket aisles. After ajump 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/us-core-cpi-rises-more-than-forecast-0-3-supporting-rate-hike?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>
 in October, economists say food inflation will get worse.

Fresh products typically get hit first. Among items that had the largest 
annual price increases in November were skirt steak at 33.3%, red potatoes at 
29.9% and Roma tomatoes at 27.5%, according to theBloomberg Price Index 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-inflation-price-index/?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>
.

“We’re just starting to get a taste of it,” said Joe Brusuelas, chief 
economist at RSM US. “Americans should prepare for rising food prices into the 
holiday season with the probability that those food prices will accelerate into 
the first half of 2027.”

Other factors are set to exacerbate food inflation over the next months, 
including fertilizer costs, tariffs and bad weather. A growing “super” El Niño 
has been reshaping rain patterns and threatening key products the US mostly 
imports — includingcocoa 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/el-nino-ravages-ecuador-cocoa-crop-threatening-global-supply?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>
, coffee andfragrant rice <https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/crops/rice/trade> 
such as Thai jasmine.



Grocery prices last month saw the biggest monthly gain since April in US 
government data. Americans shopping for Thanksgiving dinner will likely see big 
increases in some staples: sweet potatoes and sweet corn, for example, are up 
by more than 9% from a year ago in the Bloomberg Price Index, which uses 
alternative data to track prices in real time.

A survey from the American Farm Bureau Federation found 
<https://www.fb.org/intel/markets/thanksgiving-dinner-cost-ticks-upward-in-2026>
 that the price of a traditional Thanksgiving meal rose this year for the first 
time since 2022. The cost of a 10-person dinner increased by 6% from 2025 to 
$58.63, on average.

Laura Rader, a stay-at-home mother from Columbus, Ohio, says it’s harder to 
make ends meet with the higher food prices that she estimates are costing her 
an extra $200 a month for her family of four compared with three years ago. But 
she’s not letting that affect her traditional holiday meals.

“I want to keep Christmas and Thanksgiving the same with good quality stuff 
and just like things that we’ve always enjoyed,” said Rader, 34. “So it’s 
probably just going to have to be something we budget for and try to make up 
the difference somewhere else.”

El Niño anxiety

By Coco Liu 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AU-LNn94_HY/coco-liu?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>
,Monique Mulima 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWqywVvBhlE/monique-mulima?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>
, andLauren Rosenthal 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXCV-bQx5Dk/lauren-rosenthal?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>

As this year’s powerful El Niño approaches its peak, companies are sounding 
increasingly anxious about the business toll of the extreme weather it brings, 
from higher lending risks to weaker demand for their products.

Executives mentioned the weather phenomenon more than 600 times in 
third-quarter earnings calls in November, according to data from publicly 
traded companies around the world compiled by Bloomberg. That’s more than in 
any single month over the past decade, even though there have been three other 
El Niño cycles during that period, including a particularly strong one that 
wound down in 2016.

“This is not just any El Niño,” said Mekala Krishnan, who advises business 
leaders on climate risks at consulting firm McKinsey. “This is the super El 
Niño.”

The current El Niño phase is on track to eclipse its modern predecessors as it 
continues to fuelfires 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-12/el-nino-climate-change-supercharged-indonesia-fires-study-says?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>
,floods 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/india-s-richest-state-rations-water-after-worst-monsoon-in-years?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>
 anddroughts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-10-05/el-nino-hits-asia-with-wildfires-drought-as-global-weather-risks-build?cmpid=BBD112426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11794>
 across multiple continents. It’s expected to fade beginning in the Northern 
Hemisphere’s spring, but its weather effects — and impact on businesses — will 
likely ripple out for months.

Some companies are already feeling those effects. German chemical giant Bayer 
AG said in November that sales of its core crop protection business, which 
includes pesticides and herbicides, declined this year in part because of a 
strong El Niño.
