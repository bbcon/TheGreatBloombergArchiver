US prices aren’t disinflating obediently as they were supposed to do. Digging 
deeper into the numbers makes clear thatinflation is growing ever stickier 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/us-consumer-prices-climb-by-most-since-april-on-fuel-costs?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
, for reasons that go well beyond the current oil price shock. But after a 
fascinating download of data, the way markets responded may well have been more 
important.

This is our now standard breakdown of the Consumer Price Index into its four 
broad components, with energy emphasized in black. Services continue to make up 
the bulk of the problem, even as fuel prices certainly don’t help:

Strip out energy and leave everything else, and you get the following measure. 
It’s been below the Federal Reserve’s upper bound of 3% for more than two 
years, but never reached the 2% target. It just ticked up:

This is no disaster or repeat of the 1970s 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2021-12-26/inflation-in-the-1970s-traumatized-a-generation-here-s-why?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
, but it’s not good enough for a central bank under new leadership that says 
it’s committed to the 2% target.

That picture is confirmed by various, more sophisticated statistical measures 
of underlying inflation. The Cleveland Fed’s trimmed mean (stripping out the 
greatest outliers in either direction) is stuck above 2% and is moving in the 
wrong direction:

Oil has no direct impact on this measure. The same is true of the Atlanta 
Fed’s sticky-price CPI of prices that take a while to rise and are difficult to 
cut. It has also just notched higher and remains a ways from the 2% target:

Meanwhile, the measure that former Fed Chair Jerome Powell called “supercore” 
— services inflation stripping out shelter, whose measure is contentious — has 
risen back above 3%:

There’s nothing catastrophic about this, but every indication that inflation 
is become more embedded makes it far harder to cut rates as President Donald 
Trump wants 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-10-14/2026-midterms-could-be-worse-for-trump-and-republicans-than-2018?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
. That problem is compounded by wage growth, or the lack of it. Pay for the 
lowest-paid 25% of the population suffered a slower increase last month, and is 
now growing more slowly than headline inflation. That makes the issue ever more 
salient politically:

So what does that mean for the Fed? That’s where this gets very interesting.

Market Discipline

Initially, the bond market reacted just as might be expected. The 10-year 
Treasury yield jolted up to 5.32%, back to its recent highs. The question was 
whether the appetite existed to take the yield even higher — or if such high 
yields would attract buyers. It proved to be the latter:

The 10-year yield 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/us-treasuries-slump-as-hot-inflation-lifts-fed-rate-hike-bets?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
 slid all the way back to 5.18%, on no new news of any significance. US stocks 
had their worst day since July, with the Nasdaq 100 shedding 2.02% — a trend 
that continued with a brutal start in Asia, particularly for chipmakers.

As for expectations of the Fed, the initial response to CPI was, logically, 
that a hike at the Federal Open Market Committee’s meeting in two weeks was 
back on the table. Fed funds futures briefly moved to price in a 50/50 shot of 
a hike, but that subsided to end the day barely changed. This contract’s moves 
since ChairmanKevin Warsh 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/fed-s-warsh-to-get-final-word-in-bangkok-as-inflation-roars-back?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
 surprised everyone with his hawkishness at Jackson Hole have been something to 
behold:

The afternoon brought publication of the Fed’s Beige Book, a collection of 
anecdotal evidence from its regional branches. It made clear that the Fed 
contacts were complaining a lot about rising input prices and are keen for 
higher rates to deal with them. Oxford Economics’ regular index gauging concern 
about inflation from the numbers of references in the Beige Book suggests the 
Fed will feel pressure to hike.

These data make it more or less impossible to cut but aren’t bad enough to 
force an imminent tightening. Besides, the Fed’s decision depends on more than 
inflation.

The arrow of causation may in fact run from Treasury yields to the Fed, and 
not the reverse. The pressure to raise short-term rates comes from the risk 
that the bond market loses confidence in it and long-term borrowing costs run 
amok. Wednesday provided a great test of this thesis — and showed that for now, 
there are plenty of buyers for a rock-solid investment that promises a yield of 
5.2% for 10 years (particularly when stocks are selling off). That reduces the 
urgency to hike fed funds.

Jean Ergas of Tigress Financial Partners suggested that many players were 
choosing to “average in” to the bond market, rather than make a big bet on 
timing, now that higher yields give them a margin of safety. He added:
There’s a distinction between fed funds and Treasuries. The 10-year yield has 
an immediate pass-through to the economy. It’s instant. You can use fed funds 
to regulate the cost and availability of bank credit, but with a year’s lag.
The bond market, even more than politicians or the consumers who drive the 
CPI, appears now to be in the driving seat. As long as yields don’t rise 
further, the pressure for instant rate hikes might abate. Meanwhile, the impact 
of higher borrowing costs onstocks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-14/the-close-10-14-2026-video?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
 (outside the select group directly boosted by artificial intelligence) will 
encourage the Fed to stay its hand.

The news on inflation was, unambiguously, not good. Thanks to the market’s 
reaction, a rate hike this month is still on balance unlikely.

The Sting in the Tail

Dig through the CPI components and something always bites you. This month, it 
was tuition fees (a particular concern for Points of Return). After years of 
hiking fees far in excess of inflation and pushing college further out of reach 
for the middle class, the pandemic forced colleges to change. Tuition has risen 
less than inflation ever since — until this month:

It’s a good bet that colleges felt they could no longer sit on accumulating 
costs without passing them on. It adds to affordability travails for the middle 
class.

The Pain That Keeps on Giving

Days after the Golden Week holiday 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/china-holiday-spending-slips-as-weeklong-break-can-t-lift-gloom?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
, Chinese consumer confidence disappointed and export data surged. This 
divergence is familiar, but grows ever harder to ignore. Exports soared 36% 
last month from a year earlier, on track to top last year’s record $1.2 
trillion trade surplus:

As Points of Return has noted 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TLSTYIKIJH9M?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
, exports’ continued dominance of China’s growth keeps alive the hopes for a 
broad-based consumer stimulus ignited by the2024 whatever-it-takes pivot 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-03-27/roach-casts-doubt-on-china-consumer-pivot-as-more-slogans?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
. Bloomberg analysis of official data suggests that consumers scaled back on 
spending over the holiday compared to two years ago. Weak consumer confidence 
has coincided with a sharp equity pullback, with the Shanghai-Shenzhen CSI 300 
Index down nearly 20% since June:

Even technology and industrial companies, drivers of China’s exports, have 
disappointed. Strong exports haven’t translated into stronger fortunes for the 
private sector (which might give ballast towestern claims that China is dumping 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-30/china-export-shock-why-it-s-hard-for-economic-rivals-to-fight-back?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
 goods at uneconomically cheap prices):

Possible restrictions by the European Union 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TLSTYIKIJH9M?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
 pose another problem. Beijing’s goods trade surplus with the bloc grew to 
$34.8 billion in September, up more than 20% since September 2025. Increasingly 
loud voices areurging Brussels to clamp down on “unfair” trade 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/china-and-eu-risk-new-trade-clash-over-what-comes-after-car-deal?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
 practices:

No restrictions are imminent beyond the recently concluded talks on hybrid and 
electric vehicles. But pushback fromGermany and France 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/germany-to-push-eu-to-impose-caps-on-additional-chinese-imports?cmpid=BBD101526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10124>
 shows that China must take these concerns seriously.

Beijing has experience from the World Trade Organization in navigating such 
threats. China analystMitch Presnick explains 
<https://www.linkedin.com/in/mitchpresnick/> the reluctance to shore up 
domestic demand:
I don’t think consumption is the main story. It would be consumption third, 
growth second, and industrial dominance first. They’re trying to set up for the 
21st century and what that looks like to them: a hub-and-spoke model where they 
provide the R&D and technology, capital, know-how, and automation and robotics 
expertise.
Lower borrowing costs also work in Chinese companies’ favor, reversing a 
decades-long pattern in which their bond yields exceeded Treasuries. Absent a 
major shock, that edge should prove difficult to dislodge:

The financing advantage is a self-reinforcing cycle. Lower yields support 
domestic activity, and put downward pressure on the yuan, making exports 
cheaper in dollar terms.

Still, growing pushback and anti-China sentiment 
<https://www.nytimes.com/2024/05/10/opinion/anti-china-sentiment.html> show 
this model’s flaws. AsAndy Rothman of Sinology LLC 
<https://www.linkedin.com/in/andy-rothman-34982137/> points out, there’s a 
sense in Beijing that China can’t rely on exports long term, and a desire to 
return to the pre-2020 economic model when consumption accounted for almost 
two-thirds of growth.

However, China shows little urgency to shift, Rothman suggests, because 
Brussels and Washington have little leverage. In the first three quarters of 
2026, the G7’s share of China’s exports shrunk to 23%, down from 48% in 2000 
before joining the WTO. Southeast Asia is now Beijing’s largest market. Rothman 
says this strengthens China’s hand:
Consumption will return as the main engine of growth, but it will take time. 
The government needs to restore confidence among the nation’s entrepreneurs and 
households... When confidence returns, Chinese consumers, small businesses, and 
investors will be able to draw down household savings, which have doubled since 
2020.
That explains the reluctance to unleash a large fiscal stimulus; policymakers 
probably agree that it isn’t necessary. What China needs instead, Rothman says, 
is a consumer boost to get the domestic economy moving. And that’s still not on 
the horizon.

—Richard Abbey
