Chronicle of a Hike Foretold

US inflation has blindsided us many times since the pandemic. July 2026 was 
not one of them. Very unusually, both headline and core inflation (excluding 
food and energy) came inexactly as expected <https://bloom.bg/4qaRGOx> by a 
consensus of economists. Falls in services and energy inflation helped bring 
the headline down, but it remains above 3%. Here is our regular breakdown into 
the four main inflation components, which you can reproduce with ECAN <GO> on 
the terminal:

The alternative measures of core or underlying inflation that we regularly 
monitor all declined. That applies to the trimmed mean (excluding outlying 
components in either direction), the median, an index of products and services 
whose prices are “sticky” or difficult to reduce, and the Federal Reserve’s 
favored “supercore” (services excluding shelter). But while the decline is good 
news, theuncomfortable bad news <https://bloom.bg/4g7Is0R> remains that all 
remain above 2.5%, as they have been ever since the pandemic.

This is a marked change from the decade before Covid-19. If the Fed’s new 
chairmanKevin Warsh <https://bloom.bg/4qbd1ra> is serious about treating 2% as 
a target, it’s difficult to see that the central bank can go much longer 
without taking some concrete measure to get there — which would mean hiking 
rates.

Not only was this a predictable report, then, but it also required a classic 
economist’s “on the one hand and on the other” response. Here’s Josh Hirt of 
Vanguard:

This CPI report is reassuring because it confirms that some of the inflation 
pressures we saw earlier this year have begun to ease. At the same time, we’re 
not yet at a point where inflation is clearly returning to the Fed’s target.

The lack of surprises confirmed the prior belief that there was no 
justification whatever for rate cuts. Carl Weinberg of High Frequency Economics 
put this clearly:

So… great… headline figures matched expectations. Disinflation is not 
particularly apparent in core CPI since the start of the war with Iran. It was 
all but invisible before the war. There is no case in this report for FOMC 
policymakers to cut interest rates. 

Combine this with labor market reports suggesting the US is operating at full 
employment <https://bloom.bg/4g7SpLI>, and the only path for rates is upward. 
That’s still the market’s base case, but there’s increasing certainty that 
there will be only one more hike this year:

This is probably about right. Warsh cannot delay action much longer, but these 
numbers are sufficiently benign that he can get away without a hike at the next 
meeting.

Meanwhile, one detail from the report should demonstrate how much the 
revolutionary goings-on in technology have complicated the job of predicting 
price levels. For a generation, the prices of both software and devices have 
followed a steady downward path. But software has suddenly stopped getting 
cheaper.

AI might well be disinflationary in the long run. But in the short run?

Up to a Point <https://bloom.bg/4g7EAgl>, Lord Copper

Copper has been on a remarkable six-week rally that has brought it to an 
all-time high for the fourth time this year. Its course has still been largely 
horizontal since May, but strong fundamentals suggest it should only be a 
matter of time before it jets off again. That in part is because the drivers of 
the copper price are changing:

For decades, a robust Chinese economy has meant strong demand from copper’s 
largest consumer, and translated into strong prices. Beijing’s economic woes 
have weakened that correlation, and the metal is fueling its latest rally 
elsewhere.

China hasn’t lost its influence over prices, of course. But there and beyond, 
the ongoing massive AI buildout is a huge factor in the demand shift. Copper is 
required in power distribution, cooling systems, server interconnects, and 
building wiring. All told, the metal is said to account for approximately 6% of 
total data-center capital expenditure. S&P Global Research forecasts copper 
demand just for data centers will increase from 1.1 million metric tons last 
year to 2.5 million by 2040:

But AI-related demand is dwarfed by the huge energy transition — encompassing  
electric vehicles <https://bloom.bg/4i6JxIU>, renewables, and grid 
infrastructure. In general, EVs require nearly three times as much copper as 
conventional cars, and their rapidly growing sales add to demand. Global EV 
sales last year were 25% higher than total new-car sales in the US, the world's 
second-largest new-car market.

Altogether, global copper demand is expected to shoot up by about 50%, rising 
to 42 million metric tons by 2040. Meanwhile,supply is expected to fall short 
<https://bloom.bg/3RNExOZ> by as much as 10 million. Freedom Broker argues that 
this will be driven by declining ore grades, limited large-scale discoveries — 
even as the global copper exploration budget has increased in recent years — 
and extended project development timelines averaging 17 years from discovery to 
production:

The first chapter of any economics textbook establishes that the price will go 
up a lot if these predictions for supply and demand come true. Beyond the 
long-term structural deficit, Washington’s tariffs on certain copper products 
exacerbate rising prices. The US now imposes a 50% duty on semi-finished and 
derivative copper products while, for now, exempting refined copper cathodes. 
That could change following a Commerce Department review, which could pave the 
way for tariffs of 15% in 2027 and 30% in 2028. Societe Generale’s Michael 
Haigh notes the market has begun toprice this risk <https://bloom.bg/4hwKkmi>:

US copper inflows hit a 12-year high in mid-2026 as traders rushed to 
stockpile ahead of a possible tariff, and the policy direction looks firmly 
skewed toward more duties rather than fewer, with the Commerce Department 
simultaneously expanding Section 232 coverage to new derivative products and 
opening entirely new metal fronts.

This all adds to the upward pressure on overall inflation indexes. There 
should be beneficiaries, most obviously the major producers Chile and Peru, 
whose equity markets have historically tracked the red metal closely. This 
helps to explain the rally in their stock markets over the past six weeks. 
International investors tend to treat both nations as though they are very 
large copper mines:

Further rises could have a profound impact on both economies. Bank of 
America’s Ezequiel Aguirre forecasts copper above $16,000 a ton by the middle 
of next year — a further 10% increase from here. As about 30% of Peruvian 
annual export revenues come from copper, that should help bring real gross 
domestic product growth to 4.4%.

—Richard Abbey


Blue Wave

The dog days will soon be over <https://bloom.bg/4fVDsOg>, as US politics is 
about to provide a new wave of market turbulence. Midterms come in November, 
and history suggests that a wave for Democrats should be close to inevitable. 
Presidents in their second term always suffer major losses in Congress, and 
with both chambers finely balanced that should normally mean a Democratic clean 
sweep.

That would be true even without the unpopular war in Iran, and even if 
President Donald Trump didn’t have the lowest approval rating of his time in 
office. But the path to a blue wave isn’t smooth. The problem for the Democrats 
is the Senate, where they are defending the majority of the seats up for 
election this time (and were last on the ballot in 2020, a good year for 
Democrats when Joe Biden won the presidency). They need to flip four seats in 
the full chamber. That’s a heavy lift, made harder by the party’s own internal 
ructions.

On prediction markets, their chances were seen to dip severely when their 
original nominee in Maine, a blue state held by the Republican veteran Susan 
Collins, was forced to withdraw after sexual assault allegations. Insurgent 
campaigns by a range of avowed Democratic Socialists in other states, notably 
Michigan, did further damage to their prospects.

But in the last two weeks, the Democratic Socialists have sustained reverses. 
Combine that with thelatest model from the highly influential poll aggregator 
Nate Silver <https://bloom.bg/4hwWJql> that finds even Texas leaning toward the 
Democrats, and prediction markets are back to betting on a blue Senate:

The critical races are in far-flung corners of the country, in states that 
have little in common — from Alaska and Maine to Texas and Iowa — so the 
picture will stay confused and the odds are bound to shift frequently between 
now and November.

While a blue wave is no better than a 50-50 shot, a wave of stock market 
volatility seems much more likely. Vol tends to be seasonal, and generally 
increases from now until the election. This chart from Barclays Equities 
Tactical Strategies overlays the course of the VIX volatility so far this year 
with the average over the last 10 years. The pattern is obvious:

To some extent, the extraordinary AI boom has so far insulated against 
domestic and international political uncertainty under Trump 2.0, but Barclays’ 
Alexander Altmann cautions this might not last:

Bear in mind that AI has become a weaponized political topic now, it also may 
not be plain sailing for the tech bulls between now and the elections in early 
November.

The last 18 months have seen a US president exercise power in an 
unprecedentedly unencumbered way. On the most salient issues for markets, 
support from Congress has either been irrelevant or a given. There’s a risk 
that will soon change, and markets will start to try to price that risk.
