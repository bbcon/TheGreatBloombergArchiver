How to electrify cheaply

By Akshat Rathi 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUdOobfNzkQ/akshat-rathi?cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>

For much of the past decade, countries and companies tackling climate change 
have focused on setting goals to reach net zero. The remarks at this year’s 
Climate Week NYC indicate that the next 10 years of climate action (even if 
it’s not framed as such) will center around further electrification of the 
economy and lowering power prices.

Take the comments from Miatta Fahnbulleh, the UK’s energy secretary, who said 
atBloomberg Green’s summit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/uk-energy-secretary-says-cutting-power-bills-is-her-top-priority?cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>
 that the country’s 2030 target to run the grid almost entirely on clean 
sources isn’t about the climate, but affordability. “We’re really clear that 
it’s our exposure to global fossil fuel markets” that is raising energy costs, 
she said.


Miatta Fahnbulleh, UK energy secretary, during the Bloomberg Green event in 
New York.Photographer: Michael Nagle/Bloomberg
New York’s climate week often gives a sneak peek of conversations that happen 
later in the year at the United Nations climate summit. The Turkey-led COP31 
team primed some of those discussions by attempting to persuade all countries 
to sign up to the35-by-35 goal 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-22/turkey-s-environment-minister-lays-out-agenda-for-cop31-summit?cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>
 aimed at increasing thefinal share 
<https://unfccc.int/news/cop31-presidency-announces-new-targets-on-global-electrification-cutting-waste-resilient-cities>
 of energy coming from electricity to 35% by 2035, compared to about 23% today.

The International Energy Agency backed that ambition in its 198-page report 
<https://www.iea.org/reports/electrification> published this week, declaring 
that electricity is already the source of energy that powers nearly half the 
world’s economic output. Its sector-by-sector analysis concluded that raising 
final energy consumption from electricity to 33% by 2035 is cost-effective with 
today’s technology, energy prices and financing conditions.

Crucially, the IEA notes that electrification can be the key to energy 
security. It’s why, since the Iran War increased oil and gas prices, countries 
reliant on imported fossil fuels have beenrapidly deploying 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-energy-transition-iran-war/?srnd=undefined&sref=jjXJRDFv&cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>
 electricity generation and consumption technologies.

While electrification on its own isn’t sufficient to put the world on track to 
meet climate goals set under the Paris Agreement, the IEA says electrification 
will cut emissions simply because using electricity for transport, heating and 
cooling is a much more energy efficient process than burning fossil fuels for 
the same purposes.

None of this is inevitable or easy. The IEA notes that meeting the 2035 goal 
will mean serious global efforts: doubling annual solar additions to 1,200 GW, 
increasing annual wind deployment two-and-half times to 400 GW and growing 
total battery storage 10-fold to 2,900 GW. All this will have to be backed by 
investments in grids that need to increase 40%.
The sun rises over solar panels at Wuling Power Corp.’s Yuding photovoltaic 
power station in Zhongwei, Ningxia, China.Source: Bloomberg
Meeting those goals is not just about technology, warns Arthur Downing in his 
new book “Power and the People 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-24/how-to-build-an-electrostate?cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>
.” He analyzes the history of the UK’s grid and concludes that the reason the 
country has some of the highest power prices in the world is down to it 
breaking up the grid into parts and then pushing them into private ownership. 
The process began in the 1980s under Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher.

The result is that the UK’s per capita electricity demand has fallen rapidly. 
“There’s only five countries in the world, including Syria, Tajikistan, Puerto 
Rico and Tanzania that have had bigger reductions in [per-capita] electricity 
demand,” said Downing on this week’sZero podcast 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-24/how-to-build-an-electrostate?cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>
.

That’s a problem because it means the cost of the power system is spread 
across fewer units of electricity and thus raises power prices. It’s why, even 
as the UK gets more and more of its electricity from cleaner sources, power 
prices continue going up. Building renewables may be cheap, but integrating 
them in a grid that’s shrinking in consumption reverses those cost gains.

So how do you build a system that provides clean electricity cheaply? It will 
need a radical rethink from how the grid is owned, regulated and operated 
today, says Downing. The next decade will involve less of a focus on technology 
and more of a focus on changing the institutions that enable building the grid 
and electrifying the end use of energy in transport, industry and homes.

“Something has gone wrong in our society if we are willing to be super 
optimistic about the infinite flexibility and possibilities around technology, 
but we just don’t seem to think that we can change institutions and markets,” 
he said. “These are human made things.”

Power hungry

By Will Wade 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQKkcaEIb6M/will-wade?cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>
 andRebecca Torrence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVRjw85GvGc/rebecca-torrence?cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>

Investors have poured $4.6 billion into US nuclear startups so far this year, 
up from $3.8 billion raised in 2025, according to Crunchbase.

They’re betting that deep-pocketed tech firms desperate for power 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/data-centers-on-track-to-suck-up-a-fifth-of-us-power-use-by-2035?cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>
 will pay higher prices for carbon-free electricity that’s available around the 
clock. The key question is when those big checks will yield actual steel and 
concrete power plants.

Demand for electricity is climbing, especially from data centers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-ai-data-center-heat-pollution-cities/?cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>
. However, there are only two new commercial reactors under construction in the 
US and Canada and no other such projects have received approval from 
regulators. The contrast reflects the complex realities of the US nuclear 
industry: Rising investor interest may not result in more electricity in time 
to meet the first wave of demand.

“The reality is starting to sink in,” said Scott Levine, a senior analyst with 
Bloomberg Intelligence. “This is going to take longer than expected.”

Startups that have lined up venture financing still have to navigate a complex 
regulatory process, prove they can actually build new reactors that can 
generate electricity, and then commercialize those never-before-built 
technologies at scale.

According to Bloomberg Intelligence, widespread commercialization won’t arrive 
until about 2035. High up-front costs and a still-emerging supply chain are 
additionalhurdles 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-06-11/us-nuclear-power-s-technological-rebirth-will-take-some-time?cmpid=BBD092526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9327>
.
