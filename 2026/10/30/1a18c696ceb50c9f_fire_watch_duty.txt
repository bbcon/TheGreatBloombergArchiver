Evacuation mindset

By Todd Woody 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVl1IPrK6vs/todd-woody?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>

On a weekend getaway up the California coast this summer, temperatures neared 
100F (38C) as we reached our destination in the hills above the Mendocino wine 
country. Worrisome, but withwildfires ravaging Canada and Europe 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-europe-summer-wildfires/?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>
 that week, the Golden State, for once, had been spared major conflagrations.

Relaxing on the deck, we noticed a gray-tinged cloud floating above a forested 
ridge on an otherwise blue-sky afternoon. Reflexively, I checked Watch Duty, the
go-to disaster app 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2024-10-15/watch-duty-wildfire-tracker-is-the-hottest-app-of-the-year?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>
 of theAnthropocene 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2021-01-29/the-scientist-who-warned-of-ozone-hole-then-named-our-climate-age?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>
. It wasn’t a cloud. A fast-moving wildfire had broken out several miles away, 
by the vineyards we had just passed.

As authorities ordered evacuations and shut down the freeway, I started 
plotting escape routes and drove down the hill to charge the car, just in case. 
Bags were repacked, and after a fitful sleep punctuated by wildfire alerts, we 
awoke the next morning to the sound of sirens and helicopters churning overhead 
as another fire broke out in the tinder-dry hills directly west of us.

Being a Californian means living with a certain level of existential anxiety. 
After all, a cataclysmic earthquake could strike before you finish reading this 
sentence. But when the Big One will hit is unknowable; wildfires, though, are 
now year-round. Watch Duty’s distinctive and unceasing pinging reminds me I 
must always be ready to run.

This isn’t garden-variety climate anxiety but something new: Evacuation is 
becoming a mindset 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-07/wildfire-evacuation-grows-more-urgent-as-blazes-move-faster?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>
 as one climate shock after another rewires our brains to prepare for disaster 
rather than simply worry about it. Thanks to climate change, any of the small 
spot fires thatroutinely erupt within miles 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-12-12/why-making-your-california-home-wildfire-resistant-requires-herd-immunity?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>
 of my Bay Area home when temperatures soar couldexplode into an urban inferno 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/latest/los-angeles-wildfires?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>
 within minutes. So when Watch Duty calls in the middle of the night, I pick up.

Around the world, climate-driven catastrophes are no longer random, distant 
disasters happening to someone else; they’re everything everywhere all at once. 
The toxic smoke from remote wildfires; the waves generated by a Pacific storm 
taking out multimillion-dollar beach houses a thousand miles away; the drying 
up of the Colorado River and its reservoirs; theglacier in Nepal that collapses 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-04/nepal-floods-himalayan-glacier-collapse-shows-risk-from-climate-change?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>
 in an instant. But now threats feel more personal, knowing that when you leave 
your home it might not be there when you return.
The Oakland Hills engulfed in flames on Oct. 20, 1991.Photographer: Steve 
Ringman/San Francisco Chronicle/Getty Images
“We are now living in an era of cascading traumas,” says Elissa Epel 
<https://profiles.ucsf.edu/elissa.epel>, a professor of psychiatry at the 
University of California at San Francisco who helps college students cope with 
climate stress. “The new normal is to expect disaster. We don’t know when, but 
it could be at any time.” For Epel, her climate vigilance began one morning in 
September 2020, when she and the rest of the Bay Area woke toapocalyptic orange 
skies 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-09-10/under-orange-skies-the-bay-area-sees-the-future?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>
 from wildfires burning across Northern California. This always-on alertness 
feels different from the general eco-anxiety many experience when thinking 
about a climate-constrained future, or the trauma suffered by those who’ve 
survived a wildfire or hurricane.

Climate vigilance creates stress but can also have an upside, spurring people 
to make their homes more disaster-resilient and to develop and act on 
evacuation plans. That sense of agency and being ready for the worst can help 
keep watchfulness from spiraling into chronic stress, according to mental 
health experts.

As our Mendocino weekend ended, the fires continued to burn, and the freeway 
remained closed. Forced to find a route around the wildfire, we ended up 
driving down a less-traveled road that winds through a bucolic countryside of 
vineyards and orchards. Watch Duty, for a blissful hour, fell silent.

Envision eyes greener data centers

By Bloomberg News 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>

Chinese renewable energy firm Envision Group plans to finish building its 
Galaxy Campus data center in Inner Mongolia by the end of 2028, with capacity 
to eventually reach 3 gigawatts.

Ricky Zheng, general manager of the company’s AI Data Center business 
disclosed the timeline in an interview withBloomberg TV 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/live?cmpid=BBD103026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10763>
 on Friday. Envision sees “strong demand” in the Chinese market and has already 
lined up offtake agreements through 2029 or 2030, he said.
Envision’s Galaxy Campus data center in Inner Mongolia.Source: Envision Group
Envision, whose main business is making wind turbines and batteries, said in 
August that it had begun operating the data center at Ulanqab, a city about 350 
kilometers (217 miles) northwest of Beijing with abundant renewable energy 
resources. Until now, most such facilities have been built by specialized 
developers, or telecom and internet firms, but the artificial intelligence boom 
is luring other types of companies.

In the longer term, the company plans to build data centers outside China, 
aiming for an eventual global capacity of as much as 10 gigawatts, he said.

“We are looking for countries or cities that have renewable resources, so that 
we can build large-scale data centers,” Zheng said. Latin America, Southeast 
Asia, Europe and Africa all offer potential, he said.
