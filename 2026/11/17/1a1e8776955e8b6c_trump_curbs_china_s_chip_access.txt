Deep Dive: Can Farming Take the Heat?
A greenhouse at the research center in the Abu Dhabi desert.Photographer: 
Natalie Naccache for Bloomberg Businessweek
In the deserts of the United Arab Emirates, rain comes about once a year, and 
when it does, it floods. The sun is relentless, with temperatures regularly 
topping 50C. The groundwater is salty. The soil, if you can call it that, is 
sand. Shamal Mohammed is trying to grow cucumbers, tomatoes, melons and berries 
there.

 * He heads a three-year-old, $200 million farming-tech incubator for Silal, a 
food producer created by the Abu Dhabi government to boost local agriculture. 
With the world losing vast quantities of productive land to desertification, 
the UAE wants to nurture technology that could be central tofarming in a 
climate-changed future 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-17/the-uae-s-silal-tests-desert-tech-in-abu-dhabi?cmpid=111726_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11504>
. “If you can make it here, you can make it anywhere,” Mohammed says during a 
tour of the airy, glass-paneled facility.
 * Since its founding, the hub has welcomed 70 companies to test their tech 
and, if successful, fund their business. No idea is off the table: raising 
shrimp indoors; using nanotech material to block the sun’s scorching heat from 
greenhouses; deploying robots to determine whether fruit is ripe. So far, only 
three have turned their products into profitable businesses.
 * But global sales of desert technologies have been growing at 15% annually 
and will reach $4.2 billion this year, according to Stratistics Market Research 
Consulting. With its immense financial resources and parched climate, the UAE 
is well-positioned to help develop vast areas of previously nonarable land in 
Africa, the Middle East and, increasingly, other areas as the climate warms.
