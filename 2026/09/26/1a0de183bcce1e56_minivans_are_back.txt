Minivan renaissance

By Kyle Stock 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ARKz8x17rNs/kyle-stock?cmpid=BBD092626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9303>

Toyota Motor Corp. has five different SUVs that offer seating for seven, the 
cheapest of which goes about 24 miles on a gallon of gas.

However, the company’s minivan, dubbed Sienna, seats eight and travels 36 
miles to a gallon; it costs about $1,000 less too.

One might call the SUV price premium a coolness tax, but lots of drivers are 
finding minivans – a genus of vehicle that is greener, cheaper and more 
spacious than so-called sport-utility machines – plenty cool in their own right.

Indeed, the much-maligned minivan is having a renaissance, as prices for both 
gas and swanky SUVs hover near historic highs.
Photos and animation: Jenn Ackerman and Tim Gruber for Bloomberg Businessweek
US drivers bought 281,000 minivans this year through August, an 8% increase 
from the same period last year, even as total new vehicle sales declined, 
according to Edmunds.com, a data and car-shopping platform. And though the 
family trucksters represent just 3% of the US auto market, American drivers are 
buying as many minivans as large SUVs for the first time in almost a decade.

“The value proposition is unparalleled,” said Ivan Drury, director of insights 
at Edmunds. “There is no area in which the minivan underperforms an SUV unless 
you are going off-road. The minivan is going to come out ahead on space, 
mileage, price — you name it.”

Granted, there are plenty of hybrid SUVs on the market as well, but they tend 
to be less efficient, because they are engineered for off-road use with 
all-wheel drive and a less aerodynamic profile. A hybrid van is proving a 
no-brainer green option for both the EV-curious and EV-skeptical.

Van sales have really started zipping in recent months, since the war in Iran 
sent fuel prices surging. Four of the six vans on the US market are either 
hybrid or fully electric, offering sizable savings for people who do most of 
their driving around town, where braking for stoplights and school drop-offs 
can recharge the battery.

Toyota, meanwhile, has been so emboldened by its van momentum that it expanded 
production at the Indiana factory that makes the Sienna.

Web discount

By Leslie Kaufman 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUZ8izNmriE/leslie-kaufman?cmpid=BBD092626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9303>

As climate change increases the threat of destructive wildfires, traditional 
mortgage lenders are making it harder or more expensive to secure a home loan 
in fire-prone parts of California. Online lenders are behaving differently.

Fintech lenders — companies that originate mortgages primarily online —account 
for roughly 17% of the US mortgage market. The new research finds they were 
more likely than traditional lenders to approve mortgages in areas with high 
wildfire risk and charged relatively lower interest rates there.

The findings come from a study 
<https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0739456X261482067> published Friday 
in the Journal of Planning Education and Research.
Homes under construction and empty lots destroyed by the 2025 Eaton Fire in 
Altadena, California.Photographer: Josh Edelson/AFP/Getty Images
The authors, Tyler Haupert, an assistant professor of urban studies at New 
York University Shanghai, and Jesse Keenan, a professor of sustainable real 
estate and urban planning at Tulane University, examined more than 730,000 
California mortgage applications from 2018 through 2020. They sorted the 
neighborhoods where borrowers were seeking homes into wildfire-risk categories 
using the Federal Emergency Management Agency’s National Risk Index, which 
measures factors including historical wildfire exposure, the probability of 
fires and expected losses to buildings on a census-tract level.

The data predates the 2025 Los Angeles fires, and lender behavior may have 
changed since then.

Still, the contrast was pronounced. As FEMA’s measure of wildfire danger 
increased, traditional lenders became less likely to approve mortgages and 
charged more for those they did approve. In high-risk neighborhoods, their odds 
of approving a mortgage were about 15% lower than in neighborhoods with 
essentially no wildfire risk.

Fintech lenders, by contrast, showed little change in approval odds as 
wildfire risk increased.
