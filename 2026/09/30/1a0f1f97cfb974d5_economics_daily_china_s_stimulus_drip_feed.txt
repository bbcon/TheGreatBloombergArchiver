‘Targeted Cushion’

China has unveiled its latest “stimulus” measures.

In a bid to underpin the flailing property sector, the country will subsidize 
interest payments for homebuyers on some cheaper homes nationwide.

To keep money flowing to favored industries, there’s measures to encourage 
banks to lend to sectors including infrastructure and technology. Meantime, the 
central bank raised the quota for a relending program supporting technological 
innovation and equipment upgrades by 200 billion yuan ($30 billion).

The blended fiscal and monetary boost 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/china-s-mini-stimulus-seen-securing-gdp-target-not-much-more?cmpid=BBD093026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9504>
 — while better than nothing — is hardly a game changer for growth (which 
explains my quote marks around “stimulus”).

that swings the growth impetus to strategic drivers such as technology.

Need-to-Know Research

Further analysis is emerging that artificial intelligence isn’t yet leading to 
mass job destruction. (See the Research section of theAug. 13 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-13/uk-economy-shrugs-off-iran-war-shock-for-now?cmpid=BBD093026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9504>
 newsletter for a previous take.) A new National Bureau of Economic Research 
paper offers a look at the experience of the recent cohort of US college 
graduates.

June, July and August mark “the first summer in which AI use in the workplace 
was widespread enough to expect observable effects on employment,” economist 
Robert Fairlie and Jane Wu, a management-strategy professor, wrote in thepaper 
<http://www.nber.org/papers/w35796>. Because grads typically start in junior 
roles, performing “relatively standardized tasks,” that enhances the interest 
in looking at what’s happened to the class of ’26.
Source: Robert Fairlie, University of California, Los Angeles; Jane Wu, UCLA 
Anderson School of Management
The findings: both the official unemployment rate and an expanded measure the 
UCLA authors devised to capture grads not actively searching for a job showed 
rates “within the normal range of variation” for the past several years. “We 
also do not find evidence that unemployment among recent college graduates rose 
in the occupations that AI is most likely to affect,” they added.

Of course, “These findings do not rule out larger effects in the future,” the 
duo cautioned. “Additional years of data will be needed to hone in on whether 
effects emerge as workplace use of AI deepens.”
