Divided-Government Blues

It used to be that the bond market appreciated a divided distribution of power 
in Washington, with opposing parties holding the White House and at leastone of 
the two chambers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-03/trump-faces-referendum-as-voters-head-to-polls-in-midterm-elections-2026?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
 of Congress.

Episodes from the 1990s and 2010s showed how such a set-up could impose 
discipline on spending — essentially via Congress being unwilling to grant the 
president the fiscal firepower he might have otherwise wanted to deploy for his 
agenda. But economists and budget analysts are cautioning that may not be what 
unfolds after Tuesday’smidterm congressional elections 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-03/trump-faces-referendum-as-voters-head-to-polls-in-midterm-elections-2026?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
.

If polls from the final days 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-30/florida-nebraska-senate-races-shift-left-in-midterm-homestretch?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
 of the campaign are anything to go by, Democrats will at least take control of 
the House, bringing an end to “unified” Republican control of the presidency 
and the legislature.

Once the new Congress 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-01/2026-midterm-elections-what-a-democratic-win-would-mean-for-trump?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
 takes office in early January, there will then need to be some sort of 
bipartisan agreement on two key pieces of fiscal legislation in order to 
prevent, to use a non-technical term, chaos.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-02/midterms-put-washington-risk-in-focus-video?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
One is the regular appropriations needed to keep the government running. The 
Republican-controlled Congress will need to deal on its own with the first such 
hurdle, which looms Dec. 11 — that’s when the current temporary spending 
package runs out. Assuming the GOP funds the government through the rest of 
fiscal 2027, some deal will be needed with Democrats by Oct. 1 for 2028.

The second one is the federal debt ceiling, which, at the pace of issuance 
that the Treasury is running nowadays, is expected to be hit in the first few 
months of 2027. It’s not impossible that Republicansattempt to resolve this 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/gop-weighs-quick-us-debt-limit-hike-to-deny-democrats-leverage?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
 before they lose their majority, but that may be tough.

“Given time constraints and a number of Republicans who oppose a debt limit 
increase, this is not a likely outcome,” Citigroup economists Andrew 
Hollenhorst and Veronica Clark wrote in a recent note. Alec Phillips and Abhay 
Duggirala at Goldman Sachs had the same take: “Thin margins in the House and 
pushback from fiscal conservatives make this unlikely to succeed.”

That means President Donald Trump 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-01/2026-midterm-elections-what-a-democratic-win-would-mean-for-trump?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
 will need agreement with Democrats sometime before the Treasury runs out of 
sufficient funding for all of the government’s obligations, orsome sort of 
default 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-05-12/yellen-says-only-good-outcome-is-congress-raising-debt-ceiling?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
 will ensue. Economists see the drop-dead deadline for a deal as coming in the 
second half of 2027.

Democrats are likely to press for restoring spending on Medicare and food 
assistance previously cut by Republicans, both in the annual legislation for 
fiscal 2028 and/or the bill to increase or suspend the debt ceiling. 
Republicans, meantime, have their own spending-boost wish-list, on defense.

After an election cycle 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-us-election-results/?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
 that saw no big calls for reining inbudget deficits 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-16/us-posts-1-99-trillion-budget-gap-for-2026-as-spending-climbs?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
, the likelihood with these must-pass legislative items is for further 
largesse, rather than discipline. The implications “lean toward higher 
spending,” Goldman’s Phillips and Duggirala wrote. “A ‘win-win’ deal between 
the parties on spending would boost defense and non-defense funding.”

Bond vigilantes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-27/bond-market-doubts-grow-over-gridlock-as-cure-for-deficit-angst?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
, take note.

Need-to-Know Research

This year’s powerful El Niño 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/what-is-a-super-el-nino-how-it-affects-weather-and-food-production?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
 may have the effect of boosting next year’s US inflation and employment, 
according to San Francisco Fed economists writing in the central bank’s latest
economic letter 
<https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2026/11/the-impact-of-el-nino-on-the-us-economy/>
.

The economists looked at 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-02/el-nino-could-raise-us-inflation-boost-jobs-in-2027-fed-study?cmpid=BBD110326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10925>
 the 22 El Niño episodes dating back to the 1950s, along with current weather 
data indications. “The results of our historical analysis point, in particular, 
to a risk of higher inflation from the current episode,” which looks set to be 
a particularly strong El Niño. “Estimates imply a 1 percentage point increase 
in PCE prices over the course of 2027 related to this El Niño episode,” they 
wrote. (PCE is the Fed’s preferred inflation gauge.)

The bump to prices comes via food and services, which the economists didn’t 
break down. Employment tends to get a boost from El Niños thanks to warmer 
winters in the north — which encourage construction activity — the economists 
wrote.
