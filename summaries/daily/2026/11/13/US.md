**Labor Market and Inflation Dynamics**
The most consequential domestic macro development this week was the reassessment of labor market conditions. The October payrolls report brought the three-month average payroll gain to roughly 137,000 — more than double the pace economists generally consider consistent with flat labor supply — while the quits rate climbed to 2.1% in September, returning to its 2024 average. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh had characterized the job market as balanced as recently as August's Jackson Hole address, when the three-month average sat near 20,000. The sharp acceleration, combined with immigration-driven constraints on labor supply, has raised the prospect that employment could become a renewed source of wage and price pressure heading into 2027. New York Fed President Williams acknowledged the economy "seems to be strengthening" in the labor market, while simultaneously insisting there were no signs of overheating — itself a signal the debate is shifting.

**Monetary Policy**
Williams joined Daly and Goolsbee in signaling that additional rate increases remain necessary. Market pricing implies quarter-point hikes at each of the December, January, and March meetings. Treasury yields surged over the week — the two-day jump in the two-year yield was the largest since March 2023, and the 10-year rise the steepest since April 2025 — as stubborn inflation data and hawkish Fed rhetoric overwhelmed any safe-haven demand generated by equity volatility. The 10-year yield stood at 5.13%, and bond investors appear increasingly skeptical that Treasuries have reclaimed their traditional haven status.
