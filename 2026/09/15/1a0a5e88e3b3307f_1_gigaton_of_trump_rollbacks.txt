The collective impact of Trump’s reversals

By Zahra Hirji 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVypnhGtqak/zahra-hirji?cmpid=BBD091526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8912>
,Eric Roston 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQdgGkZhizQ/eric-roston?cmpid=BBD091526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8912>
 andLili Pike 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXt16hTqABg/lili-pike?cmpid=BBD091526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8912>

President Donald Trump’s broad rollbacks of federal greenhouse gas rules since 
January 2025 have altered the trajectory of US energy use and helped set back 
cuts to planet-warming pollution by about a decade.

The latest turnabout 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-14/trump-epa-axes-carbon-pollution-standards-for-coal-gas-plants?cmpid=BBD091526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8912>
 came on Monday when the Environmental Protection Agency eliminated 
requirements that fossil fuel power plants install technical controls to curb 
their emissions. The move came alongside a proposal to permanently release the 
nation’s largest industrial source of greenhouse gas emissions from federal 
oversight.

Each of these actions, which come just as data centers are sending US power 
demand surging, is set to boost emissions by a sizable chunk. For example, the 
EPA estimates the power plant repeal will result in an additional533 million 
metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions 
<https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-09/utilities_ria_final_partial_repeal_of_cps_2026-09.pdf>
 by 2040, which is more thanmost countries’ annual carbon footprint 
<https://www.worldometers.info/co2-emissions/co2-emissions-by-country/#google_vignette>
.

But it’s the collective effect of these and other administration moves that’s 
reshaping America’s carbon footprint.

A worker stands in the coal yard at the John E. Amos Power Plant in Winfield, 
West Virginia.Photographer: Luke Sharrett/Bloomberg
The world’s second-largest emitter after China may now see its contribution to 
climate change rise by roughly 1 gigaton more than it would have otherwise, in 
2040, according to oneanalysis by Energy Innovation 
<https://energyinnovation.org/report/economic-consumer-cost-and-pollution-impacts-of-federal-energy-policy-changes/>
, a nonpartisan energy and policy think tank. That’s the emissions equivalent 
of the energy used by 134 million houses in a year.

Under this analysis, which attempts to take into account the rescinding of 
climate controls for current and future coal and gas power plants, US emissions 
will still drop by 2040, but only to the level they would have roughly been in 
2030 had previous policies remained in place.

Under Biden’s policies, the US was on track to start dramatically cutting 
emissions in the next few years, saidRobbie Orvis 
<https://energyinnovation.org/bio/robbie-orvis/>, Energy Innovation’s senior 
director of modeling and analysis.

“The one positive note is we’re still on a downward decline even 
notwithstanding all of the policy changes — it’s just nowhere near as fast as 
it was nor is it anywhere close to where we should be going.”

Germany’s new green hurdle

By Petra Sorge 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AV7cfwcPiCo/petra-sorge?cmpid=BBD091526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8912>

Germany’s proposed renewable energy overhaul risks delaying projects and 
deterring investment by making them harder to finance, according to some of the 
country’s biggest banks.
Wind turbines and high voltage electricity transmission towers near 
Wusterhusen, Germany.Photographer: Krisztian Bocsi/Bloomberg
A group of 18 lenders warned the government that simultaneous changes to 
renewable projects’ revenues, costs and access to the grid will “jeopardize the 
planning certainty needed for investment and financing decisions,” per a letter 
seen by Bloomberg. “As a result, projects may be delayed, scaled back, or — if 
they are not economically viable or financially feasible — can no longer be 
implemented.”

The warning is the latest pushback from investors against the government’s plan
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-18/germany-to-slash-renewable-subsidies-as-solar-surge-tests-grids?cmpid=BBD091526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8912>
 to scale back renewable-energy subsidies as a way to tame energy prices. 
German consumers currently pay about €37 billion ($42.7 billion) a year in 
network fees, but the transmission grid is not keeping pace with the nation’s 
rapid renewable expansion.

As part of the sweeping reform proposal, grid operators would be allowed to 
reduce curtailment payments for new solar or wind farms in areas where the 
network is already frequently congested. As developers cannot know when and 
where a grid operator will declare such a bottleneck situation in the future, 
this could make business plans for new projects unprofitable, according to the 
letter from the Financing Advisory Board of the German Wind Energy Association.

Wildfire haze hits Singapore

By Gabrielle Ng 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXaNeCwLKQk/gabrielle-ng?cmpid=BBD091526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8912>
 andMegan Durisin Albery 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AROex3pWjCE/megan-durisin-albery?cmpid=BBD091526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260915&utm_content=8912>

Singapore was shrouded in haze on Tuesday, with smoke from wildfires in 
neighboring Indonesia raising islandwide pollution to levels that pose a health 
risk.
The city skyline is blanketed by haze in Singapore on Sept. 14.Photographer: 
Roslan Rahman/AFP/Getty Images
All five regions of the city-state recorded unhealthy air-quality levels, the 
first time that’s happened since September 2019, according to the National 
Environment Agency. Singapore’s air currently ranks among the 10 most polluted 
cities in the world, according to IQAirdata 
<https://www.iqair.com/world-air-quality-ranking>, with the capitals of 
Malaysia and Indonesia also among the top spots.

Indonesia often battles wildfires during its annual dry season, but hotspots 
this year have been higher than normal. There were more than9,000 
<https://sipongi.gakkum.kehutanan.go.id/grafik-titik-panas> recorded in August, 
the most for the month in data going back to 2015, and more than 7,000 have 
been recorded so far in September, according to the Forestry Ministry’s 
fire-monitoring platform SiPongi.
