German ‘Consequences’

A political earthquake is about to test Germany’s first meaningful growth 
recovery since before the pandemic.

The election win 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-07/putin-s-shadow-across-europe-grows-after-german-far-right-wins?cmpid=BBD090826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8616>
 on Sunday of the far-right Alternative for Germany, or AfD, in a state in the 
east of the country, has rocked Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s coalition and 
attractedglobal attention 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-07/putin-s-shadow-across-europe-grows-after-german-far-right-wins?cmpid=BBD090826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8616>
 for all the wrong reasons.

The extremist party secured 44% of the vote in Saxony-Anhalt, clinching a 
total of seats just short of a majority in its legislature. It poses one of the 
biggest challenges to the republic’s post-World War II order.

Even so, the economic implications are far from clear. For a start, the state 
is small, with only 2 million people or less than 3% of the national total. 
It’s Germany’s poorest region.

The bigger picture in Europe’s largest economy is that, after several lost 
years and false dawns, and with hundred of billions of euros in stimulus 
flowing into infrastructure and military spending, evidence is increasingly 
mounting of a sustained recovery finally taking hold.

Despite hiccups, as seen with drops in the most recent industrial production 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-07/german-industrial-output-unexpectedly-drops-most-in-almost-year?cmpid=BBD090826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8616>
 andexport 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/german-exports-unexpectedly-drop-for-first-time-since-january?cmpid=BBD090826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8616>
 data, all the signs point to a pickup. The Munich-based Ifo institute, which 
compiles a closely watched index of business sentiment, even raised its 
forecast for 2026 growth last week to 1.4%. That would be the fastest spurt of 
expansion in a non-pandemic year since 2017.

Companies are still nervous 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-07/german-business-owners-urge-faster-reforms-after-afd-victory?cmpid=BBD090826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8616>
 about the fallout, not least on the German brand among investors. Before the 
vote, Deutsche Bank AG Chief Executive Officer Christian Sewingspoke out 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-02/afd-election-fears-stoke-alarm-among-german-business-leaders?cmpid=BBD090826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8616>
 about how populist parties “propagate concepts that contradict the basis of 
our success.”

The main danger for now appears to be on the Berlin government’s push to pass 
pro-growth measures that might help drive future prosperity.

“You do have a change in the political landscape,” Jens Eisenschmidt, chief 
European economist at Morgan Stanley, told Bloomberg Television. “It will 
likely have an impact on the way reforms are conducted, the speed by which this 
is conducted. And of course you might get discussions around leadership.”

Merz and his coalition face further political tests in coming weeks, with 
state elections slated for Berlin and another German state on Sept. 20, and a 
secret ballot to install the state premier in Saxony-Anhalt in October.

“This day not only changed things in Saxony-Anhalt, but also Germany as a 
whole,”he told reporters 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-07/merz-moves-to-contain-fallout-from-far-right-rout-in-state-vote?cmpid=BBD090826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8616>
. “There will have to be consequences.”
