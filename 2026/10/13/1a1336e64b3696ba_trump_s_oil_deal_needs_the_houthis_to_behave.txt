Troubled Waters for Oil

The Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia’s airports are only the latest catalyst 
driving oil prices higher. Brent crude first-month futures are back to $106 per 
barrel, while the price for six months hence has reached a record as investors 
abandon the notion that this conflict will be over soon:

Oil is still swayed by geopolitical moves, and near its highest in a month as 
Riyadh weighs an appropriate response to thedeadly attacks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-10/saudi-arabia-suspends-operations-at-riyadh-airport-after-attack?cmpid=BBD101326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9961>
:

All of this is despite last week’s controversial 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/audio/2026-10-12/balance-of-power-trump-s-diesel-deal-stirs-gop-unease-podcast?cmpid=BBD101326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9961>
deal with Russia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/trump-says-putin-agreed-to-release-diesel-into-global-market-mv1bqka7?cmpid=BBD101326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9961>
 to lift sanctions on diesel exports, and President Donald Trump’s appeals for 
Ukraine 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-12/trump-s-diesel-deal-with-putin-risks-rift-with-zelenskyy-video?cmpid=BBD101326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9961>
 to desist from striking Russian energy infrastructure. Far from taking 
confidence, markets seem to be interpreting this as US desperation. Diesel is 
close to its all-time high, and moved little after the deal:

Tina Fordham of Fordham Global Foresight argues that investors could be 
underestimating the risks to energy prices and supply security, and called a 
turning point in US-Russian relations:
Russia conceded nothing, secured a reversal of US sanctions without 
congressional approval, later followed by White House demands that the EU also 
cease delivering weapons to Ukraine viathe PURL program 
<https://jeis-zcmp.maillist-manage.eu/click/13f60b40f50d93b8/13f60b40f50d8c0d>. 
The Treasury issued atemporary licence 
<https://jeis-zcmp.maillist-manage.eu/click/13f60b40f50d93b8/13f60b40f50d8c0e> 
within hours, barely a month after Trump signed a sweeping Russia sanctions law 
put forward by the late Senator Lindsey Graham, a Republican Russia hawk.
Any escalation elsewhere that erodes Trump’s efforts to cushion prices at the 
pump won’t be taken lightly. The question now is less whether Saudi Arabia 
retaliates so much as how far it might be prepared to roil an already strained 
energy market even further.

The US will be reluctant to deploy forces to assist the Saudis, but it’s hard 
to predict how this front will unfold. Bloomberg Intelligence’s Dina Esfandiary 
says it is not a zero-sum game:
While the Houthis don’t take their orders from Tehran, their recent moves help 
Iran up the ante in its stalemated war. But they also risk playing against 
Tehran: the Saudis have been anti-war and pushing for a deal between the US and 
Iran. Riyadh may feel increasingly cornered into pushing for US escalation 
against Iran — a move that wouldn’t end well for anyone involved.
So far, Houthi attacks on energy infrastructure have been relatively 
contained. This, BCA Research’s Marko Papic argues, may reflect a deliberate 
effort by the Houthis to avoid sending oil prices surging and thus putting 
themselves in Washington’s crosshairs:
The point is that the Houthis are rational and focused inward. Their intention 
is not regional domination or invasion of Saudi Arabia. Instead, they are 
focused on winning the contest for the domination of Yemen itself. And, in the 
service of that goal, they will make deals with external enemies when it serves 
their purpose.
They are no match for the might of the US. But the problems that America has 
haddealing with Iran in the Strait of Hormuz 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-08-05/hormuz-toll-oil-in-our-time-may-be-as-good-as-this-get?cmpid=BBD101326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9961>
, despite obvious military superiority, show that isn’t the point. Pushed into 
a corner, the Houthis’ ability to inflict damage on energy facilities or 
disrupt oil shipments throughthe Red Sea, the other key Middle East shipping 
route 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-07-24/game-theory-the-odyssey-and-bab-el-mandeb?cmpid=BBD101326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9961>
, cannot be dismissed.

One notable example is the East-West pipeline network and export complex in 
the industrial city of Yanbu. Weeks ago, an attack from anIraqi militia was 
effectively contained 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-14/asia-refiners-seek-saudi-updates-after-oil-pipeline-closure?cmpid=BBD101326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9961>
. Still, Capital Economics’ Jason Tuvey points out that there’s no guarantee 
another attack, this time from the Houthis, wouldn’t cause more lasting 
disruption. The pipeline can handle 7 million barrels a day, but limited 
capacity at Yanbu capped shipments of crude and refined products at 3.5 million 
to 4 million barrels per day from April to July, as this Capital Economics 
chart shows:

Any response that pushes the Houthis to broaden their attacks would be 
detrimental to oil prices. Unlike at the onset of the Iran war, oil prices are 
already elevated, and escalation aggravates the pain. A shutdown of routes that 
bypass Hormuz could easily bring the price past $120.

A Bullion Resistance

Gold is showing remarkable resilience despite one of its poorest runs in the 
past five years. The cocktail of factors weighing on it is led by the Federal 
Reserve’s return to rate hikes, which has driven the dollar to multi-month 
highs, and heightened inflation expectations caused by the Middle East war. 
Real 10-year yields, long regarded as having a key inverse relationship with 
the gold price as it pays no income, have risen a full percentage point this 
year to 2.88%. And yet while gold is down, it has only dropped about 3% during 
the same time:

This is damaging because gold has been seen as a hedge against debasement, or 
a fecklessly loose US monetary policy. Such fears drove its rally to record 
highs back in January. Since then, the Fed under Kevin Warsh has proven more 
hawkish than expected, and gold’s return as of last Friday is among the weakest 
in the last decade, behind only 2022, another year marked by a surprising 
return to rate hikes. This slide could have been a lot worse but for structural 
demand largely driven by central banks, which has somewhat put a floor under 
gold:

The World Gold Council’s most recent data on reserve accumulation show that 
the metal still appeals to monetary authorities, led by China, despite 
expectations of higher US interest rates. Does this demand have staying power? 
The council’s survey shows that 45% of policymakers plan to increase their 
holdings over the next year – mostly leaning on gold’s ability to hedge 
geopolitical risks as well as a long-term store of value:

Constraints on mine production have kept supply from keeping pace with demand. 
Output increased only 2% year-on-year in the second quarter. As Stonegate’s 
David Storms points out, new mines require years of exploration, permitting, 
financing and construction, while mature operations face grade decline and 
rising capital requirements. The bullish argument is that gold prices could 
re-rate well before the industry brings meaningful new production online.

Another support for gold is the continuous flow of money into gold-backed 
exchange-traded funds.September net inflows 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TMNMDPKJH6VD?cmpid=BBD101326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9961>
 surged more than in any month since the first quarter of 2022, according to 
Bloomberg:

Over the past two weeks, bullion has shrugged off structural headwinds and 
recouped about 2% of its losses. Whether the rebound holds will depend in part 
on September’s inflation data, due Wednesday. Record fuel prices could keep the 
latest reading elevated, with the both the headline (from 3.4% to 3.6%) and the 
core (from 2.4% to 2.5%) rates of CPI expected to tick upward. That might 
increase the pressure on bullion by raising the prospect of interest rates 
staying higher for longer. Against that, if the Trump administration’s deal 
with Russia to release more diesel works out — despite the many levels of 
political disagreement surrounding it — traders may be less bothered by 
backward-looking inflation numbers.

Beyond this immediate catalyst, TD Securities’ Ryan McKay argues that 
geopolitical risks, fiscal concerns, dollar debasement, de-dollarization and 
stagflation fears remain firmly in play. That would allow gold to decouple from 
real interest rates and embark on a new bull run into 2027. Louis-Vincent Gave 
of Gavekal Research holds a similar favorable outlook:
In spite of its recent pullback, gold continues to outperform all major bond 
markets since 2019. We thus have to assume we remain in a “currency debasement” 
environment in which “cash is trash.”
The big spike in yields even as gold’s price remains relatively contained 
underscores the extent of bullion’s resistance. While it’s premature to suggest 
a new norm, it’s striking how gold prices have held up in the face of surging 
yields:

A favorable outlook would feed into gold equities. Daniel Major of UBS 
projects that gold prices above $4,000 an ounce would help margins, making free 
cash flows attractive. But he he still urges caution, looking to avoid negative 
cost guidance or production downgrades and mergers and acquisitions’ risk:
We expect more M&A, and while not every deal is poorly received by the market, 
our strategy in large caps is avoiding potential deals and favoring sustained 
cash returns versus identifying potential mid/small cap targets.
Complications in the bull case for gold remain. If rates stay higher for 
longer, it will have to contend with a stronger dollar and higher costs of 
holding an asset that pays no income. Those forces could blunt any rally, even 
as central-bank buying and geopolitical uncertainty continue to lend support.

Back in 1993, I was lucky enough to sing in his backing choir when he was at 
the peak of his popularity andperforming before crowds of 20,000 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EBFLI0ngNBw&list=PLaOrMciSnv6-ItbFl6hImj4Iz00SR9zz_>
 — and soon discovered whymusicians didn’t like working with him 
<https://www.theguardian.com/world/2004/aug/05/biography.arts>. Downright rude 
<https://www.theguardian.com/world/2000/apr/14/rorycarroll1> much of the time 
and unapologetic that he didn’t feel equal to singingNessun Dorma 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q_hLh4qCqpg&list=RDQ_hLh4qCqpg&start_radio=1>,

used as the theme song for the 1990 World Cup. This didn’t make him any less of 
an artist, but it was difficult to accept that he seemed to have taken his 
God-given talent
