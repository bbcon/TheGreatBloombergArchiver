Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Senior Reporter Alex Longley looks at the causes of a tumultuous period for 
crude prices.

A week is a long time in the oil market.

Last Friday, crude futures were rising toward $120 a barrel as the risk of 
further US attacks on Iran loomed large. Seven days later, prices have tumbled, 
shedding as much as $25 from their high.

The Democrats’ triumph 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-04/democrats-win-control-of-the-house-ddhq-projects?cmpid=BBBXT110626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11108>
 in the US midterms accounted for a large part of the rout, with traders 
betting there won’t be an escalation in the conflict with Iran — something that 
would haverisked further retaliation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-31/iran-s-khamenei-orders-military-to-expand-us-retaliation-plans?cmpid=BBBXT110626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11108>
 against regional oil supplies.

But listening to top energy and trading executives at a major oil conference 
in the United Arab Emirates this week, there were signs of weakness in the 
market even before President Donald Trump’s party received an electoral 
thumping.

Several said at the event they saw crude supplies as robust 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/oil-s-cooling-geopolitical-risk-lays-bare-a-well-supplied-market?cmpid=BBBXT110626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11108>
, with exports from the Persian Gulf surging just as demand in various corners 
of the globe fell markedly from a year earlier. In China, the world’s largest 
buyer, imports remain far below prewar levels.

Executives from Adnoc, Abu Dhabi’s national oil company, emphasized their 
resilience 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-02/uae-says-it-will-be-better-prepared-for-any-energy-disruptions?cmpid=BBBXT110626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11108>
 in the face of the Iran war. Chief Executive Officer Sultan Al Jaber told the 
opening ceremony of the conference that “energy leadership is not measured by 
what you produce in good times. It is measured by what you deliver in hard 
times.”

Right now Middle East producers are delivering a lot. Combined exports from 
Saudi Arabia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-03/saudi-aramco-profit-rises-9-as-war-drives-oil-prices-higher?cmpid=BBBXT110626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11108>
, Kuwait, Iraq, the UAE and others through the Strait of Hormuz and other 
bypass routes are running around prewar levels. Flows can be volatile from week 
to week, but the overall trend has been rising.

Conditions were ripe for the rug to be pulled from under crude prices once 
fears abated of a worsening conflict.

But global oil markets aren’t out of the woods yet 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-nov-6?cmpid=BBBXT110626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261106&utm_content=11108>
. Trump’s next move isn’t clear and he lacks an obvious off-ramp from his war 
with Iran. Tehran’s intentions, and its resilience in the face of the US 
economic blockade, are also hard to gauge.

Just as traders’ bets on escalation last Friday proved to be misplaced, being 
certain of peace today may be equally foolhardy.

—Alex Longley, Bloomberg News
