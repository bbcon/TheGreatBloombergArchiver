The world’s No. 1 container carrier is partially restoring services through 
the Red Sea and Suez Canal, joining rivals in slowly returning to the shortcut 
that major shipping lines have mostly avoided since late 2023.

MSC issued the advisory 
<https://www.msc.com/en/newsroom/customer-advisories/2026/august/resumption-of-selected-east%E2%80%93west-services-in-the-red-sea-region?_gl=1*19njcsn*_up*MQ..*_ga*MTQ3OTczOTc0My4xNzg3NzM3NjI1*_ga_9HMJRMP77C*czE3ODc3Mzc2MjQkbzEkZzAkdDE3ODc3Mzc2MjQkajYwJGwwJGgwJGQ1ZUR4N0FjcHJYX0FXTE5SZkE0ck1nYjVyZER6QWNjS193*_ga_2WVVR2C39J*czE3ODc3Mzc2MjQkbzEkZzAkdDE3ODc3Mzc2MjQkajYwJGwwJGgwJGRURDkzRFJNdHhGNzR2Q3NKQXVmR2poejBjbEd4RW9CeXZB>
 earlier this week, saying the return follows “a comprehensive review of the 
latest security and operational conditions in the Red Sea region.” The 
Geneva-based company’s shift applies to sailings in both directions between 
Asia and Europe.

The move comes almost two months after partial rerouting plans were announced 
by Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd — European partners in a capacity-sharing alliance 
called Gemini.

“These steps are sparking some optimism that we are seeing the start of a 
gradual return to normal levels of container traffic through the waterway,” 
Judah Levine, head of research with Freightos, wrote in a note Tuesday.

 that it’s reasonable to think Red Sea routings could return to normal by year 
end.

Daily northbound and southbound Suez transits by container ships remain 
depressed compared with their pre-2024 levels, but they have been inching 
higher in recent weeks:

Cargo ships have largely avoided the Red Sea since late 2023, when Yemen-based 
Houthis started attacking vessels in solidarity with Palestinians during a 
conflict with Israel.

The subsequent war in Iran intensified the risks, delayed plans for Suez 
returns and cut traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. Iranian and Omani 
officials this weekhave discussed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-25/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-aug-26?cmpid=BBD082626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260826&utm_content=8121>
 an “interim framework” aimed at resuming shipping through Hormuz.

Why does the return to Suez matter? Because the diversions stretched capacity 
and helped underpin container rates, so the shorter routes should help add 
capacity back to the market and relieve some of the pressure. That’s important 
now because, as Levine pointed out recently, there’s been an increase in port 
congestion “that just won’t seem to go away.”

“The current spike in congestion can be attributed to external shocks like 
recent storms and drought,” Levine wrote. “Major ports in the Far East and 
especially Europe have been plagued with higher-than-normal delays due to 
steady increases in volumes that are pushing past port capacity levels.”
