Muck piles up

By Kyle Stock 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ARKz8x17rNs/kyle-stock?cmpid=BBD090826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8272>

The federal government finally stepped in to referee the Colorado River 
earlier this summer, cooling one of the most pitched battles in the war for 
water rights that has engulfed much of the American West.

Arizona, California and Nevada will have to reduce the amount of water they 
take from the river by about one-quarter for the next two years. The goal is to 
let reservoirs along the Colorado fill up in the midst of a prolonged drought 
and one of the lowest snowpacks on record.

But while the top of the lakes may rise again, an arguably bigger problem is 
lurking at the bottom, and the government is doing little about it. A steady 
buildup of sediment – sand and silt and mud – is shrinking the nation’s water 
storage capacity precisely when we need it most.

Bloomberg spent months poring over more than 100 years of federal sediment 
surveys and talking to hydrologists. What emerged is a troubling picture: Of 
the reservoirs we analyzed, nearly one in three are more than 10% full of 
sediment.

Sediment is natural and it was entirely expected when engineers built 
America’s more than 90,000 dams. But those dams are, on average, 64 years old 
and we’ve done little to address the rising sediment levels, or measure them, 
for that matter. In the midst of a historic water crisis, we still don’t have 
an accurate picture of just how much water we have stored.
As the West grapples with record-low snow and rainfall, crucial reservoirs 
like Strontia Springs are experiencing diminished storage capacity.
Photographer: Chet Strange/Bloomberg
“You can’t see the problem unless you’re looking for it, and we’re kind of not 
looking,” said Aaron Hurst, a geomorphologist who left the Bureau of 
Reclamation in 2024.

The surveys suggest we probably have even less water than we think. Sure, we 
have a decent picture of sediment in our largest reservoirs, but data is scarce 
on the tens of thousands of smaller ones, where sediment tends to stack up more 
quickly. The Colorado River, for example, feeds 15 storage lakes, though we 
usually only hear about the two biggest: Lake Mead and Lake Powell.

When factoring in those smaller bodies of water, the country has lost an 
estimated 8% of its water storage capacity, the equivalent of 25.5 million 
Olympic-sized swimming pools, according toa study 
<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-76986-3> published this week in 
Nature.

Federal engineers say they haven’t been given enough money to stay on top of 
the problem. External hydrologists, in turn, say the government would be smart 
to spend a little on it now, rather than a lot later.

Ultimately, the muck is expensive, threatening to clog billions of dollars in 
economic activity from hydroelectric power and agriculture. Farmers, in 
particular, are spending millions each year to pump extra water from wells, 
which, in turn,inflates prices  
<https://static.waltonfamilyfoundation.org/17/51/1485324048cca60290ba76007743/state-of-the-basin-2026-a-guide-to-the-colorado-river-crisis-1.pdf>
at the grocery store.
A water-quality measuring tool in Strontia Springs Reservoir.Photographer: 
Chet Strange/Bloomberg
Climate change, meanwhile, may force the issue. Its symptoms, including 
drought, wildfire and more volatile, intense rainfall, are all recipes for huge 
sediment surges.

Google aims for cleaner flights

By Saritha Rai 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASxkTfI9dW8/saritha-rai?cmpid=BBD090826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8272>

Alphabet Inc 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/GOOGL:US?cmpid=BBD090826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8272>
.’sGoogle 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/8888000D:US?cmpid=BBD090826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8272>
 andCathay Pacific Airways Ltd. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/293:HK?cmpid=BBD090826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8272>
 are putting artificial intelligence to work on one of the aviation industry’s 
most visible but least-addressed climate problems.

Contrails, the crisp white condensation plumes that often trail aircraft 
flying at high altitudes, are composed of frozen water vapor attached to engine 
exhaust particles. While they mar blue skies and are the basis ofconspiracy 
theories 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2019-07-25/conspiracy-theorists-abound-in-climate-change-conversation-on-youtube-study-finds?cmpid=BBD090826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8272>
, their main risk is as anenvironmental hazard 
<https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/are-contrails-harmful-to-the-environment>
 by trapping heat — just like naturally forming cirrus clouds. Contrails are 
responsible for roughly one-third of aviation’s total climate impact, according 
to Google.
Contrails are composed of frozen water vapor attached to engine exhaust 
particles.Photographer: Olivier Chassignole/AFP/Getty Images
But this effect can be reduced significantly based on early-stage operational 
trials conducted in late 2025 on several dozen Cathay routes using Google’s AI 
algorithms, the companies said Monday. A pilot project achieved a 40% decline 
in the warming impact of contrails by simply prompting pilots to avoid 
atmospheric sweet spots where they tend to form.

Cathay is Google’s first airline partner in the Asia-Pacific region to try out 
its AI-powered contrail mitigation technology, and the first to test contrail 
avoidance globally on ultra-long haul flights. Google has previously partnered 
withAmerican Airlines Group Inc. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/AAL:US?cmpid=BBD090826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8272>
 on similar trials.

Champagne feels the heat

By Nayla Razzouk 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AP4EwDhUUiw/nayla-razzouk?cmpid=BBD090826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260908&utm_content=8272>

France’s Champagne output is set to fall by almost half from 2025 as extreme 
weather batters yields.
French Champagne bottles in a refrigerated display cabinet at a store in 
Marseille, France.Photographer: Jeremy Suyker/Bloomberg
Champagne production is estimated to fall to 1.34 million hectoliters, 48% 
below last year, according to astatement 
<https://agreste.agriculture.gouv.fr/agreste-web/disaron/IraVit26107/detail/> 
from the Agriculture Ministry. Overall, wine production in 2026 is estimated at 
34 million hectoliters, down 6% year-on-year and 17% below the five-year 
average.

The drop in production reflects a reduction in vineyard area and weaker yields 
after this year’s scorching temperatures and drought. The national harvest 
would be one of the smallest in the past 30 years, according to the statement, 
which is based on data through Sept. 1.
