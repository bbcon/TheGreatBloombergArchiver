AI’s great repricing is gathering force, and Asia is taking the hardest hit. 
South Korea’s Kospiplunged 8% 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-15/chip-stocks-deepen-losses-as-concerns-mount-over-rates-ai-debt?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10129>
, extending declines from its June peak to almost 40%, as investors raced out 
of the semiconductor names that had powered the market higher. SK Hynix and 
Samsung dropped more than 9% each, while foreign investors have dumped more 
than $10 billion of Korean shares this month. Japan’s Nikkei 225 slid almost 
5%. The trigger is bigger than chips: HotterUS inflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/us-consumer-prices-climb-by-most-since-april-on-fuel-costs?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10129>
 has revived fears of Fed tightening, while distress inCoreWeave’s debt 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/coreweave-s-brazen-dream-crashes-into-reality-as-yields-top-15?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10129>
 is forcing investors to confront the financing bill behind the AI buildout. 
Korea, one of the purest expressions of the boom, is now becoming its shock 
absorber.

That tension runs straight through the IMF’s latest outlook for Asia. The fund 
actually raised its 2026 growth forecast for the region to 4.8%, in large part 
because the AI investment boom has been such a powerful tailwind for economies 
including Korea and Taiwan. But it seesgrowth slowing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-15/imf-sees-asia-s-growth-slowing-despite-ai-fueled-resilience?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10129>
 to 4.4% next year, and warns that the very engine cushioning Asia today could 
become its vulnerability tomorrow. A correction in AI investment or valuations 
would hit a region sitting at the center of the global technology supply chain 
particularly hard, while oil-importing economies such as Bangladesh, Cambodia 
and Laos face the opposite problem as they bear the cost of higher energy 
prices without enjoying much of the AI windfall.

And then there’s Anthropic, whose numbers neatly capture how the market’s AI 
debate has changed. The company delivered a striking$16.8 billion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/anthropic-revenue-jumps-to-16-8-billion-in-quarter-before-ipo?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10129>
 in quarterly revenue, positive free cash flow and an 85% adjusted gross 
margin. These are results that should bolster the IPO expected by year-end. 
Until a year ago, numbers like those might have lifted the whole AI complex. 
But investors now increasinglywant to know 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-15/anthropic-results-may-aid-ipo-but-not-ai-trade-analysts-say?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10129>
 not simply whether AI can generate spectacular growth, but whether the 
enormous compute and infrastructure spending required to sustain it can earn 
adequate returns when capital is expensive. Essentially, the market has stopped 
asking how big AI can get and started asking who will pay for it.
