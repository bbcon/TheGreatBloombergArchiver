War and drought

By Laura Millan 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD110726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261107&utm_content=11132>
 andSelcan Hacaoglu 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQ6hs25pQys/selcan-hacaoglu?cmpid=BBD110726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261107&utm_content=11132>

Even after the start of Syria’s civil war in 2011, beekeeper Muhammad Salih 
regularly traveled along the country’s northern border with Turkey in search of 
the best spots to produce the honey that sustained his family. It wasn’t until 
four years later, when Islamic State militants took control of his hometown and 
killed his brother-in-law along with his seven children, that Salih decided it 
was time to move for good.

“We buried them and crossed into Turkey,” said the 46-year-old, who now lives 
in Karkamis, a small Turkish town near the Syrian border. “I had to sell all 
250 of my beehives for a third of what they would have been worth if I hadn’t 
been in such a rush.”

But while Salih has left sectarian violence behind, he hasn’t been able to 
escape extreme weather. Drought has cut his honey production in half over the 
past five years. This year, the rains returned, but brought intense flooding 
and hail that destroyed the flowers his business depends on.
Syrian refugee workers lay an irrigation pipe at a pistachio plantation in 
Karkamis.Photographer: Selcan Hacaoglu/Bloomberg
Over the past decade, Turkey has hosted one of the largest refugee populations 
in the world — millions of Syrians for whom an increasingly unpredictable cycle 
of heat, drought and floods has continuously shaped daily routines as well as 
long-term prospects. Severe drought compounded the economic and social 
pressures that ultimately led to Syria’s uprising. But while the civil war 
ended in late 2024, man-made climate change has supercharged the extreme and 
erratic weather that is hampering people’s efforts to rebuild their lives on 
both sides of the border, threatening agricultural production in Turkey and 
complicating Syria’s economic and social recovery. Flooding along the banks of 
the Euphrates river in Syria this year displaced over 3,600 people.

Their experience is a harbinger of a broader threat. Almost 90 million 
individuals, or three-quarters of the world’s forcibly displaced people, live 
in countries that are highly exposed to climate hazards, according to theUnited 
Nations 
<https://www.unhcr.org/publications/no-escape-frontlines-climate-change-conflict-and-forced-displacement>
. What’s happening in Turkey is an emblem of how, even when war ends, global 
warming can make displacement and destitution all but intractable for vast 
numbers of people.
Syrian refugee Zahra Jasib outside her home near the Turkish town of Harran.
Photographer: Laura Millan/Bloomberg
As the COP31 climate summit begins in Turkey next week, the impact of global 
warming on the world’s most vulnerable populations will be in focus. Nearly 4 
million displaced people have returned to Syria over the past two years, 
according toNovember data 
<https://www.unhcr.org/media/no-escape-iii-displaced-voices-frontlines-climate-crisis>
 from the UN’s refugee agency. Their ability to rebuild depends on replacing 
infrastructure wrecked during the 14-year war, but also on ensuring efforts 
account for growing climate pressures, the agency said.

Residents salvage belongings following flash floods in Nepal’s Nuwakot 
district.Photographer: Arun Sankar/AFP/Getty Images
Adding to the challenges this year is the Iran war, which has pushed climate 
action down the agenda for many governments as they prioritize security of 
energy supply. And for the second year in a row, the US won’t be sending an 
official delegation to COP, skipping its last chance to participate in 
negotiations before its withdrawal from the UN Framework Convention on Climate 
Change (UNFCCC) takes effect in February.

The gathering has been billed as a “COP of implementation 
<https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/4th_Presidency_Letter_signed_Kurum_Bowen.pdf>
” that seeks to translate talk into action. COP discussions have a tendency to 
get bogged down in the minutiae. Securing an agreement at the end of the summit 
will require more than 190 countries to reach a consensus.
