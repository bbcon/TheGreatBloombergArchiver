Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Asia Energy Team Leader Stephen Stapczynski explores why the high cost of 
imports might make Asia’s emerging economies think twice about liquefied 
natural gas.

LNG was once touted as a cheap and abundant energy source for Asia’s emerging 
economies. Five months of war in the Middle East — and hundreds of millions of 
dollars in unforeseen costs — is threatening this hypothesis.

Since the conflict began, Pakistan and Bangladesh alone have spent a combined 
$1.9 billion on at least 30 spot cargoes to replace canceled shipments and 
stave off crippling energy shortages, according to Bloomberg calculations.

Under their long-term contracts with Qatar, these same volumes would have cost 
roughly $900 million.

With every day that the Strait of Hormuz remains essentially closed, Asia’s 
pivot to LNG is proving more expensive.

The strain is most visible 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/iran-war-forces-cash-strapped-asian-nations-to-buy-expensive-lng?cmpid=BBBXT072926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7115>
in South Asia, where Bangladesh and Pakistan have slashed gas supplies to 
factories. When electricity simply isn’t available, rolling blackouts have been 
imposed.

Those measures have bought time, but they haven’t solved the problem. So the 
countries are shifting their focus back to investing in domestic production, 
coal and renewables to feed industrial demand.
QatarEnergy has extended force majeure on shipments from its Ras Laffan 
complex in the Gulf state.Photographer: Karim Jaafar/AFP/Getty Images
The crisis exposes LNG’s fundamental weakness: inherently, it’s an expensive 
product. Sometimes described as the “champagne of fuels 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-16/video-how-the-iran-war-triggered-a-global-natural-gas-shock?cmpid=BBBXT072926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7115>
,” it must be cooled to -163C (-260F) in liquefaction plants that cost billions 
of dollars.

Then, it must be shipped in specialized tankers that function like giant 
thermos flasks before being warmed back into gas at equally expensive import 
terminals.

Over years, the industry convinced governments across Asia that LNG could 
compete with domestic coal as a greener and more affordable bridge fuel. 
Long-term contracts were designed to make that possible, smoothing out price 
spikes and shielding buyers from volatility.

But every major disruption — from Russia’s invasion of Ukraine to the ongoing 
conflict in the Middle East — has revealed the same vulnerability. When 
contracted supply disappears, cash-strapped nations must dig deep into their 
pockets to secure replacement fuel, or else face blackouts.

This week, QatarEnergy — operator of the world’s largest LNG export plant — 
extended force majeure 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-28/qatarenergy-extends-lng-force-majeure-for-european-buyers?cmpid=BBBXT072926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7115>
 on shipments for Asian consumers into September.

The longer the crisis continues, the further Asia’s emerging economies will 
revert to safer alternatives. And that’s bad news for LNG, which had banked on 
the region for the next leap forward in demand.

—Stephen Stapczynski, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Limited LNG supply under construction in the Pacific Basin could leave room 
for a new investment cycle before regional output declines after 2030, 
according to BloombergNEF. The area represents just 14% of new capacity being 
built, potentially leaving Asian buyers more reliant on long-haul imports from 
outside their home basin.
