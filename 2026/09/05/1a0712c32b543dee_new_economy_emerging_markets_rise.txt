The big story in global markets the past couple of months has been a slide in 
developed-nation government bonds that’s sent yields to thehighest levels in 
decades 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/japan-s-10-year-bond-yield-hits-3-for-first-time-since-1996-mti4e8ui?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
. With the US and other major economies having failed to contain inflation or 
rein in their public borrowing, investors are demanding higher compensation.

This kind of dynamic, decades ago, would have proved disastrous for emerging 
markets, and for a logical reason. If the rich world’s bonds are offering
notably higher yields 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/australian-benchmark-bond-yield-jumps-to-level-last-seen-in-2011?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
, then why would you want to take the extra risk of diving into the developing 
world? You would expect premiums to surge on those countries’ securities.

Except they aren’t. Far from shunning supposedly riskier countries, investors 
continue to give them money. Pakistan this weekraised a record amount 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/pakistan-raises-3-billion-in-two-part-junk-bond-sale?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
 in an issue of dollar bonds. Demand was so strong that the spreads over US 
Treasuries tightened by a quarter percentage point during the sale process. 
More broadly, the JPMorgan Chase & Co. EMBI+ gauge of emerging market bonds’ 
premiums over the US remained stable — this week’s readings were down slightly 
from the start of the year.

So what’s changed since the 1980s Latin American debt crisis or the 1990s 
Asian financial crisis? Partly it’s that many developing countries have hugely 
improved their macroeconomic policymaking. As this newsletter noted in 2022,
lessons learned 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2022-10-08/debt-lessons-have-hardened-these-economies-new-economy-saturday?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
 from past crises helped emerging markets weather the US Federal Reserve’s 
drastic monetary tightening remarkably well. By 2023, some were saying it was a 
“watershed moment 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2023-01-06/what-s-happening-in-the-world-economy-emerging-markets-turn-optimistic?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
” for the asset class.

Now another narrative is gaining currency: emerging markets don’t look quite 
so risky on a relative basis because developed markets, and especially 
benchmark Treasuries, don’t appear quite so safe anymore. Just last month, 
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent seemed concerned enough about the state of the 
US government debt market that he announced ahighly unusual 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/long-dated-treasuries-rally-as-treasury-boosts-bond-buybacks?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
, out-of-cycle expansion in his department’s program to buy back outstanding 
bonds.

“In recent years, the world has turned upside down in more ways than one,” 
Udith Sikand, senior emerging markets analyst at Gavekal Research, wrote in a 
recent note. “EMs are the new DMs.”

The developed world effectively “dropped the ball” in recent years on the 
inflation front, Sikand said.

Indeed, the latest US consumer price index reading, due on Sept. 11, is 
expected to show a 3.4% annualinflation rate 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/fed-rate-decision-still-hangs-on-inflation-after-jobs-report?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
. That’s still more than a percentage point above the Fed’s target five years 
on from when prices started spiking higher. Euro-zone inflation is also 
elevated, while the debate in Japan is about how far behind the curve the 
central bank is in restoring price stability.

Many emerging nations have done a better job, Sikand and others argue. It 
shouldn’t, then, come as a surprise that some have lower 10-year yields than 
the US. Malaysia’s yields were just under 4% at this week’s close, with 
Thailand’s yields under 2.2%. Ten-year Treasuries meanwhile were over 4.75%.

The latest increases in US yields have come just as energy prices resumed 
their upward climb, sparked by a war with Iran that showsno sign of ending 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/-we-are-stalled-us-iran-conflict-stuck-with-no-end-in-sight?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
 soon. Before the US joined Israel in launching strikes at the end of February, 
10-year Treasury rates were under 4%. Bessent has argued that energy prices 
will come down in time, in part as Persian Gulf producersbypass 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/bessent-says-hormuz-will-soon-be-worthless-as-oil-bypasses-it?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
 the Strait of Hormuz, and that will in turn pull down yields.
US gasoline prices averaged $4.15 per gallon as of Thursday, the highest price 
ever for September.Photographer: Micah Green/Bloomberg
But market participants point to other, deeper concerns, and not just with the 
US but with Japan, France and other developed nations. In the wake of outsized 
spending during the Covid-19 pandemic, few have moved to reduce fiscal 
largesse. Some leaders, including Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, have 
even explored boosting subsidies to households to cope with higher costs. This 
week, the nation’s ministrieswent big 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/japan-ministries-seek-larger-budget-as-fiscal-questions-mount?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
 in their annual spending requests.

To put it succinctly, “governments are reticent to reduce budget deficits 
because it is unpopular,” as Steven Barrow, head G10 strategist at Standard 
Bank in London, wrote in a note this week.

In the case of the US, the federal government is adding roughly $2 trillion in 
extra debt each year. In an April report, the International Monetary Fund tied 
this to the reduced relative attractiveness of that debt. “The increase in the 
US Treasury security supply iscompressing the safety premium 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-15/imf-says-treasuries-losing-premium-warns-us-on-debt-management?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
 that US Treasuries have traditionally commanded,” the fund said.

Bessent last month promised a new “fiscal consolidation” plan, set to be 
released within days, to help boost confidence. But this week, he indicated it 
may bemonths away 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-31/bessent-talked-with-druckenmiller-touts-us-debt-outperformance?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
. In any case, budget watchers highlight thatthere’s little chance 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-24/bessent-s-murky-deficit-plan-faces-grim-prospects-in-congress?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
 of Congress backing any significant tightening.

To be sure, the US is far from heading toward an outright debt crisis, said 
Barrow, a longtime market and economic observer. “The global dominance of the 
Treasury market and the dollar seemingly gives the US access to funding the 
debt” that other nations can only dream of, he said.
Guillermo TolosaPhotographer: Ana Ferreira/Bloomberg
But, as Uruguay central bank chief Guillermo Tolosa told me 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-04-25/rejoicing-over-normal-recessions-in-south-america-new-economy?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
 earlier this year, things now are “probably not that risk-free in the advanced 
world” as they had been. And that may redound to the developing world’s 
benefit. One sign of that: in Uruguay, investors’ relativeattraction to dollars 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/uruguay-s-push-to-de-dollarize-boosted-by-new-money-market-funds?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
 is subsiding, Bloomberg reported this week, in favor of peso-denominated money 
market funds and government bonds.

And they’re not alone. It’s been a stellar year for emerging-market bonds, with
cash flowing in 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-30/dollar-debasement-backs-bull-case-for-emerging-bonds-funds-say?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8377>
. Emerging-market local-currency bonds have gained about 3.5% this year, while 
their developed-nation peers have dropped 1.7%, Bloomberg total return indexes 
showed.

That this happened in an environment where US yields were climbing showcases 
how things are different this time.
