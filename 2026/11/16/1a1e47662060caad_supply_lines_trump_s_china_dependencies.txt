There could end up being something clarifying about US President Donald 
Trump’s trip this week to China and his plans to meet Thursday with Xi Jinping 
and Vladimir Putin.

Almost exactly a decade after his election in part on a vow to rebalance the 
US’s economic relationship with China,Trump’s visit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/weakened-at-home-trump-looks-to-flex-power-at-putin-xi-summit?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>
 points to how he has come to prioritize his relationship with Xi over the 
decoupling many anticipated his political victory would bring. Which in turn 
points to how even as the US throws up tariff walls and other tools intended to 
disentangle its ties to China, it is continually discovering how dependent it 
remains.

That is often reduced to a story about rare earths 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/rare-earth-strategic-metals-price-gap-flags-supply-strains-trump-xi-meet?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>
 andstrategic choke points 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-12/world-trade-s-achilles-heel-grows-as-ai-boom-fuels-risks?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>
. It is much broader than that.

For almost two years I have been tracking one entrepreneur’s efforts to reduce 
his company’s dependence on China and set up a factory outside Pittsburgh. The 
result is my Bloomberg Businessweek story about Ben Kander and drinkware maker 
Welly published this morning and in the new China-themedprint edition 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2026-china-issue/?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>
 of the magazine.

Related: Futuristic Shenzhen Shows Trump the Difficulty of Stopping China 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-15/futuristic-shenzhen-shows-trump-the-difficulty-of-stopping-xi-jinping-s-china?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>

The basic story is that Kander discovered that to bring production of his 
steel coffee mugs and water bottles home to Pittsburgh, he needed to buy a 
Chinese factory and import it. It started arriving in 42 shipping containers 
this past January. Kander and his team are now in the final stages of sorting 
out the kinks and getting production up and running.

But freeing himself of Chinese production has also meant discovering a new 
dependency on China. It’s not just that Welly’s machines were installed by 
Chinese engineers who flew to Pittsburgh and trained Kander’s American 
employees. The machines’ operating system is Chinese. The molds Welly needs 
come from China. Kander’s expansion plans depend on eventually importing 
another line from China. If anything goes wrong his team jumps on WeChat, the 
Chinese social media app, to talk to a support team in China.

Ask Kander and he will tell you that eliminating one dependency means 
discovering another.

As I write in the story 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-16/welly-s-plight-shows-bringing-manufacturing-back-to-the-us-still-relies-on-china?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>
:
“There’s a story of China and America and manufacturing and so much of that 
manufacturing leaving America. We’re trying to bring it back,” Kander told me 
during one of our many conversations over the span of nearly two years. “And 
what’s funny here is we’re relying on the Chinese to build our factory.” He 
paused for a beat, registering what he’d just said. “What an irony.”
The reality today is that America’s manufacturing sector doesn’t just compete 
with China. It often depends on China for components and machinery.

There is of course another dependency that’s been highlighted in a 
high-profile way over the past couple of weeks in the downfall of Nvidia’s 
Jensen Huang.

It’s not just a story about romance, espionage, technology, AI or geopolitics. 
It’s about Trump’s instinct to do business and his attraction to other powerful 
men. And also his recognition of how dependent American companies remain on 
China as a market and how important those businesses are to US equity investors 
and retirement accounts.

Related: ‘I’m a Piece of Sh—’: Jensen Huang’s Last Hours as Nvidia CEO 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-13/inside-jensen-huang-s-final-hours-as-the-ceo-of-nvidia?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>

Nvidia will report earnings on Tuesday. By some estimates 
<https://culperresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Culper_NVDA_5-13-2026.pdf>
 as much as 20% of its revenues are linked to China. As we know now, that is 
not just a story about mainland China or alleged smuggling of advanced chips. 
It’s also linked to a data center boom in places like Malaysia that depends 
heavily on trans-national demand for computing power from China.

The foundation of the Jensen Huang tale isn’t about geopolitics, espionage or 
a CEO’s bad choices. It’s about the vast amount of profits US companies still 
derive from doing business with China. Just ask Apple or Tesla.

Lutnick’s Role

Had we not learned what we did about Huang’s affair 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-15/nvidia-s-ceo-search-draws-fire-with-bessent-s-call-for-shakeup?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>
 with an alleged Chinese spy and a subordinate, there is a very good chance 
that the former Nvidia CEO would be joining Trump on his trip to China. Or at 
the very least offering his counsel.

Things may change in the days to come as we learn more about the role of Trump 
administration members like Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick in the Nvidia 
saga. Or as the blame wars inside the administration continue and pressure from 
Congress grows.

Related: Lutnick Under Fire as Bessent Blames Commerce in Nvidia Crisis 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-13/nvidia-crisis-prompts-scrutiny-of-lutnick-s-role-in-chip-policy?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>

The likelihood, though, is that Trump will seek to move on without even 
raising the issue with Xi or pursuing any stark change in policy or personnel. 
Because Trump has never beenmuch of a China hawk 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-24/trump-s-real-china-strategy-is-doing-deals-not-decoupling?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>
. What makes actual China hawks in Washington queasy is that they believe Trump 
prioritizes his relationship with people like Huang or Xi over what ought to be
national security priorities 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-05/china-hawks-grow-queasy-over-trump-s-push-for-deals-with-beijing?cmpid=BBD111626_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11432>
.

The indications so far are that Trump is less concerned about the fall of 
Huang than he is about keeping the relationship with Xi on track. That’s not 
just about bonhomie among superpower leaders though. It may just be a 
recognition that every time you try to get rid of one dependency on China 
another one pops up.
