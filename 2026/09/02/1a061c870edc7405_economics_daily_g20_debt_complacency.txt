G20 Debt Complacency

One of the main discussion sessions at this week’s gathering 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-30/us-treasury-bars-reporters-from-several-outlets-from-g20-meeting?cmpid=BBD090226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8412>
 of finance and central bank chiefs from the world’s biggest developed and 
emerging markets focused on “sovereign debt.”

Opening that discussion, Bessent went over some well-worn G20 talking points 
about bringing public and private-sector creditors together to speed up debt 
restructurings for developing nations facing distress.

There was talk of increasing accuracy on lending data — in the context of 
China often being a giant lender butnot necessarily 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-01-20/yellen-swipes-at-russia-china-over-actions-she-says-hurt-africa?cmpid=BBD090226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8412>
 a transparent one. “A technical level study group” on debt management will be 
set up ahead of the G20 summit in December.

What wasn’t on the docket this week in Asheville, North Carolina: any sort of 
detailed pledges by G20 members to rein in their own borrowing. The group’s 
host, the US, is heading toward a fiscal deficit in excess of 6% of GDP for 
2026, at a time of full employment. It’s difficult to find an economist who 
predicts that gap will approachBessent’s 3% target 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-24/bessent-s-longshot-deficit-goal-undercut-by-war-and-tariff-news?cmpid=BBD090226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8412>
 by the time President Donald Trump steps down in January 2029.

Japan’s government — which has the largest debt load of any major nation at 
over 200% of GDP — has tossed aside its previous target of achieving a primary 
budget surplus. (The primary budget excludes debt-interest costs). As the next 
budgeting cycle gets going, at least one ministry is seeking anoutsized increase
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-31/japan-s-industry-ministry-seeks-unprecedented-49-billion-budget?cmpid=BBD090226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8412>
. Germany is anticipating an expansion in borrowing in 2027 thanks in part to 
weaker-than-expected tax revenue.
US Secretary of Treasury Scott Bessent, center left.Photographer: Allison 
Joyce/AFP/Getty Images
What almost all nations also face is a steady increase in the cost of 
servicing debt. The yield on a Bloomberg gauge of global sovereign bonds on 
Monday climbing to 3.72%, the highestsince mid-2008 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/australian-benchmark-bond-yield-jumps-to-level-last-seen-in-2011?cmpid=BBD090226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8412>
.

In that context, a G20 commitment for each nation to at least pledge to 
restrain future deficits might have helpedshape the narrative 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/why-government-bond-yields-are-rising-and-causing-alarm?cmpid=BBD090226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8412>
 among market participants. Bessent said last month a new “fiscal consolidation 
plan” would be out probably by the week of Aug. 24. Tuesday, he said it may be 
months away. But, it will be a “fulsome” package, he said. Budget watchersare 
dubious 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-24/bessent-s-murky-deficit-plan-faces-grim-prospects-in-congress?cmpid=BBD090226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8412>
.

Most of the G20 members are meantime ramping up defense spending — in large 
part to take on each other — adding to strains on spending.

Bessent’s hypothesis is the G20 can embrace growth to bring down deficit and 
debt ratios. The idea is to expand the denominator, which is nominal GDP. Japan 
has actually been doing that, as this newsletterhas noted 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-01-08/japan-s-debt-burden-tumbles-to-a-16-year-low-on-economic-upswing?cmpid=BBD090226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8412>
. But without credible commitments to restrain borrowing and temper inflation, 
the risk is that giant interest bills will keep inflating the numerators, as 
well.

Need-to-Know Research

Facing competition with China so intense it’s threatening a hollowing out of 
the likes of Germany’schemical industry 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-23/germany-s-troubled-chemical-makers-risk-disorderly-decline?cmpid=BBD090226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8412>
, and continuing tensions with the Trump administration, the European Union is 
likely to ramp up efforts to secure trade deals with a major third element in 
the global economy: Southeast Asia.

“For EU exporters seeking growth opportunities, ASEAN’s fast-growing markets 
could help offset tariff-induced trade pressures from the US. They could also 
serve to diversify supply chains in strategic sectors,” Christopher Graham and 
Edward Lee at Standard Chartered wrote in a note Tuesday. ASEAN is the EU’s 
third-largest trading partner outside Europe, after the US and China.

It’s been more than a decade now since the EU concluded free trade agreements 
with Vietnam and Singapore. “Indonesia is likely to join this list later this 
year,” the Standard Chartered economists wrote. They see abreakthrough with 
Thailand 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-10/thailand-pushes-eu-free-trade-deal-to-reduce-us-reliance?cmpid=BBD090226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260902&utm_content=8412>
 as possible by year-end. “Talks with Malaysia and the Philippines continue to 
make steady, albeit slower, progress.”
