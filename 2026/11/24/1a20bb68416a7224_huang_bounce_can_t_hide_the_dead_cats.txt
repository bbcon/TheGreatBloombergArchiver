Last week, Points of Return needed to address the moves to allow Jensen Huang 
to return as CEO of Nvidia Corp. For full disclosure,this was what I said then 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-11-18/nvidia-gets-a-breather-but-jensen-huang-shouldn-t?cmpid=BBD112426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11753>
:
To guard shareholders’ long-term interest, Nvidia cannot let Huang return. 
That would tell the world that his behavior was excusable in return for a 
short-term buck.
I’m inclined to stand by that judgment in the wake of the extraordinary news 
that he’s back with immediate effect, afterdirect intervention from President 
Donald Trump 
<https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/cnbc-transcript-president-donald-trump-speaks-with-cnbcs-eamon-javers-and-squawk-on-the-street-today.html>
. It did, however, mean quite a short-term buck. During Monday’s trading, 
Nvidia’s market cap rose by about $968 billion. That’s more than the current 
market cap of JPMorgan Chase & Co.:

We cannot know what the long-term impacts will be yet. But Monday’s startling 
stock market bounce is, for now, still only a bounce. The news that created 
almost a trillion dollars in market value at Nvidia did no more than arrest a 
free-falling plummet in the major US indexes. Both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq 
100 remain significantly below their peaks, and down for the month of November:

Looking closely, the news didn’t stop another gain for the dollar, with the 
DXY index gaining 0.49%. It would normally be expected to fall on days when 
risk had reduced. And Nvidia’s rebound failed to restore most of the main 
indexes even to where they were on Nov. 17, when Huang had already resigned. 
That includes even the SOX semiconductors index, in which Nvidia has a 
weighting of more than 10%:

The decision came as a shock, which boosted the scale of the rebound. 
Prediction markets had made Huang the single most likely candidate for the next 
job, butonly with odds of 18% <https://polymarket.com/event/next-ceo-of-nvidia>
. Luke Lango, a technology analyst who has generally been very bullish on the 
sector, said major problems were unresolved:
Nvidia is back to where it was essentially when they allowed him to resign. We 
haven’t regained any of the prior losses before Jensen got the boot — and same 
thing across the entire AI complex. There’s still massive technical damage. 
We’re still beneath all the major support levels on all these stocks and all 
these benchmarks.
Others said the move smacked of panic, with George Noble 
<https://georgenoble.substack.com/p/desperate-times-call-for-desperate?utm_source=post-email-title&publication_id=2899640&post_id=226248742&utm_campaign=email-post-title&isFreemail=true&r=1tnx7&triedRedirect=true&utm_medium=email>
, a long-time fund manager for Fidelity Investments, arguing: “Whena board 
rehires the CEO who resigned 12 days earlier 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-11-23/bringing-huang-back-as-ceo-confirms-nvidia-board-s-irrelevance?cmpid=BBD112426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11753>
, with the White House pushing for it, that tells you how desperate things 
really are.”

So the short-term gain is evident, but the long-term costs remain to be 
determined. They could be considerable. Tina Fordham of Fordham Global 
Foresight argued:
It’s hard to escape the conclusion that national security is a distant 
secondary consideration compared to propping up financial markets that are 
juiced by the AI trade. The Chinese Communist Party will hardly believe their 
luck. From a geopolitical as well as a governance perspective, whetherAlice 
Huang was formally a Chinese spy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/spy-chief-says-us-probing-alleged-spy-tied-to-former-nvidia-ceo?cmpid=BBD112426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11753>
 or merely an asset that fed sensitive information is a detail.
She added that the most important takeaway might be “to invest where Trump 
does, and this is the ultimate safe haven trade.”

Others agree that this could damage the US as a jurisdiction. Arjun Divecha, 
veteran emerging markets investor at GMO,argued 
<https://x.com/arjundivecha/status/2124930382936310267>:
I’ve always said that the difference between the US and EM long- term returns 
is the quality of institutions and the rule of law. Regardless of the merits of 
the underlying facts, today’s action undermines the US on both dimensions.
While the underlying issues of the AI trade remain unresolved, a new array of 
political risks now arises. The president gave assurances that the government 
was not “in possession” of evidence that Huang had committed crimes, but didn’t 
promise not to prosecute, while a number of investigations remain open. CEOs in 
such a position would normally have at the very least to take paid leave.David 
Garrity of Laidlaw Capital Markets 
<https://dmg03860.substack.com/p/the-wager-of-ai-nvidia-captain-restored?r=1uhpaw&utm_medium=ios>
 called the maneuver “a key-person override of governance, underwritten by 
government assurance rather than a completed investigation.” That creates 
numerous problems in the medium term:

 * Policy: Nvidia’s code of conduct names personal relationships as a conflict 
of interest, and boards have removed CEOs for less. Reinstating before the 
investigation finishes puts the decision ahead of the facts.
 * Security: A political assurance is not a completed counterintelligence 
review. Skepticism is bipartisan, from Rep. Don Bacon (R-Neb.) to Rep. Raja 
Krishnamoorthi (D-Ill.), who wants a public hearing.
 * Credibility: Two unanimous votes in opposite directions within two weeks 
weaken the board’s standing as an independent check.
 * Political exposure: Nvidia’s governance risk now moves with Washington: The 
assurance is only as durable as the administration that gave it and the 
oversight it faces.
The Republican defeat in the midterms also grows in importance — not just 
because Democrats will now control Congress and take any opportunity to 
embarrass the administration, but because the weakening of Trump’s power enables
Republican China hawks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-23/jensen-huang-s-return-to-nvidia-ramps-up-congressional-probes?cmpid=BBD112426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11753>
 in the Senate to come outwith fists flying 
<https://www.mediaite.com/politics/gop-senator-calls-out-trump-commerce-sec-howard-lutnick-over-nvidia-fiasco-needs-to-answer-some-hard-questions/>
. Huang is likely to be at the middle of an extended political controversy that 
drags on for months, and his long-term survival is not assured. It will get in 
the way of his ability to do his job.

Normally, a chief executive in his position would resign or accept a suspension
 
<https://qz.com/how-boards-handle-ceo-scandal#filing-a-form-8-k-and-other-disclosure-obligations>
. But Huang made himself undismissable, so Nvidia must now tolerate months 
going through a public wringer. It also raises issues ofmoral hazard 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2020-04-13/stock-markets-are-rising-on-lehman-style-faustian-bargain-k8xy83v3?cmpid=BBD112426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11753>
: Are Huang and other tech CEOs now so important to the US economy that they 
cannot be allowed to fail?

The issue is reminiscent of the “too big to fail” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/quicktake/big-fail?cmpid=BBD112426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11753>
 debate during the Global Financial Crisis, when the need to rescue some of the 
biggest banks revealed that they enjoyed an implicit government guarantee. That 
prompted demands for the government too regulate them far more tightly, as 
otherwise the banks had no incentive not to take risks.

Trump argued that “we must protect our geniuses,” but also declined under 
questioning from CNBC to give rules as to which tech CEOs would receive such 
protection. “Many will see this as a statement that the tech industry is 
supported by the full faith and credit of the US government,” said Jean Ergas 
of Tigress Financial Partners.

That could be taken as a license to take more risks by both the tech industry 
and those who invest in it. Huang’s reinstatement could yet prove very 
expensive for the US taxpayer.

Huang More Time

Copper’s recent slump 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TOZ2OVT96OSL?cmpid=BBD112426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11753>
 got a reprieve Monday as the Nvidia events renewed confidence in the 
artificial intelligence trade. The U-turn appeared to put a floor under the 
crucial component in data center construction, which closely tracks other 
measures of the AI buildout. But decline in metals prices is still a clear 
signal of a global liquidity squeeze.

The recent weakness has reflected AI’s problems, while expectations that 
Washington would impose tariffs — which supported prices — have quieted since 
the US midterms. Before Monday’s rally, copper had dropped by more than a fifth 
since its September all-time high:

It’s not clear that this is more than a respite. The run on metal prices has 
been spurred by more than the AI trade implosion. Equally crucial is a 
liquidity crunch, particularly in China, which is still the biggest consumer of 
copper:

The Bloomberg Economics China Credit Impulse Index, which measures the flow of 
new credit into the Chinese economy relative to its gross domestic product, is 
almost flat, breaking a pattern in which for years the government had 
periodically primed the pump when activity began to slow. That implies more 
problems ahead for copper (and for liquidity-sensitive investments around the 
world). Withprivate borrowing subdued 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/china-s-consumption-and-investment-wilt-as-factories-power-ahead?cmpid=BBD112426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11753>
, Beijing is accelerating bond issuance to achieve its “near 5% GDP” target, 
which further crowds out meaningful credit creation. This suggests that the 
liquidity squeeze has further room to run, and could have ramifications far 
beyond metals.

Well-documented weak Chinese domestic activity 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/china-s-economy-weakens-more-than-forecast-despite-stimulus-push?cmpid=BBD112426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11753>
 further weighs on metals’ prospects. The yuan’s sustained appreciation since 
late 2025, which makes dollar-denominated metals cheaper for Chinese buyers, 
hasn’t ignited demand, and there’s still no sign of the stimulus policymakers 
signaled in the heavily publicized 2024 policy “pivot”:

That economic gloom, Macquarie Insight’s Larry Hu argues, could continue 
because Beijing’s “Just Enough” rule is about meeting growth targets, but no 
more. Stronger exports could result in weaker domestic demand, as politicians 
avoid overshooting on growth:
When will policymakers do more to support domestic demand? Only when they can 
no longer rely on exports to drive growth. That consideration makes last week’s 
tech selloff particularly relevant to China’s economic outlook in 2027.
Gavekal Research’s Louis-Vincent Gave points to a breakdown in a relationship 
that has historically helped fuel recoveries in risk assets. Typically, bond 
prices rise as oil falls, creating room to move money out of bonds and into 
risk assets. Cheaper energy also boosts consumer incomes and business 
confidence. That sequence isn’t playing out this time; oil has fallen by about 
$20 a barrel, but yields haven’t dropped meaningfully:
As of right now, lower yields are not providing any relief in the unfolding 
liquidity squeeze. Also problematic is the fact that many institutions have, in 
recent years, replaced long-dated bonds with private equity, private credit, 
real estate or infrastructure funds; illiquid vehicles that cannot easily be 
sold to buy today’s dip in equities and commodities.
Unless there’s a credible shift in the demand and supply fundamentals, the 
Huang-inspired rally could prove short-lived. Chinese demand remains more 
crucial than ever to metal prices. Without that, commodity investors’ renewed 
optimism will only run ahead of the fundamentals, which won’t be enough to 
deliver higher prices:

—Richard Abbey
