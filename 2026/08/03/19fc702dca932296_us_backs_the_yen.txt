The US and Japan moved to defend the yen in their first joint action 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-02/katayama-to-say-japan-us-acted-together-on-yen-reuters-reports?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7284>
 on the tumbling currency in 15 years. On Monday morning, Japanese Finance 
Minister Satsuki Katayama confirmed their joint campaign — sending a clear 
signal Tokyo had a powerful partner in its bid to lift the currency froma 
four-decade low 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-japanese-yen-timeline/?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7284>
. South Korean officials alsoappeared 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-31/korea-cites-close-ties-with-us-japan-after-overnight-fx-surge?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7284>
 to be part of the coordination. The yen gained as much as 1.4% versus the 
dollar during morning trading in Tokyo, as that message was digested.

US President Donald Trump described Washington’s assistance as a sign of 
friendship. But the US had its own incentives to back Tokyo. As the largest 
foreign holder of US Treasuries, Japan could have been forced to sell some of 
those bonds to fund currency intervention, potentially pressuring the Treasury 
market. A stronger yen would also reduce concerns that Japanese exporters were 
benefiting from a persistently weak currency.

Speculation of further joint moves is now emerging.

Malaysia is considering allowing some exports of unprocessed rare earths

 in order to bolster its position in a supply chain feeding industries from 
carmaking to defense and consumer goods.

The Southeast Asian nation put a moratorium on exports of raw rare earths in 
2024 to stimulate more investment in domestic processing — a familiar strategy 
for resource-rich developing nations. But authorities are now assessing the 
feasibility of easing the curbs as competition for the minerals heats up, 
according to a senior government official.
