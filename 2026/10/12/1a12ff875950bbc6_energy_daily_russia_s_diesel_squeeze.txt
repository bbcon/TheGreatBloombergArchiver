Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, we 
look at the US president’s efforts to bring down diesel prices by boosting 
imports from Russia.

A diesel deal 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/trump-says-putin-agreed-to-release-diesel-into-global-market-mv1bqka7?cmpid=BBBXT101226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9978>
 between Vladimir Putin and Donald Trump won’t be enough to ease the global 
fuel market. Russia needs a lasting halt to energy strikes and weeks of 
refinery repairs before it can materially boost exports.

Following Friday’s phone call between the two leaders, Moscow pledged 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-10/russia-lifts-ban-on-diesel-exports-as-part-of-trump-putin-deal?cmpid=BBBXT101226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9978>
 to release 500,000 tons of diesel for export “at this stage,” without saying 
when they will be shipped or when additional shipments might be authorized.

Yet even if all the volumes reach buyers by the end of the month, they will 
amount to less than 180,000 barrels per day, a mere fraction of what the nation 
used to export.

Prior to the intense Ukrainian campaign targeting oil refineries — part of 
Kyiv’s response to Putin’s war and attacks on its own energy system — Russia 
sent an average 800,000 to 900,000 barrels of diesel-type fuel abroad on 
tankers per day, roughly 10% of global seaborne flows.

Now the nation simply cannot produce as much fuel as before, following 
near-dailyUkrainian drone attacks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/ukraine-says-it-attacked-fourth-russian-refinery-this-week?cmpid=BBBXT101226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9978>
.
Trump speaking to members of the media in Washington, DC, on Saturday.
Photographer: Ron Sachs/CNP/Bloomberg via Consolidated News Photos
Assessments of Russian refinery runs <bbg://news/stories/TM66BKVTTCZT> 
underscore the damage.Energy Aspects <bbg://news/stories/TM66BKVTTCZT> and 
Rystad Energy <bbg://news/stories/TMHBGOT9NJLV> see Russia’s oil-processing at 
around 3.5 million to 4 million barrels a day, around 30% below seasonal norms. 
With several major diesel-producing plants halted or operating at reduced 
rates, a significant output decline is inevitable.

Trump has promised that Ukraine’s attacks on Russian oil assets will stop 
thanks to anenergy ceasefire 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-11/trump-says-russia-and-ukraine-agreed-to-energy-ceasefire-mv3wxxro?cmpid=BBBXT101226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9978>
, which would also require Moscow to halt its strikes on Ukraine’s energy 
infrastructure.

So far, the Kremlin has nothing to say <bbg://news/stories/TMSH1XKIP3IF> on 
the truce, Putin’s spokesman Dmitry Peskov told Russian media. Since Trump’s 
statement on Sunday, Moscow has continued to strike Ukrainian energy 
infrastructure, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said, adding that Kyiv will
respond accordingly 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/zelenskyy-says-strikes-to-continue-if-putin-rejects-energy-truce?cmpid=BBBXT101226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9978>
 if Russia does not agree to the ceasefire.

Even if a truce materializes, restoring diesel production will require several 
weeks of refinery repairs if the pace of recent works is any guide.

Russia’s Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak said monthly diesel exports of 
3 million tons — near levels seen before Ukraine’s drone campaign — will only 
be possible after December.

That timetable could frustrate Trump’s plan to bring domestic diesel prices 
down before the midterm elections three weeks from now.
