Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Brian Eckhouse and Naureen S. Malik explore the reasons behind tight supply on 
America’s biggest power network.

When US power-grid operators issue warnings, they typically come in the summer 
or winter, when widespread use of air conditioners or heaters drives up 
electricity demand.

But such notices now appear to be turning into a year-round phenomenon.

At the beginning of last week, PJM Interconnection LLC, the nation’s largest 
grid operator, declared alevel one 
<https://insidelines.pjm.com/operations-update-extends-maximum-generation-alert-calls-on-demand-response/>
 emergency alert, which means there’s little or no excess capacity available to 
meet a spike in demand.

The company followed that up with another warning for Monday.

The fact that these missives came in mid-October is striking. And Dave Souder 
<https://usea.org/profile/david-souder>, PJM’s executive director of system 
operations, said he expects it to happen again.
Linemen at work. The AI boom, wild weather and maintenance are all putting 
power grids under pressure.Photographer: Joe Raedle/Getty Images North America
Part of the explanation for this shift is the extreme strain imposed on power 
grids across much of the country by the artificial intelligence boom.

After about two decades of essentially flat US electricity demand, the 
proliferation of new data centers means consumption is growing again — and 
power suppliers and grid operators arestruggling to keep up 
<https://dataviewer.pjm.com/dataviewer/pages/public/load.jsf>. It’s a big 
reason why energy bills have surged, a key voter concern ahead of next month’s 
midterm elections.

But there’s another explanation for why fall and spring, even with their more 
moderate temperatures, are no longer risk-free for network operators.

Power plant owners often schedule repairs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/us-grids-strain-under-heat-during-peak-repair-season?cmpid=BBBXT102026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261020&utm_content=10264>
 outside summer and winter, resulting in reduced availability. Yet demand from 
AI isn’t seasonal.

On top of all that, the weather has played its part this autumn. The US 
Mid-Atlantic and Midwest, where PJM operates, recently endured a strong
nor’easter storm 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-27/new-york-and-northeast-facing-another-day-of-wild-weather?cmpid=BBBXT102026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261020&utm_content=10264>
, while temperatures last week were significantly higher than normal.

Souder maintains that PJM’s system is reliable. But supplies are getting 
tighter, he said: “The margin for error is shrinking.”

—Brian Eckhouse and Naureen S. Malik, Bloomberg News
