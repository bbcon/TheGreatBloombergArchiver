I have a new story out from the forthcoming economics issue of Bloomberg 
Markets magazine and it hits on a lot of themes I’ve been thinking about for 
some time.

The Ballad of the American Tin Can, as my editors dub it in the print issue, 
is a story about tariffs, unfulfilled promises, unintended consequences and the 
places that get caught up in all of this. Bloomberg great David Gura, who is a
skilled fiddler <https://x.com/davidgura/status/1634533479365980160?lang=en>, 
emailed after he saw the title on a list somewhere and asked cheekily if there 
was an actual song to go with it. There isn’t yet. But David has agreed to get 
out his fiddle if there ever is. Perhaps we can get another Bloomberg scribe 
withsongwriting skills <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xpe8Z2jub9M> in Joe 
Weisenthal to help cobble something together and strum his guitar. (If he’s 
booked, maybe the Financial Times’s Soumaya Keynes, theco-author 
<https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/How-to-Win-a-Trade-War/Soumaya-Keynes/9781668221310>
 ofHow to Win a Trade War and an occasional trade balladeer 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0G-DeGsJRjE>, wants to have a try?)

You can and should read the full version of the story, which is today’s 
Bloomberg Big Take,here 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-26/how-trump-s-steel-tariffs-impact-america-s-tin-can-industry?cmpid=BBD072726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260727&utm_content=6967>
. I don’t have any actual lyrics (feel free to email me if you have thoughts). 
But here’s a run through the themes the five verses would have to hit in an 
actual ballad. The chorus, as it clumsily stands, probably goes something like 
this: “He’s a tin can man. Oh he is. He’s a tin can man. Tariffs mess with him. 
But nothing can stop him. He’s a tin can man.”

Verse 1: Here come the tariffs. This story all starts with Wilbur Ross going 
on TV in March 2018 and reaching for a Campbell’s soup can that he bought for 
$1.99 at a 7-11. Just-announced steel tariffs would do very little to affect 
the price, Ross declared, and the duties on steel would bring tens of thousands 
of jobs and lots of investment. Eight years on, though, things have turned out 
kind of different.

Verse 2: This is where you get into unfulfilled promises. On that day in March 
2018 there were a dozen mills in the US making the tinplate used in cans. Today 
there are just three. So that part of the investment story hasn’t worked out 
for the tin can world. Can makers rely even more on imported tinplate today 
than they used to. The reality is the steel industry is much more interested in 
making steel for cars than tinplate for cans. And the jobs? Well there are only 
1,300 more people working in American steel mills than there were in 2018.

Verse 3: Time for the unintended consequences. The price of canned fruits and 
vegetables is up almost 50% in the US since 2018 and the cost of steel cans has 
risen 80%. That makes it harder for domestic canneries and farmers to compete 
against imported filled cans, which aren’t subject to the steel tariffs. As 
Rich Hudgins, chief executive officer of the California Canning Peach 
Association, told me: “The steel tariffs just have kind of a counterproductive 
effect of compounding the cost advantage that our competition” in China and 
Greece already have.

Verse 4: We need a place. When Donald Trump was elected in 2016, we spent a 
lot of time thinking about the places that were “left behind” by globalization. 
Tariffs were all about bringing jobs back to those heartland towns. But that’s 
not necessarily what happened. In Weirton, West Virginia, the tin mill shut 
down in 2024 after owner Cleveland-Cliffs lost an anti-dumping case seeking 
even higher tariffs on imported tinplate. The reality is that decades of 
underinvestment and changing ownership meant the mill struggled to compete 
against imports. Now the town is moving on. “Steel is what got us here,” says 
Dean Harris, Weirton’s mayor and a 48-year veteran of the steel mill. “But it’s 
not our future.”

Verse 5: Time to find a conclusion for this tale. Let’s give the final word to 
Ross. He’s taken away a broader lesson about tin cans from what’s happened 
since he brandished his soup can on TV in 2018. “One thing it proves is that 
tariffs don’t necessarily solve every problem the world has,” Ross says now. 
“And that, I think, will always be true.”

Then I guess if this was a song we’d go back to the chorus and fade into the 
next track? There could be a whole album in this. “He’s a tin can man. Oh he 
is. He’s a tin can man…”
