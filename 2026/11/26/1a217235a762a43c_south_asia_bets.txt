South Asia’s capital markets are stretching their legs. In India, two Adani 
companies arepreparing to tap the public bond market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-25/stomach-churning-swings-rock-wall-street-as-ai-doubts-creep-in?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11900>
 for at least $220 million, sources said, potentially giving a lift to a corner 
of the debt market that remains tiny compared with private placements. Adani 
Enterprises has increasingly made a habit of going directly to investors (its 
January bond sale wassnapped up 
<https://www.adani.com/newsroom/media-releases/adani-enterprises-launches-its-3rd-public-issue-of-ncds-of-rs1000-crore>
 within an hour) as it broadens the pool of capital it can draw on.

Across the border, Pakistan is trying a different kind of market deepening. 
After stocks tripled in three years and retail participation surged, the 
Pakistan Stock Exchange ispreparing to introduce 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/china-backed-pakistan-exchange-prepares-to-launch-stock-options?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11900>
 monthly options on some single stocks by June, sources said. Borrowing 
expertise from its Chinese owners, and lessons from India, it is cautiously 
adding more sophisticated ways to trade. Taken together, the stories capture 
markets at different stages of the same evolution: More investors, more 
instruments and a widening appetite for capital (and risk).

Singapore Is ‘Very Disappointed’ by Trump’s Proposed Tariff Hike

Singapore said it disagrees with the US findings that the city-state’s 
policies contribute to excess manufacturing capacity and will urge Washington 
to reconsider proposed tariffs of up to 20% on its exports.
Read more 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/singapore-is-very-disappointed-by-trump-s-proposed-tariff-hike?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11900>
Washington is turning up the tariff pressure on India and China, with levies 
designed as much for leverage as punishment.. Analysts say the latest plan 
injects fresh uncertainty into India-US trade: Indian goods would face a 17% 
levy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-25/us-tariff-proposal-raises-stakes-for-india-to-clinch-trade-deal?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11900>
, rising to 25% if New Delhi fails to clinch a deal, with textiles, gems and 
jewelry and seafood among the most exposed. China, meanwhile,gets a 20% rate 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/trump-s-china-tariffs-raise-threat-of-new-test-for-trade-truce?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11900>
 that could double if its trade truce with Washington breaks down, in a threat 
hanging over Xi Jinping’s meeting with Donald Trump at next month’s G20. 
Beijing has signaled it can live with the lower rate, while Indian officials 
say their deal is nearly done. In both cases, Washington’s message is hard to 
miss: Play ball, or pay more.

Xpeng wants to put a humanoid robot in your life for roughly the price of a 
car. The Chinese EV maker’s IRON robot will be trained as a retail assistant and
go on sale in the second quarter 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/xpeng-says-its-humanoid-robot-will-cost-the-same-as-an-ev?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11900>
, priced around the median electric vehicle, Vice-Chairman Brian Gu told 
Bloomberg TV. The pivot comes as China’s bruising EV price war squeezes 
automakers at home, and protectionism complicates expansion abroad. Xpeng’s 
answer is to think well beyond cars: Robots, robotaxis and flying vehicles, 
with the hope that the next engine of growth may not need a driver at all.

Huawei Teases Next-Gen AI Memory Chip to Take On Samsung, Hynix

Huawei is preparing to release details of a new high-bandwidth memory chip 
that could potentially surpass current offerings from Samsung Electronics and 
SK Hynix, moving to launch Chinese production of a key AI component currently 
blocked by US sanctions.
Read more 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/huawei-teases-next-gen-ai-memory-chip-to-take-on-samsung-hynix?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11900>
The battle for control of India’s most storied conglomerate is heading to 
court. A majority of Tata Trusts trustees havebacked a lawsuit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/tata-trusts-back-lawsuit-against-tata-sons-chair-s-reappointment?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11900>
 challenging Natarajan Chandrasekaran’s reappointment as Tata Sons chairman, 
sources said, escalating a feud between the charitable trusts that own 66% of 
the holding company and the board they ostensibly control. At the heart of the 
fight is an even bigger question: Whether Tata Sons should be forced to list, 
as India’s central bank has repeatedly demanded. The Trusts fiercely oppose 
that prospect, and what began as a boardroom disagreement now risks becoming a 
prolonged legal battle over who really calls the shots at the house of Tata.

Japan is fighting to prop up the yen, but some of its biggest investors are 
pulling the other way. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/japan-life-insurers-complicate-yen-fight-with-record-low-hedging?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11900>
 The country’s largest life insurers have cut currency hedges on their vast 
overseas portfolios to the lowest level on record, reducing a source of demand 
for the yen even as authorities spend record sums supporting it. With US rates 
still well above Japan’s and hedging expensive, the insurers see little reason 
to protect against further yen weakness. As one strategist put it, the insurers 
have “given up on the yen.” Intervention can buy time, but it’s hard to 
engineer a lasting comeback when Japan’s own big money isn’t buying in.
