<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-13/inside-jensen-huang-s-final-hours-as-the-ceo-of-nvidia?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11162>
, is just one of scores of firms around the world operating data centers its 
customers use to run their artificial intelligence models.

These so-called neocloud providers have become part of a much bigger dynamic 
in the global economy: a massive increase in trade in services. Commerce has 
long been associated with goods — and appropriately so, given their dominance 
over centuries. But in the past couple of decades, services have been a larger 
source of growth.

From 2008 through 2024, when manufactured export volumes rose 53%, services 
volumes jumped 93%, economist Richard Baldwin wrote in a new National Bureau of 
Economic Researchpaper <http://www.nber.org/papers/w35975>. Looking just at 
digitally delivered services, which strips out tourism and other categories not 
provided via computers, those soared 137% over the same period, Baldwin 
calculates. “Globalization’s frontier is shifting from factories to offices,” 
he says.

The Megaspeed International office in Singapore. So-called neoclouds are part

These services range from back-office processing and software development to 
equity research and even online yoga instruction. While AI may end up replacing 
some jobs, such as customer-service call centers, others may open up. Frederic 
Neumann at HSBC Holdings Plc uses the example of AI-based translation that 
allows a German firm to have legal documents processed in India, which in the 
past wasn’t a cost-effective option.

This swelling globalization wave coming for the services industry threatens to 
add new challenges for developed nations (as if theydidn’t have enough already 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-23/germany-s-troubled-chemical-makers-risk-disorderly-decline?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11162>
). Neumann, HSBC’s chief Asia economist, says the fear is over the implications 
from “the uncertainty and the upheaval that breeds in middle class workers who 
had felt pretty secure in their services jobs.”

He says the politics worry him more than the economics.

For many decades, the focus of concern about job losses tied to trade has been 
on the manufacturing side. And “China shock 2.0 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-30/china-export-shock-why-it-s-hard-for-economic-rivals-to-fight-back?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11162>
” has been a narrative, in particular for Europe, for years. Services have 
received little attention. That’s in part because, even though the rate of 
growth in commerce in services has been faster than goods, it’s come from a 
relatively low base.

What is giant is the overall output. Collectively, services made up $21.2 
trillion worth of GDP in 2024, compared with global manufacturing at $17.7 
trillion, according to Baldwin, a professor at the IMD Business School in 
Lausanne, Switzerland, who specializes in globalization. He highlights that 
measuring services isn’t straightforward. In fact, in the old days of 
international trade, it was referred to as “invisibles 
<https://berl.co.nz/economic-insights/services-largely-invisible-part-trade>.” 
(Baldwin excludes real estate from his figures.)

As international communications links 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/tata-commits-450-million-to-ai-superhighway-linking-hubs?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11162>
 deepen and large language models get even more sophisticated, it’s logical to 
think more services will flow to lower-cost locations such asIndia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/modi-s-free-trade-push-could-lead-to-140-billion-gdp-gain-for-india?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11162>
, Bangladesh and the Philippines. Indeed, India’s services exports lately have 
appeared to “shoot up in a straight line,” Neumann wrote in a recent note with 
HSBC colleague Abanti Bhaumik.

The export-to-output ratio of what Baldwin calls the “global office” is “still 
far below” that of the “global factory,” he says. “My conjecture is that the 
future of trade is services,” Baldwin concluded in his NBER paper.

As the scale of trade in services increases, that may become alarming to 
policymakers in developed democracies. To the extent that some white-collar 
jobs are at risk from being replaced by AI, the outsourcing of services 
threatens a double-whammy, cautions HSBC’s Neumann.

“That is a huge, huge concern, not only not just economically but also 
politically,” he said in an interview this week in Hong Kong. When middle class 
workers sense their jobs are no longer safe, they may “turn to populism, 
extreme right or left, and to some extent that’s happening” already, he notes.

Governments may well go on to craft a policy response. Tariffs may not work so 
easily for services trade, but if officials in developed nations sense that 
swathes of theirmiddle class are at risk 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/why-the-us-economy-looks-strong-but-feels-bad-to-americans?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11162>
, officials may get creative, Neumann says.

“Data sovereignty can become a form of new protectionism,” he says. An 
American company that aims to set up a service center in India, for example, 
could be told it’s not allowed to transfer data essential for its US 
operations. “You can say, well, there’s privacy concerns, there’s national 
security concerns,” Neumann says.

“Could we see government interference in the free-flow of data around the 
world? In some industries, it’s certainly possible,” he says. “Governments 
might be quite creative in trying to stop this, because it will have such 
enormoussocial consequences 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/france-braces-for-marches-and-strikes-amid-stalled-budget?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261114&utm_content=11162>
.”
