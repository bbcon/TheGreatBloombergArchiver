Elon Musk’s SpaceX is in talks with banks and investors to borrow as much as 
$40 billion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/spacex-seeking-to-raise-40-billion-to-buy-nvidia-chips-ft-says?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9819>
 to buy Nvidia chips, people familiar said, in what could become one of the 
biggest debt financings of the AI boom. Pacific Investment Management is one of 
those looking at the deal, the sources said. The early-stage plan would combine 
about $10 billion of bank loans with $30 billion of investment-grade debt, the 
Financial Times reported earlier, underscoring just how much borrowed money is 
now fueling the race for computing power. Musk isalready building 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-25/elon-musk-aims-to-double-colossus-2-s-nvidia-chips-by-year-end?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9819>
 Nvidia-powered clusters on an extraordinary scale, and SpaceX’s plans show how 
the AI arms race is also rapidly becoming a credit story.

Meanwhile, Ray Dalio thinks the AI boom is approaching its danger zone. The 
Bridgewater founder called AI a“classic bubble” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/ray-dalio-warns-ai-bubble-is-approaching-burst-point?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9819>
 and warned that rising interest rates could help bring it to a breaking point. 
His concern is increasingly relevant as tech companies pour hundreds of 
billions of dollars into AI infrastructure, much of it financed with debt, just 
as bond yields are pushing borrowing costs sharply higher. Stocks are still 
setting records, but Dalio argues the reckoning tends to begin when soaring 
paper wealth eventually has to be converted into cash.

And Taiwan is pulling ahead 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/taiwan-dethrones-korea-atop-global-markets-as-ai-trade-widens?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9819>
 of South Korea in Asia’s AI stock-market race. Taiwan’s Taiex has surged 72% 
this year to become the best-performing benchmark among more than 90 indexes 
tracked by Bloomberg, overtaking Korea’s Kospi asinvestors bet 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-samsung-tsmc-ai-rally/?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9819>
 on its broader exposure to the AI boom. Taiwan offers everything from chip 
design and manufacturing to packaging, networking and servers, while Korea’s 
rally remainsheavily dependent 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-11/peak-memory-fears-put-focus-on-samsung-sk-hynix-cash-returns?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9819>
 on Samsung and SK Hynix, and the memory-chip cycle. The shift is showing up in 
investor positioning too: 35% of fund managers surveyed by Bank of America 
picked Taiwan as the Asian market best placed to benefit from AI’s next phase, 
compared with just 5% for Korea.

India’s economy is running hot enough for the central bank to hit the brakes. 
The Reserve Bank of India raised interest rates for the first time in nearly 
four years, lifting its benchmark rate by 25 basis points to 5.5% and signaling 
that more hikes could follow as inflation broadens and the rupee slides. Yet 
the economy is proving remarkably resilient: the RBI also sharply upgraded its 
growth forecast to 7.1% from 6.7%, even as it raised its inflation outlook. The 
World Bank had made the same growth call a day before the RBI move, lifting its 
own forecast to 7.1% on the strength of Indian industry, services and domestic 
demand. That resilience gives the RBI something many central banks would envy: 
room to fight inflation without yet sacrificing much growth.
