Water Stress

By Patrick Sisson <https://muckrack.com/patrick-sisson>

This July, in the middle of the night during one of Southern California’s 2026 
heat waves, a 36-inch utility pipe gave way beneath West Hollywood, sending 17 
million gallons of water coursing past the nightclubs of the famed Sunset Strip 
and opening up a massive sinkhole that flooded nearby homes and garages.

The size of the deluge made national headlines 
<https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/16/us/west-hollywood-water-main-break.html>, 
but it was hardly unprecedented: There wereat least five 
<https://lamag.com/news/five-infrastructure-failures-in-one-week-flood-l-a-with-damage-heres-the-timeline/>
 other significant water system leaks around Los Angeles that week, a spate of 
failures that brought scrutiny of thesystem’s advanced age 
<https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-07-25/l-a-s-game-of-triage-as-aging-water-system-hits-breaking-point>
. The Sunset water main that ruptured had been installed in 1916.

Meanwhile, municipal water systems in at least a dozen US states 
<https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/05/us/politics/water-supply-warnings.html>, 
including Minnesota, Michigan and Georgia, reported a very different threat 
that month. They were targeted in cyberattacks — believed to be the work of 
Iranian hackers — that disrupted service in several cities, triggering 
boil-water advisories and fears of more malicious actions. In 2021,hackers 
gained control of a water system in Oldsmar, Florida 
<https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-55989843>, and tried to raise the 
level of sodium hydroxide — an ingredient in drain cleaner used to control 
water acidity — in a mass poisoning attempt.
A broken water main in West Hollywood shut down a portion of Sunset Boulevard 
in July.Photographer: Myung J. Chun/Los Angeles Times via Getty Images
The roughly 50,000 community water systems across the US typically operate 
underground and out of sight, drawing attention only when they aren’t working. 
But these vital utilities find themselves increasingly in the public eye as 
they race to upgrade infrastructure and avoid catastrophic failures exacerbated 
by more severe weather and other factors.

“Climate problems make every other problem in the water sector worse,” said 
Scott Berry, senior advisor on policy and external affairs for the US Water 
Alliance. “Droughts, storms, flooding, heat waves, all of those things 
generally manifest as types of water stress. It affects different places in 
different ways, but affects everybody.”

To keep pace with climate risks and make needed upgrades, Berry estimates that 
US water systems would need an additional $140 billion a year for the next 20 
years. The US Environmental Protection Agency has a more modest, though still 
significant, estimated price tag: $625 billion over the same period. Researcher 
Zia Lyle of the University of North Carolina estimates that 67 million American 
utility customers <https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-025-03044-z> are 
served by water utilities with high climate risk, and that more than a quarter 
of water systems face high financial and climate vulnerability.

No Guidance

By Zahra Hirji 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVypnhGtqak/zahra-hirji?cmpid=BBD091126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8804>

The Environmental Protection Agency has quietly removed multiple online 
resources for companies on how to measure the carbon footprint of their supply 
chains, called Scope 3 emissions.

As the Trump administration aggressively rolls back rules and mandates to 
reduce pollution, 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2025-trump-policies-climate-science/?cmpid=BBD091126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8804>
 it is also taking downclimate change-related data 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-08-05/researchers-will-rebuild-billion-dollar-disaster-tool-trump-killed?cmpid=BBD091126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8804>
,tools 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-05-07/six-environmental-mapping-tools-the-white-house-doesn-t-want-you-to-see?cmpid=BBD091126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8804>
 andother information 
<https://www.nytimes.com/2025/12/09/climate/epa-website-climate-change-causes.html>
 from federal websites that experts say businesses, researchers and others have 
long relied on to better understand current and future environmental risks.

“Instead of holding polluters accountable, the EPA under Trump has been 
focused on reversing and deleting efforts aimed at tackling greenhouse gas 
emissions,” Senator Adam Schiff, a California Democrat, told Bloomberg News. He 
said he’s introduced a bill that would require the agency to publish even more 
guidance than it had previously for companies to track their climate pollution.

When asked about the website removals, EPA spokesperson Carolyn Holran said: 
“Corporate Climate Leadership is not a statutory obligation.”

Since Trump took office, the agency web page titled “Scope 3 Inventory 
Guidance”has been taken down 
<https://www.epa.gov/climateleadership/scope-3-inventory-guidance> and so have 
multiple other online references to Scope 3 emissions, according to a Bloomberg 
News review of the current site and archived versions, including theofficial 
government snapshot from January 19, 2025, 
<https://19january2025snapshot.epa.gov/climateleadership/scope-3-inventory-guidance/index.html>
 predating President Donald Trump’s second term. A 2021 EPA webinar titled “
Working with Suppliers to Reduce Scope 3 Emissions 
<https://19january2025snapshot.epa.gov/climateleadership/working-suppliers-reduce-scope-3-emissions/index.html>
” was also removed from alanding page for climate risk-related webinars and 
events <https://www.epa.gov/climateleadership/webinars-and-events>.
The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) headquarters in Washington.
Photographer: Al Drago/Bloomberg
This fits into a government-wide push to abandon the mandatory collection of 
corporate climate information. Last fall, the EPA proposed to effectivelyend 
its Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-12/trump-s-epa-to-stop-tracking-emissions-from-biggest-us-polluters?cmpid=BBD091126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8804>
 that collects annual emissions from big industrial polluters, such as coal 
plants, oil refiners and chemical plants. In June, the US Securities and 
Exchange Commissionproposed to rescind a rule 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-29/sec-moves-to-scrap-biden-era-rule-on-climate-risk-disclosures?cmpid=BBD091126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8804>
 requiring public companies to report both greenhouse gas emissions and 
climate-related business risks.
