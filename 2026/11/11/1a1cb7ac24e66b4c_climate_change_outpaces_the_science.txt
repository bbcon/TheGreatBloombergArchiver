Defying prediction efforts

By Eric Roston 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQdgGkZhizQ/eric-roston?cmpid=BBD111126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11276>

 andJulia Janicki 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXDBW8DczHc/julia-janicki?cmpid=BBD111126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11276>

The summer heat that killed tens of thousands 
<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44360-026-00220-z> in Europe, a 
record-breaking wildfire season in Indonesia, the protracted drought gripping 
the US Southwest: Many punishing consequences of climate change are arriving 
sooner, hitting harder and, in some cases, diverging entirely from what 
scientists have modeled.

The unpredictability about how global warming affects regional weather is a 
vexing and increasingly important challenge for governments, banks and insurers 
trying to accurately prepare for and respond to disasters that can cost 
hundreds of billions of dollars in losses each year.

That’s the backdrop for the COP31 United Nations climate conference, drawing 
dozens of world leaders among nearly 95,000 participants to Antalya, Turkey, 
over the next two weeks. A record-breaking El Niño, expected to make this year 
and the next hotter than any in modern history, is amplifying the effects of 
climate change, supercharging droughts, torrential rain, heat waves and 
wildfires against an already elevated temperature baseline.


Firefighting operations on scrubland in the Ogan Ilir region of South Sumatra 
province, Indonesia, on Oct. 10.Photographer: Videographer: Muhammad 
Fajri/Anadolu /Getty Images
“These extremes are happening faster than we were expecting,” said Emma 
Howard-Boyd <https://www.lse.ac.uk/granthaminstitute/profile/emma-howard-boyd/>
, the chair of the UK’s National Heat Risk Commission, an independent adviser 
to the government on how the country can be more resilient to high 
temperatures. As El Niño strengthens to unprecedented levels, “We will probably 
be shocked at the scale of events that we experience and at the general lack of 
preparedness.”

To better understand the unexpected 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-extreme-heat-climate-change-data/?cmpid=BBD111126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11276>
 weather, Bloomberg Green asked the researchers behind areview 
<https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adt8035> of more than two dozen 
climate-related trends to update and share their data analysis through 2025.

While the models performed well against real-world observations in most cases, 
in others the scientists found striking differences that have implications for 
efforts to guard against the impacts shaking the planet.


As greenhouse-gas emissions from burning fossil fuels continue to rise, 
damaging weather extremes are expected to become more frequent and severe. 
Scientists have accurately forecast rising global temperatures from those 
emissions, but what the models can’t predict with precision is when or where 
they will strike.

“Impacts are arriving sooner, hitting harder and spreading further than many 
anticipated,” UN Secretary-General António Guterres told leaders at the General 
Assembly in September.
Destroyed houses along the Trishuli River in Devighat, Nepal, following a 
flash flood triggered by a Himalayan glacial collapse on 29 August.
Photographer: Daniel Ceng/Anadolu/Getty Images
COP31 will highlight the growing urgency to bring down emissions and adapt to 
climate change. While there is no single worldwide estimate for adaptation 
needs, developing countries are expected to require about $305 billion a year 
by 2035, according to the latest UNreport 
<https://www.unep.org/en/publications/report/adaptation-gap-report-2026>. 
Simulations can help determine where to invest limited capital in critical 
infrastructure.

Climate scientists recently began running the seventh 
<https://www.carbonbrief.org/explainer-the-cmip7-emissions-scenarios-and-how-they-explore-future-climate-change>
 generation of models, with analyses expected to roll out with more frequency 
in the next year. AI advances may also bringsurprises 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-13/ai-scientists-help-human-ones-answer-urgent-climate-questions?cmpid=BBD111126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11276>
 to researchers probing these questions. Some wildcards will remain, including 
uncertainty around how clouds or aerosols behave, said Tiffany Shaw, a 
professor of atmospheric science at the University of Chicago and co-lead 
author of the comprehensive review of models and observations. But they will 
offer higher geographical resolution, translating global trends into impacts 
for specific regions.

“It’s incontrovertible that the models are getting better,” Shaw said. “Nobody 
really knows what’s good enough.”

A scolding from Lula

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD111126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11276>
 andJohn Ainger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD111126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11276>

Brazil’s President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva urged fellow world leaders to 
move more aggressively to combat climate change, warning countries have become 
complacent on global warming in the face of other threats.

“We started talking more about weapons and tariffs than the survival of future 
generations,” Lula, as he’s universally known, said Wednesday at the United 
Nations-convened COP31 climate negotiations in Antalya, Turkey. “With the wars 
and inflation knocking on our doors today, it is more difficult for us to deal 
with this climate catastrophe of tomorrow.”

Lula addressed dozens of other leaders at the conference following his 
reelection to a fourth term — a come-from-behind victory against climate 
skeptic Flávio Bolsonaro. That, along with Democratic gains in the US midterm 
elections, has injected some optimism into the two weeks of talks that began 
Nov. 9.
Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, Brazil’s president, speaks at the opening ceremony 
of the leaders’ summit at COP31.Photographer: Kerem Uzel/Bloomberg
Even so, Lula’s call for renewed momentum, coming more than a decade after the 
adoption of the landmark Paris Agreement, follows a bleak United Nations 
assessment that the world can no longer achieve the accord’s key commitment to
prevent global warming exceeding 1.5C 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/un-warns-major-emissions-cuts-needed-to-limit-warming-overshoot?cmpid=BBD111126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11276>
.

“The truth is that we have the power to determine how high, how long and how 
devastating that overshoot will be,” UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres told 
the meeting, making a similar appeal for accelerated action. “We have the power 
to make sure the overshoot is a temporary breach.”

The tens of thousands of diplomats, delegates and activists gathered in 
Antalya for the COP31 talks are confronting a major challenge: The world’s work 
to combat global warming has waned even as its impacts are accelerating.

A devastating glacier collapse in Nepal and tens of thousands of deaths tied 
to lethal heat waves and wildfires across Europe illustrate the mounting 
consequences of planetary warming. World leaders said they should also be a 
call to action.

“Either we look at the difficulties and we complain, or we embrace hope and 
take action,” Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan told the meeting. “With 
every minute that we fail to achieve the targets set out in the Paris 
Agreement, the risks and costs we face continue to grow.”

Bagpipes, Ottoman marches and the chants of ecstatic Brazilian delegates 
following their newly-elected president – the ultimate COP superstar – Luiz 
Inácio Lula da Silva. That’s just a sampling of the music on display at this 
year’s climate summit.

Delegates feeling exhausted after multi-hour commutes and long queues were 
energized on Wednesday by thecatchy tune 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3AXa9fl3qGw> playing on repeat in plenary 
rooms: “Bring down the heat, make it drop. One world, one call, let’s COP.”

The Ottoman military band Mehter perform for attendees at COP31.Photographer: 
Kerem Uzel/Bloomberg
 * Martial: Ahead of world leaders’ arrival at the COP31 summit in Antalya, 
the thundering drums and braying pipes of the Ottoman Mehter military band 
filled the venue, evoking martial music that once accompanied the empire’s 
advance into Europe and later inspired composers including Haydn, Mozart and 
Beethoven.
 * Chill out: Screens in the main plenary hall encouraged delegates to speak 
respectfully and inclusively with each other – a kind of coaching for how to 
bring down the rhetorical heat at a conference dedicated to lowering the 
world’s temperature. “I would like to ask you respectfully to hold your 
emotions in check,” was one suggestion.
 * Batucada: Dozens of Brazilian indigenous leaders made their presence heard 
with traditional percussion instruments at the forest event attended by 
officials including Lula. But it was the word of warning from the Republic of 
Congo’s forest economy minister Rosalie Matondo that dominated: “The list of 
promises and declarations is long, but concrete actions, reliable and 
accessible funding, and the transfer of technologies remains insufficient,” she 
said.
 * South-South: At the China pavilion, the country projected its image as a 
champion of the developing world when the country’s highest-level official made 
his debut appearance at a high-level forum on South-South cooperation. Vice 
President Han Zheng spotlighted China’s green supply chains and its MAZU 
AI-powered early warning system, which President Xi Jinping pledged this summer 
to install in 30 countries by 2030. The audience spilled outside the pavilion 
including officials from Ethiopia, Nigeria, Cameros and other African nations 
who were given pride of place.
 * Ode to joy: “I” not “We” — European Commission President Ursula von der 
Leyen left it ambiguous if the 27-member bloc would sign up to the Turkey-led 
initiative to boost the global electrification rate to 35% by 2035. At the 
leader’s summit, Von der Leyen used the “I” pronoun when supporting the pledge, 
a core ambition of COP31, rather than the more inclusive “we.” It signals talks 
are still ongoing at an EU level.
