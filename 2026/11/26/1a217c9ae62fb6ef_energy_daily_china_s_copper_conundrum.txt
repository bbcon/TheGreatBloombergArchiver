Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Chief Correspondent Alfred Cang explores how China’s underlying economics could 
affect global copper demand.

The focus of the copper market is shifting from the US to China, but the Asian 
giant’s demand for the metal may be weaker than it appears.

For most of this year, trading was driven by expectations that President 
Donald Trump would impose import tariffs on copper. That threat, which never 
materialized, drew huge volumes of the metal into the US and eroded supplies 
elsewhere.

That left China — by far the world’s largest consumer — unusually short 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-17/shanghai-exchange-intervenes-after-historic-copper-squeeze?cmpid=BBBXT112626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11903>
 of the key industrial metal. Inventories on the Shanghai Futures Exchange 
dropped recently to near historic lows, with traders paying a hefty premium to 
secure physical supplies.

But scrape away the near-term bullishness and a different picture 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-25/china-s-booming-exports-obscure-domestic-economic-strains?cmpid=BBBXT112626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11903>
 emerges of China’s ability to absorb more copper.

The nation’s growth weakened 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/china-s-economy-weakens-more-than-forecast-despite-stimulus-push?cmpid=BBBXT112626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11903>
 across the board in October. Theproperty sector 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-10-20/foreign-investors-in-china-real-estate-face-losses-and-distressed-sales?cmpid=BBBXT112626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11903>
 remains in a slump. Investment is shrinking and households are reluctant to 
spend. Factories produce cars, machinery and electronics in huge volumes that 
outstrip domestic demand.

Even the power grid — a rare bright spot for metals consumption this year — 
can only use so much copper.

Exports have helped 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-19/why-china-s-export-machine-seems-unstoppable?cmpid=BBBXT112626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11903>
 to sustain China’s appetite for raw materials. Trading giant Trafigura 
estimates that 60% of global copper demand growth last year came from the metal 
embedded in Chinese goods shipped to emerging markets.

And while plowing capital into artificial intelligence, robotics and solar 
panels has helped to maintain growth, it’s an approach that leaves China 
reliant on other countries’ trade policies.

These trends should give pause to every miner or producer that’s relying on 
China’s seemingly limitless appetite for raw materials.
Robots transfer copper plates at a smart electrolysis workshop in Yingtan 
City, China.Photographer: Xinhua News Agency/Getty Images
For much of the past year, traders have focused on how much copper the US can 
suck in, and how much new demand AI and power grids can create. Now, a selloff 
in technology stocks hasturned copper 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/from-squeezes-to-meltdown-a-week-of-mayhem-rocks-metals-markets?cmpid=BBBXT112626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11903>
 from one of US macro funds’ favorite bullish trades into something to bet 
against.

Traders expect copper to soon flow back to China from the US — potentially 
arriving as early as next month. The market’s initial verdict was bearish, with 
nearby spreads in Shanghai sharply lower in the past week.

If the world’s biggest consumer no longer needs as much copper as expected, a 
new concern looms: Where will that metal end up?

—Alfred Cang, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

El Niño-driven weather reinforced the role of coal and gas as India’s power 
backstop this year, according to BloombergNEF. Hotter-than-usual temperatures 
increased electricity demand for cooling, while below-average monsoon rainfall 
reduced hydroelectric generation from 2025 levels. Alongside higher coal-fired 
generation, gas-fired output rose 60% in September from a year earlier.
