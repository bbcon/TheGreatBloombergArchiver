Europe: Where the Action Was

It’s an old maxim for news editors that the biggest story is often where 
everyone was gathered a year ago. Once crowds have lost interest, the most 
exciting things can start happening. That maxim also applies for investors. 
Once the crowds leave, the chances improve that you can buy into good 
investments for less than they’re worth.

On that basis, it’s worth exploring Europe again, particularly Germany. Early 
last year, the continent was rocked by dramatic shifts as leaders grasped that 
they could no longer rely on the US under President Donald Trump to take their 
side. In particular, Vice President JD Vance’s speech to the Munich Security 
Conferenceshocked his listeners <https://bloom.bg/4xFDEr0>. 

When Germany chose a new chancellor, Friedrich Merz, following knife-edge 
elections a few days later, he launched a fiscal reform that arrived as an 
economic earthquake <https://bloom.bg/4htK1sz>. Constitutional limits on 
borrowing introduced after the Global Financial Crisis were repealed, and Merz 
instead announced a massive newrearmament campaign <https://bloom.bg/4gnKyuZ>, 
something that had been taboo in Germany for decades.

The market response at the time was extraordinary. Over the first three months 
of last year, MSCI’s Germany index outperformed the US by almost 40%. Since 
then, it has been one long, drawn-out letdown, with the outbreak of war in Iran 
puncturing the last resurgence by German stocks. Merz’s coalition is now in 
serious trouble, <https://bloom.bg/3RXQly5> while his approval rating has  
plummeted to 14% <https://bloom.bg/4zhXWZa>: 

But now, there might just be another chance to get in on the excitement. The 
star of the rearmament fervor was Rheinmetall AG, the biggest German weapons 
contractor. Its performance in the wake of the twin US and German political 
shocks dwarfed even the greatest beneficiaries of the artificial intelligence 
boom. It peaked earlier this year and endured a long slide, which may now be 
coming to an end:

The macroeconomic backdrop at last looks supportive. European data for the 
first few months of the Iran conflict were very disappointing, as measured by 
Citi’s European macroeconomic surprise index. But the last few months have seen 
an impressive rebound, and the index is now at its highest in more than three 
years:

Meanwhile, there is the issue of valuation. The price/book ratio of Europe’s 
Stoxx-600 used to be only slightly lower than that of the S&P 500. That has 
steadily changed since 2010 as European stocks were weighed down by the 
sovereign debt crisis, and then watched US tech mega caps dominate the AI 
revolution. The valuation gap has never been wider:

This is an unfair comparison, as it fails to take account that information 
technology stocks account for 8.5% of the Stoxx, and 38% of the S&P. Of course 
the American index is more expensive. But there is also a valuation gap between 
the US and European industrial sectors, much more comparable, that has widened 
over the last few years:

There’s a decent chance that European companies can close this gap. Inflation 
seems to be less of a problem than in the US (pending the July CPI data, due 
shortly after you receive this), and further hikes from the European Central 
Bank are now seen as less likely than from the Federal Reserve. All else equal, 
that should help Europe’s manufacturers, which also stand to benefit from 
rising German spending:

Then there is earnings season. Last year’s tariff war undermined confidence in 
European companies’ ability to generate profits. Over the last few months, 
aided by the oil sector, projected profits for the year have rebounded 
impressively:

With the AI trade hitting air pockets of late, Gavekal Research’s Cedric 
Gemehl points out that European earnings — not at all dependent on tech — have 
greater appeal:

The backbone of this earnings recovery is Europe’s macroeconomic shift towards 
domestic reflation and investment. This is most visible in the earnings of 
financials, industrials and utilities, three sectors which are at the heart of 
the shift, and which have room to catch up after deflation suppressed earnings 
in the 10 years to 2022.

None of this corrects for some deep-seated problems. The list of ongoing bear 
points that might deter investors is formidable:

 * Europe never developed a deep  tech sector <https://bloom.bg/4gplofs>, and 
it’s probably too late to try. 
 * As with much of the world,  demographics <https://bloom.bg/4zj9rj8> are 
unremittingly negative, putting a lid on growth — Germany and France have birth 
rates of 1.6 and 1.4 children per woman respectively, below the 2.1 replacement 
rate. 
 * The euro zone, with unified monetary policy but fragmented fiscal policy, is
flawed by design <https://bloom.bg/4geJclc>. 
 * The continent is not self-sufficient in oil and still uncomfortably 
dependent on energy from Russia and the Middle East. 
 * Politics are in a perilous place, with traditional elites deeply out of 
favor and a range of aggressive populist alternatives of right and left moving 
toward power.

 European stocks are cheaper than the US for valid reasons. But while nobody 
has been paying attention, the continent has made steps in the right direction, 
and geopolitical uncertainty hits it more than the US. If that uncertainty is 
resolved, Europe stands to benefit disproportionately. But that’s a big if...

Russian Roulette 

The  Russia-Ukraine <https://bloom.bg/4abQoNi> war is now in its fifth year. 
It has been almost totally eclipsed in market attention by the five-month-old 
Iran conflict — but it’s emerging from a stalemate amid intensifying aerial 
attacks from both sides. Investors need to pay attention. This chart, sourced 
from Armed Conflict Location and Event Data <https://bloom.bg/4hY7diI> (ACLED), 
shows both frequency and magnitude of attacks rapidly surging:
Source: BCA Research 
For all the human costs of the war in Eastern Europe, these attacks are 
overshadowed for investors by the intractable drama over the Strait of Hormuz, 
the critical transit point for roughly a fifth of global crude oil exports. 
Tracking news stories across the Bloomberg Terminal that mention Iran or 
Ukraine offers a useful proxy in comparing the attention both conflicts are 
getting:

Interest in the Ukraine conflict died down after six months, and has trended 
lower ever since. But Kyiv’s onslaught on critical oil infrastructure in 
Russia, largely using drones, is beginning to weigh on output. That raises the 
possibility of the war progressing beyond the current stalemate. Overseas crude 
shipments <https://bloom.bg/4gr8fm3> appeared to be breaking higher earlier 
this year, but have now dropped to the lowest since May. Key ports in the 
Baltic and Black seas are operating below capacity:

Following the recent strike on Russia’s largest oil refinery complex, in 
Tatarstan <https://bloom.bg/4hY7eDi>, with a capacity of more than 300,000 
barrels per day, it’s plausible to question how much pain Ukraine can inflict 
and its spillover effects on the broader energy markets. Alex Kokcharov of 
Bloomberg Economics argues that even these painful attacks are merely setbacks 
that the Kremlin is willing to accommodate:

Ukraine would need to strike much more on Russian oil export terminals on both 
the Baltic and Black seas. They have done some strikes, but they have been more 
sporadic compared to the strikes on oil refineries, which appear to be 
systematic, and they don’t appear to have the same impact on Russia’s ability 
to export crude oil – which, instead of going to refineries that have been 
damaged – is now being exported to external markets.

Moscow’s response could yet create trouble that would be impossible to ignore. 
BCA Research’s Matt Gertken notes that Russia is redoubling its attacks on 
Ukraine and has made a series of provocations against NATO. It wouldn’t be 
surprising if the Kremlin were to resort to nuclear brinkmanship, perhaps on 
the greatest scale since the Cold War, over the next 12 months:

A new fear factor is likely to hit financial markets in the coming weeks and 
months, despite the perceived de-escalation of geopolitical risk due to US-Iran 
diplomacy. It may not dissipate until the US has staged a show of solidarity 
along with NATO to deter Russia from the path of conventional war, which may 
take half a year or more.

The 2022 energy crisis <https://bloom.bg/4hY7eTO> that broke out in Europe 
after the initial invasion shows that any such escalation comes at a high cost. 
Natural gas prices are rising, with the Dutch TTF Natural Gas one-month futures 
contract, Europe’s benchmark, near its highest since the hyperbolic surge four 
years ago:

Whether it rises further depends on fundamentals. Bloomberg Economics 
estimates that Russia accounted for more than half of EU gas imports in 2021. 
But theEU policy <https://bloom.bg/4xzes58> to free itself from Moscow’s 
stranglehold has brought that share down to about 15%, with the bloc on track 
to phase out Russian imports entirely next year. (Some such resolution 
presumably lies ahead for the Strait of Hormuz.)

The latest jump in gas prices has probably not been driven by Ukraine, whose 
attacks so far have targeted oil refineries rather than gas pipelines or 
Russia’s Arctic LNG terminals, which are well beyond its reach. The Strait of 
Hormuz remains the more potent driver.

Even so, Bloomberg Intelligence’s Patricio Alvarez notes that prices may not 
fully reflect the risk that the US and Iran cannot agree to reopen the Strait, 
through which roughly 20% of global LNG trade normally passes:

Europe’s vulnerability is amplified by low inventories, with our analysis 
suggesting storage could enter winter about 20% below its five-year norm. TTF 
is almost twice its prewar level of €30/megawatt hour (MWh), but prolonged 
Hormuz disruption and stronger Asian LNG demand could push prices toward 
€70-€90/MWh in the fourth quarter.

Without a durable resolution to the Middle East conflict, the EU’s attempt to 
phase out Russian gas imports could drive greater volatility in prices. A 
raging Russia-Ukraine war would make matters worse.

—Richard Abbey
