Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
energy reporter Dan Murtaugh looks at the challenges the US faces in trying to 
rejuvenate its solar industry.

Chinese solar companies will go to great lengths to reach the US market: 
20,000 miles in at least one case.

As detailed in this story 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-14/china-s-20-000-mile-solar-trade-route-that-battled-us-tariffs?cmpid=BBBXT081726_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7788>
, Chinese firms sent polysilicon wafers made on the mainland to Kenya and 
Nigeria, where they were wired into solar cells. Those were then shipped to 
Indonesia where they were pieced together into modules and marketed to the US 
as being of African origin, thus avoiding tariffs on Chinese and Indonesian 
solar imports.

The White House’s frustration with manufacturers’ ability to stay a step ahead 
of US trade laws finally boiled over earlier this month, when President Donald 
Trumpannounced a plan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/trump-signed-order-on-tariffs-trade-actions-on-polysilicon?cmpid=BBBXT081726_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7788>
 to target solar imports regardless of where they come from.

Equipment made outside the US will be subject to minimum prices all along the 
supply chain, from pure polysilicon to wafers to cells to finished modules. 
There will also be an additional tariff.
Solar cells at the Hanwha QCells manufacturing plant in White, Georgia.
While that may be enough to put a stop to the catch-me-if-you-can game played 
by foreign suppliers, it remains to be seen whether it will be sufficient to 
help rebuild US solar manufacturing.

Trump’s new rules 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/trump-signed-order-on-tariffs-trade-actions-on-polysilicon?cmpid=BBBXT081726_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7788>
 do include carrots for domestic producers along with sticks. Companies that 
commit to building new factories can keep importing equipment without the 
penalties.

But the president is also partially responsible for one of the biggest 
deterrents to investment in a US supply chain. American energy policy has 
whipsawed over the past decade, with the current administration favoring fossil 
fuels, making it difficult for manufacturers to gauge future demand.

Even if US firms do build solar plants, their products will almost certainly 
be more expensive and less advanced than those the rest of the world is 
receiving from Chinese companies.

In a world that desperately needs more power for growing fleets of electric 
vehicles and a surge in AI data centers, it’s a risky bet. US solar 
manufacturing needs to take such a giant leap for it to pay off that it will 
make 20,000 miles look like a short hop.

—Dan Murtaugh, Bloomberg News
