Russia’s Supreme Court made a decision this week that underlines the scale of 
the challenge for Europe in the showdown with Vladimir Putin over Ukraine.

The court barred the only political party 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-10/russia-bars-anti-war-yabloko-party-from-parliamentary-elections?cmpid=BBD081426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7697>
 campaigning on an openly anti-war platform from September’s parliamentary 
elections.

The ban was predictable and of little practical consequence. Yabloko (meaning 
Apple) was never going to win and took less than 2% support at the last two 
pre-war elections, though there were signs its popularity was growing this time.

But it shows that the Kremlin won’t permit even marginal dissent from the 
message that Russians fully support Putin and the war.
Supporters of Russia’s liberal Yabloko party outside the Supreme Court in 
Moscow on Monday.Photographer: Igor Ivanko/AFP/Getty Images
Because, to misquote John Lennon, all Putin’s saying is give war a chance. 
It’s been his consistent position since he ordered the full-scale invasion on 
Feb. 24, 2022, and he continues to reject any ceasefire in Ukraine.

Europe has spent 4 1/2 years applying sanctions in the expectation that Putin 
will eventually exhaust Russia’s military and economic reserves before 
accepting the inevitable and striking a deal.

The strategy’s had mixed results. Russia continues to recruit tens of 
thousands into the army each month andits economy hasn’t collapsed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-12/russian-economy-poised-to-resume-growth-despite-drone-strikes?cmpid=BBD081426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7697>
 even as it’s straining underpressure from Ukrainian strikes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/russian-diesel-flows-are-lowest-in-years-as-drones-hit-refiners?cmpid=BBD081426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7697>
 on oil refineries and logistics warehouses.

Meanwhile, Ukraine’s air-defense situation is so dire that it’s no longer 
reporting 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-11/ukraine-stops-reporting-russia-missile-data-as-defenses-struggle?cmpid=BBD081426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7697>
 Russian missile attacks, and US stockpiles of interceptors are said to be 
dangerously depleted.

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy also faces his own political, military and 
economic pressures 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/zelenskyy-is-losing-trust-in-his-central-banker-over-imf-ties?cmpid=BBD081426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7697>
.

Even at the peak of summer’s heat, attention is turning to winter. The fear is 
Russia will repeat its relentless bombing of energy infrastructure that plunged 
Kyiv and other cities tothe brink of despair 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-01-16/putin-s-attacks-on-ukraine-s-energy-grid-bring-fresh-misery-to-freezing-kyiv?cmpid=BBD081426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7697>
, seeking to compel Ukraine’s capitulation.

Europe appears intent on continuing the waiting game. But there’s no sign it 
will force a different Putin to emerge.

For European leaders intent on helping Ukraine continue to resist his 
invasion, that poses the question: What next?— Anthony Halpin 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASAcLGhGNAk/anthony-halpin?cmpid=BBD081426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7697>
