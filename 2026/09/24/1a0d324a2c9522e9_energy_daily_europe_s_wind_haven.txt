Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Will Mathis looks at how the turbulence on global energy markets is 
providing shelter to Europe’s wind turbine industry.

With so much turmoil in global energy flows — a potential US diesel export ban 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/wright-warns-industry-of-diesel-export-cuts-as-debate-heats-up?cmpid=BBBXT092426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260924&utm_content=9271>
, the collapse in Middle Easternliquefied natural gas 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-24/qatar-boosts-lng-traffic-through-hormuz-as-global-shortage-bites?cmpid=BBBXT092426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260924&utm_content=9271>
 exports — security of supply has never been higher on Europe’s agenda.

For the continent’s wind turbine manufacturers, this is cause for relief. The 
industry has been working for years to fend off efforts by Chinese competitors 
to make inroads into one of the world’s biggest markets for renewable power.

The entrance of cheaper machines from China once seemed like an inevitability, 
but the renewed political focus on sovereignty and security are deepening the 
moat for European incumbents.

“There are sectors in Europe now, and I think globally, that are actually 
being much more restrictive: it’s defense, it’s energy, and it’s telecom data,” 
said Henrik Andersen, chief executive officer of Vestas Wind Systems A/S, which 
pioneered wind turbine technology from its home base in Denmark.

For years, Chinese companies used their massive manufacturing scale to get 
into Europe. In some areas they’ve won — the vast majority of the world’s solar 
panels and batteries are made in China.

But in wind they’ve seen so little success that they’re considering 
partnerships 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/chinese-wind-turbine-makers-mull-partners-to-break-into-europe?cmpid=BBBXT092426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260924&utm_content=9271>
 with local companies. Europe’s turbines are supplied almost exclusively by 
local companies like Vestas, plus the American GE Vernova Inc.

In Hamburg this week at the wind industry’s biggest European trade fair, the 
CEO of China’s Ming Yang Smart Energy Group Ltd. in Europe, Horatio Evers, said 
he didn’t expect to sell any offshore turbines in the region this year and 
couldn’t commit to booking an order in 2027.

Europe has learned from its experience with solar panels, so for wind power the
bloc is determined 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-05/chinese-turbine-supplier-targets-new-danish-offshore-wind-farms?cmpid=BBBXT092426_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260924&utm_content=9271>
 not to let its lead slip away.

“We always welcome competition as long as we think it’s fair,” said Ilya 
Hartmann, chief financial officer at German turbine maker Nordex SE. The 
Chinese “want to have dominance in all energy technology, and that sometimes 
might lead to conditions that are not always equal for everyone.”

So even if turbines made in China could help lower the cost of wind power, 
particularly offshore, so far Europe isn’t buying.

—Will Mathis, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Global road fuel demand is set to peak in 2029 at over 48 million barrels a 
day, according to BloombergNEF. Mainland China is already in structural 
decline, while Europe and the US move from near-term plateaus into contraction. 
In contrast, demand in India, Brazil and other emerging markets continues 
growing into the 2030s.
