Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Julian Hast looks at what’s fueling a new wave of consolidation among 
US natural gas producers.

The US natural gas industry is evolving fast.

At the start of 2024, the top 10 producers included Chesapeake Energy Corp., 
Southwestern Energy Co., Coterra Energy Inc. and Range Resources Corp. Thanks 
to a wave of mergers, none of those names will exist by next year.

The latest came earlier this week, when EQT Corp. announced it had agreed to 
acquire 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/eqt-to-buy-range-resources-in-an-all-stock-transaction?cmpid=BBBXT103026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10779>
 Range in an all-stock deal for about $12.8 billion, regaining its title as the 
largest US producer.

Two decades into the US shale boom, the industry remains relatively 
fragmented, offering scope for further consolidation. Even after bulking up 
with Range, EQT’s total gas production of more than 9 billion cubic feet 
equivalent per day will still account for less than a 10th of the country’s 
total output.

It’s not just simple economies of scale behind the dealmaking. Other factors 
include reduced fears aboutantitrust scrutiny 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-12-02/eqt-gets-antitrust-request-over-5-2-billion-appalachia-deal?cmpid=BBBXT103026_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10779>
 under the Trump administration, rising demand from the reshoring of US 
manufacturing and further opportunities to displace coal-fired power.

But two forces stand out for gas drillers: a wave of new liquefied natural gas 
export terminals along the Gulf Coast and new power plants serving AI data 
centers.

By signing long-term contracts with these large consumers, shale drillers are 
locking in steadier revenue rather than relying on the vagaries of the spot 
market or selling via an intermediary marketing company. Competition for such 
contracts is fierce, and it helps to be able to show a deep inventory of shale 
reserves and the ability to ship gas to where it needs to go.

EQT’s purchase of Range is a case in point. Both companies already operate 
alongside each other in Appalachia, but their marriage doesn’t just combine gas 
wells and shale inventory. The merged company will also integrate Range’s 
pipeline transportation contracts with EQT’s portfolio and trading 
infrastructure, giving it heft and scale.

After Range is gone, myriad small operators will remain across the nation’s 
shale basins. But the pull toward larger operators is getting stronger.

“The value of being integrated, I think, has been significantly 
underestimated,” said EQT’s Chief Executive OfficerToby Rice 
<bbg://people/profile/18495401>.

—Julian Hast, Bloomberg News
