Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Chief Correspondent Alfred Cang explores how Chinese producers of key materials 
from metals to polysilicon are weathering lower prices and adding to wider 
deflationary pressures.

China’s oversupplied commodity markets are ignoring textbook economics — and 
prolonging the country’s struggle withweak inflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-09/china-s-inflation-cools-as-oil-shock-of-iran-war-starts-to-ease?cmpid=BBBXT081226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7637>
.

Prices for everything from solar materials 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-20/china-s-still-building-wind-and-solar-despite-threat-of-waste?cmpid=BBBXT081226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7637>
 to construction steel have sunk toward, and in some cases below, the cost of 
production. The standard response would be to close factories, causing supply 
to shrink and prices to rebound.

But China’s producers are playing by different rules.

While the property boom might be over, and infrastructure can no longer absorb 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-07/key-china-steel-price-hits-near-decade-low-on-construction-slump?cmpid=BBBXT081226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7637>
 seemingly endless volumes of steel and cement, the supply of many commodities 
remains stubbornly strong.

Hedging is one reason. Chinese producers have become sophisticated users of 
futures markets, selling forward their output when prices rally and locking in 
margins. In many cases, spot prices later fall below cash costs while factories 
keep producing against hedges made months before.

Provincial governments, with little appetite to eliminate capacity, also play 
a role. Closing an inefficient plant might make sense on paper, but local 
officials — worried about jobs, taxes, bank loans and social cohesion — often 
prefer an unprofitable enterprise to a shuttered factory.
Workers at a polysilicon plant in Xuzhou, Jiangsu province.Source: Bloomberg
Ever resourceful, metals producers are finding new income streams to keep 
plants ticking along. Forcopper smelters 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-21/chinese-copper-output-hits-record-on-tailwind-from-sulfuric-acid?cmpid=BBBXT081226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7637>
, higher chemical prices — thanks largely to the Iran war — have made 
byproducts such as sulfuric acid a meaningful source of revenue.

The aluminum supply chain offers another example. Low alumina prices may hurt 
producers of the feedstock, but they’re boosting margins for processors. One 
industry’s glut has become a cost advantage for the next.

And production costs continue to fall. Struggling manufacturers tend to 
squeeze their suppliers, who cut the price of raw materials to defend market 
share. The floor keeps shifting downward.

Therein lies the paradox of China’s commodity slump. Low prices are supposed 
to cure oversupply. Instead, hedges postpone losses and authorities discourage 
factory closures. Falling input costs lower the threshold for survival, and 
cheap commodities subsidize production downstream.

Everyone is waiting for somebody else to capitulate. Until they do, China’s 
commodity producers may keep delivering what the wider economy doesn’t need: 
more deflationary pressure.

—Alfred Cang, Bloomberg News

Global oil markets are undergoing a more severe supply squeeze 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-12/iea-sees-wider-oil-supply-deficit-despite-hit-to-demand-from-war?cmpid=BBBXT081226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7637>

The world faces a shortfall of 1.8 million barrels a day this quarter — more 
than double earlier projections — as “renewed hostilities and maritime 
disruptions” derail a production recovery in the Middle East, according to the 
IEA’s monthly report. For 2026 as a whole, the deficit will likely be the 
widest in five years.
