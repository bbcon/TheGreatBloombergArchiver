New Tools, Old Problem

When governments impose new taxes, it’s usually because they want to collect 
more revenue. But sometimes there’s another motive, too: influencing behavior. 
So-called sin taxes on cigarettes, alcohol or gambling are the classic example.

In the case of China’s exploration of a potential new charge on interest 
income from government debt — an idea floated recently by the country’s central 
bank, as my colleaguesreported here 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-11/china-gauges-tax-on-bond-interest-with-yields-near-record-low?cmpid=BBD111226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11284>
 — it’s possible there’s an element of behavior-shaping. Beijing might want its 
bond yields to kick a bad habit.

After Bloomberg broke the news the People’s Bank of China had sounded out 
investors on the tax idea, market-watchers immediately began trying to figure 
out how much higher yields might go in response. They’re near rock-bottom now. 
The rate on 10-year notes hitan all-time low 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-06/china-s-10-year-yield-hits-record-intraday-low-on-ample-cash?cmpid=BBD111226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11284>
 of 1.57% early this week.

That’s seen as a problem in Beijing — albeit the opposite of the one 
confronting most developed-world governments right now — because it could 
entrench pessimism about the economy’s prospects.

“Any move to tax interest income,” Bloomberg Intelligence wrote, “would likely 
signal discomfort with declining yields.” And that also suggests the PBOC isn’t 
wild about lowering short-term interest rates to spur China’s moribund domestic 
economic growth, according to the BI team.

Indeed, China’s central bank itself said in a report Wednesday that borrowing 
costsare already low 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-12/china-s-loan-interest-rates-are-already-low-central-bank-says?cmpid=BBD111226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11284>
.

All of which suggests that the PBOC won’t be trying out its new policy-setting 
tool. Officials just recently had switched its benchmark rate to an overnight 
one rather than a seven-day one — something that brings it in line with 
practice at global peers including the Federal Reserve. But theshiny new toolkit
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-30/pboc-reveals-overnight-rate-at-1-25-before-policy-anchor-shift?cmpid=BBD111226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11284>
 has become available just when it’s of limited use.

The problem for the PBOC is the kind you get in near-deflationary conditions: 
even if the central bank brings rates down, households and businesses don’t 
really want to borrow.

What could really boost the economy — and take yields higher along with it — 
is large-scale fiscal stimulus. But with powerful export gains helping keep 
China’s overallgrowth target 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-19/china-s-growth-rebounds-to-4-5-puts-annual-target-within-reach?cmpid=BBD111226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11284>
 in reach for 2026, there’s little sign of that coming.

Need-to-Know Research

Paul Krugman, who later went on to win the Nobel Prize in Economics, in 1994 
stoked controversy with his assessment in anarticle 
<https://cooperative-individualism.org/krugman-paul_the-myth-of-asia's-miracle-1994-dec.pdf>
 — “The Myth of the Asian Miracle” — that Asian economies’ superb growth rates 
had been due to “perspiration rather than inspiration.”

He detailed how that growth reflected deploying more capital and people to 
producing stuff rather than figuring out how to make stuff more efficiently. 
Now, JPMorgan Chase economist Joseph Lupton says there’s something similar 
behind the US productivity boom of recent years. The huge capital spending 
surge underway is having an impact onlabor productivity 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/us-labor-productivity-increases-at-fastest-pace-in-a-year?cmpid=BBD111226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11284>
 “akin to the Asian ‘tigers’ of the early 1990s,” he wrote in a note Wednesday.

What’s not yet happening is people using artificial intelligence to make 
things more efficiently. The data suggest the sort of total-factor productivity 
efficiency jump a-la the 1990s “has yet to arrive for the AI boom,” Lupton 
wrote. “Like most technological advances, the benefits could take longer to 
arrive thanconsensus anticipates 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/neuberger-sees-productivity-golden-age-boosting-bonds-in-2027?cmpid=BBD111226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11284>
.”
