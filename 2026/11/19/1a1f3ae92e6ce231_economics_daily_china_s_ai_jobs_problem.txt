China’s AI Problem

China’s labor market has been in rough shape since the nation’s property 
market begancontracting 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/china-s-property-crisis-from-evergrande-collapse-to-beijing-s-latest-measures?cmpid=BBD111926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11564>
 half a decade ago — wiping out what had been the most important impetus to 
domestic growth.

Now there are signs that the diffusion of artificial intelligence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-19/imf-s-chief-is-warning-ai-correction-has-little-bit-more-to-go?cmpid=BBD111926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11564>
 is making a bad situation worse, Bloomberg reports in a deep-dive analysishere 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-china-ai-jobs-slump/?cmpid=BBD111926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11564>
.

Hiring for occupations that are more exposed to AI fell about 10 percentage 
points more than less-exposed positions, the study of nearly 10 million online 
job postings found. It echoes early academic research that points to a net loss 
of new employment due to AI that hit early-career workers the hardest.

The blow is already visible on the ground. At top tech companies, workers 
suffered rounds of quiet layoffs this year that affected as many as 30% of 
staff in some departments.

Companies are combining several jobs including programmers and designers into 
one position — allowing fewer employees to do more. In the boomingmicro-drama 
industry 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-08-12/microdramas-take-hold-on-us-smartphones-after-rise-in-china?cmpid=BBD111926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11564>
, human actors and production crews are being eliminated by AI-generated 
content.

Assessing the impact of AI on workers is challenging in China, where little 
official data are available and companies avoid linking any job cuts to the 
technology. It’s a politically sensitive topic that policymakers refuse to 
divulge their views on, even in close-door meetings.

Behind the public stonewalling, however, officials are concerned and 
scrambling to track the impact of AI on jobs. And for workers, the employment 
shock may only be starting.

“It feels like seeing a tsunami coming from far away,” said Li Jia, an 
economics professor at Singapore Management University who studies the impact 
of AI on jobs. “You don’t know how hard it will hit by the time it reaches your 
doorstep, but just seeing it in the distance is frightening enough.”

Need-to-Know Research

In the global competition to tap new AI technology, the US and China tend to 
get all the attention. But don’t discount a significant role forGermany 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-12/infineon-to-open-german-chip-fab-as-part-of-eu-sovereignty-push?cmpid=BBD111926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11564>
, ING economists Franziska Biehl and Carsten Brzeski wrote in a note this week.

“The AI race isn’t only about developing models; it’s also about having enough 
people who know what to do with them,” theduo wrote 
<https://think.ing.com/articles/germanys-hidden-advantage-in-the-ai-race/?utm_campaign=November-17_germanys-hidden-advantage-in-the-ai-race&utm_medium=email&utm_source=emailing_article&M_BT=2537034784>
. “The winners might be those able to adopt, integrate and put AI applications 
to productive use quickly. And once we turn from traditional innovation to 
artificial intelligence, Germany suddenly looks much more competitive.”

More than 5% of employees work in jobs related to information and 
communications technology with roughly 6% of university graduates completing 
their studies in an ICT-related field, according to ING. About a quarter of 
German businesses already uses AI, and Germany is among the leading locations 
for AI-related patents, they said.

The nation’s comparative strength may life in applying large AI models across 
its industrial base and providing the infrastructure to do so, Biehl and 
Brzeski wrote. “Investment plans are moving in that direction too.”
