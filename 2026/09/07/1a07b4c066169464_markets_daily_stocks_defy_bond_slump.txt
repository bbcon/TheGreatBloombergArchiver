Risk assets defy bond slump

The global bond selloff is repricing the cost of money — without setting off 
the usual scramble 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/wall-street-risk-complex-defies-rate-threat-after-jobs-blowout?cmpid=090726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8560>
 out of risk assets.

Friday supplied the latest test of that resilience. A stronger-than-expected 
US jobs report delivered another blow to Treasuries as traders stepped up bets 
that the Fed will start raising interest rates at its meeting on Sept. 16. The 
dollar rose anew and the S&P 500 closed lower.

What’s remarkable is how little of the disruption has escaped the bond market. 
Credit premiums remain subdued and downside protection across risky assets is 
still relatively cheap. Even the strain in market plumbing has been 
concentrated: JPMorgan finds sharply deteriorating liquidity in Treasuries but 
little comparable stress in stock-index futures or corporate-bond ETFs.

“Financial conditions remain easy and credit spreads remain remarkably tight,” 
said Collin Martin at the Schwab Center for Financial Research. “Companies 
don’t appear to be too fazed by the current level of corporate borrowing costs 
when theirearnings

 are growing by more than 20% on a year-on-year basis.” —Isabelle Lee

Europe’s inflation risk

In that global bond selloff, no region is feeling the pressure more than 
Europe, wherespiraling natural gas prices and growing political risks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-07/bond-market-gilts-btps-oats-hit-by-fears-of-energy-shock-political-risk?cmpid=090726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8560>
 are accelerating the rise in yields.

French, Italian and UK longer-term borrowing costs have risen the most in the 
G7 over the past month, with yields hitting multi-year highs.

Investors are wary of another energy shock at a time of huge pressure on 
public finances. Gas prices have gained more than 120% since the start of the 
Iran war, reaching a three-year high last week. That’s fueling fears that the 
ECB will have to keep lifting interest rates.

“Europe’s energy dependence is back in focus,” said economist Ludovic Subran 
at Allianz. “Higher inflation expectations shifted anticipated central bank 
policy rates up – a major driver for the increase in longer-dated bond yields.” 
—Alice Gledhill 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUFfTWYF4c4/alice-gledhill?cmpid=090726_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260907&utm_content=8560>
