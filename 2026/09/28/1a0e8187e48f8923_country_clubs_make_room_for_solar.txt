Greener golf courses

By Kai Wilson 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AYaZlJCIZdI/kai-wilson?cmpid=BBD092826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9374>

The perfectly manicured fairways at Minohara Country Club wind through the 
forested hills of Gunma Prefecture about two hours from downtown Tokyo. In a 
few years, they’ll disappear under row-upon-row of solar panels.

In one of the world’s most densely populated and mountainous countries, 
developers are running out of sites big enough to host major solar projects. 
Golf courses, which multiplied in Japan during the boom years of the 1980s, are 
some of the last tracts of open land available.
The Fukushima Solar Farm was developed by EDP on a 60-hectare golf course at 
the outskirts of the prefecture.Source: EDP S.A.
“No one wants to do mega solar projects anymore. I really don’t think there’s 
any space,” said Daisuke Toya, representative director ofKamisato Kensetsu Ltd. 
<https://kamiken.info/>, a construction and real estate company that develops 
solar facilities and bought the 27-hole course in 2025. “The most we could do 
is buy up the golf course to use the land.”

The result is that a relic of Japan’s economic bubble is playing an unlikely 
role in the country’s energy transition. For the past decade, defunct golf 
courses have been vital to solar growth. About 9% of Japan’s utility-scale 
solar capacity is on former golf courses, according to an analysis of 
BloombergNEF data that includes arrays with capacity of 0.9 megawatts or 
greater.

Some of the country’s biggest projects — known as ‘mega solar,’ as projects 
are larger than a megawatt — have been built on former fairways, including 
Japan’s largest in Okayama prefecture in western Honshu.

Japan embraced solar power after the 2011 Fukushima nuclear disaster resulted 
in the closure of its 54 nuclear reactors, which used to supply around a third 
of its electricity. The government introduced generous subsidies — known as a 
feed-in tariff — that guaranteed the purchase of renewable power at a price 
that was economical for developers.

After an initial boom in installations that peaked in 2015, the buildout 
slowed and Japan gradually lowered the benefits of the feed-in tariff. This 
deceleration is posing a problem for the government, which is targeting solar 
supplying as much as 29% of electricity by 2040 — nearly triple the current 
proportion — and wants to get to net zero a decade later.

As well as the dwindling subsidies, mega solar projects have faced increasing 
opposition from communities concerned about deforestation, landslides and 
changes to landscapes. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has also been vocal in her 
opposition to such developments.

The biggest issue facing developers is just finding a place to build, 
according toAndrew Chapman 
<https://hyoka.ofc.kyushu-u.ac.jp/html/100025407_en.html>, an associate 
professor at Kyushu University, who estimates that using all the land 
technically suitable for solar and wind could produce 14 times Japan’s annual 
electricity generation. The country has the space, but that isn’t translating 
into actual construction, he said.

Investors prepare for El Niño

By Ishika Mookerjee 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUJMYcOXODs/ishika-mookerjee?cmpid=BBD092826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9374>

T. Rowe Price 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/TROW:US?cmpid=BBD092826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9374>
 has started relying on custom-made models to protect its $165 billion 
portfolio of emerging-market debt and equities from what promises to be an 
unusually destructive El Niño.
The water reservoir at the Guatape hydroelectric dam in Colombia, on 
Wednesday, Aug. 12, 2026.Photographer: Esteban Vanegas/Bloomberg
Aaron Gifford, associate director of research for global sovereigns at T. 
Rowe, says planning began when the last El Niño was forming. Two years ago, 
researchers at his alma mater, Johns Hopkins University, started developing the 
suite of climate and weather analytics that T. Rowe now uses alongside its own 
econometric analyses.

Everything was set in motion “the minute I saw news come up of another El 
Niño,” Baltimore-based Gifford said. “We started realizing we could really 
benefit from much more rigor on the academic side.”

The climate phenomenon associated with warming across the equatorial Pacific 
Ocean is already upending weather patterns across the globe. Since itstarted 
forming in May <https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/wmo-prepare-el-nino>, El Niño 
has hit maize and bean crops in Honduras and El Salvador, while in Asia uneven 
rainfall is hurting rice and palm-oil production. Analysts atGoldman Sachs 
Group Inc. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/GS:US?cmpid=BBD092826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9374>
 are among thosewarning 
<https://apnews.com/article/asia-el-nino-drought-rice-palm-oil-31c88df9ce8f67bcbfe3ea7f39431d57>
 of the broader implications for global food prices.

In response, investors including Man Group Plc 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/1720703D:LN?cmpid=BBD092826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9374>
, the world’s largest publicly traded hedge fund firm, are trying to map out 
possible contagion effects. But investing around El Niño also requires insight 
into how policymakers are likely to react.

A case in point is T. Rowe’s strategy on Colombian sovereign bonds. Gifford 
notes that the country is “highly impacted by El Niño drought conditions” 
because of its reliance on hydroelectricity and agricultural output, all of 
which impacts inflation, he said.

For now, though, the central bank in Bogota 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-07/colombia-inflation-jumps-more-than-forecast-to-hit-two-year-high?cmpid=BBD092826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9374>
 has “stopped short of the amount of interest rate hikes that we anticipated, 
or at least our analysts anticipated,” Gifford said. “And now we get to ask: 
Are they making a policy mistake?”
