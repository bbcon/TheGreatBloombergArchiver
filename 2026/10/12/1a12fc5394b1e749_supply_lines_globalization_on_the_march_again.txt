The global economy and all the forces 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/imf-chief-alarmed-at-slow-moving-tsunami-of-ai-for-jobs-taxes?cmpid=BBD101226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9927>
 reshaping it will be front and center at two big gatherings this week. So if 
you’re either attending or simply digesting from afar, here’s a handy 
recommendation for a contrarian conversation starter: Globalization is on the 
march again.

Whether you’re tuned into the Bloomberg New Economy Forum 
<https://www.bloombergneweconomy.com/2026-new-delhi-forum-program/> in New 
Delhi or the IMF-World Bankannual meetings 
<https://www.imf.org/en/meetings/2026/annual/schedule> in Bangkok, that’s a 
line likely to raise eyebrows. But, well, it also happens to be true.

In an essay 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/trump-brexit-and-tariffs-haven-t-stopped-globalization?cmpid=BBD101226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9927>
 that ran this past weekend, I make the case that a decade after the populist 
revolution in the Anglo world (the one that yielded Brexit and Donald Trump’s 
first election and lots of obituaries for globalization) we live in a world 
that’s more integrated than it was in 2016. “Globalization is not merely alive. 
It remains on the march, whether we like it or not,” the essay concludes.

But a very good piece of statistical evidence for that also came out of the 
World Trade Organization last week in itslatest outlook 
<https://www.wto.org/english/res_e/booksp_e/gtos1026_e.pdf>.

Economists at the WTO now expect the global trade in goods by volume to grow 
by 3.9% this year and 4.1% in 2027. That is far faster than expectations for 
global GDP, which the WTO expects to grow by 2.6% this year and 2.9% in 2027.

One trait of globalization for a significant stretch leading up to the 2008 
global financial crisis was that trade grew faster than world GDP.

AI-Driven Growth

That hasn’t happened very much since then. But if the WTO is right 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-08/wto-lifts-goods-trade-outlook-as-ai-demand-outweighs-war-strains?cmpid=BBD101226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9927>
 about this year and next, then that will be three straight years. Because in 
2025 the volume of goods traded grew 4.2% and GDP expanded by 3%.

Like many things, the reason for this 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/ai-drives-global-goods-trade-facing-trump-tariffs-war-headwinds?cmpid=BBD101226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9927>
 is artificial intelligence and the vast amounts of capital being invested in 
data centers and other physical infrastructure that AI needs. According to WTO 
economists the AI boom was responsible for nearly half the year-on-year 
increase in the value of goods traded in the first half of this year.

The reality is that even Trump is doubling down on globalization when he bets 
on AI as a growth engine for the US economy.

Yes, US AI labs and their models may lead the world 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/ai-drives-global-goods-trade-facing-trump-tariffs-war-headwinds?cmpid=BBD101226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9927>
. But the chips and other hardware needed for those models to operate are 
almost entirely imported. And Trump has so far kept his tariffs off those 
inputs, making them as duty free as single malt Scotch at an international 
airport.

Related: Kuehne+Nagel CEO Expects to Ride AI-Driven Cargo Wave Into 2029 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-26/kuehne-nagel-ceo-expects-to-ride-ai-driven-cargo-wave-into-2029?cmpid=BBD101226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9927>

The role of AI in trade growth also means, of course, that it’s another area 
of the global economy vulnerable if AI doesn’t live up to its promise and a 
boom becomes a burst bubble. Which is yet another reason to be worried about 
the economic consequences of an AI bubble bursting.

‘The New Ports’

But growth in trade of physical goods isn’t the only pillar of the case for 
the continuing march of globalization. It’s just the old-fashioned one.

Much more important to the case for global integration is what has happened to 
digital trade. As I write in my essay: “Data centers are the new ports, servers 
the new shipping containers and undersea cables the new silk roads.”

Yes, all of those have an AI connection. But AI isn’t the whole story. In 2016 
there were roughly 3 billion people on the internet. Today there’s more than 6 
billion.

There will be much more to talk about this week in New Delhi and Bangkok. From 
debt mountains 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-10/rich-countries-are-now-on-front-lines-as-world-s-risk-map-shifts?cmpid=BBD101226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9927>
 and rising bond yields in the rich world, to the slump in development funding 
headed for the poor world. But be brave. Utter the words. Globalization is not 
just hanging strong. It’s marching right now.
