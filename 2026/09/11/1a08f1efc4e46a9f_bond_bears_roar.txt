Deep Dive: A Quaint Town Is the Future of Energy
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-11/dutch-village-faces-demolition-for-power-grid-expansion-in-the-netherlands?cmpid=091126_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8793>
Moerdijk.Photographer: Lina Selg/Lina Selg
By any metric, Moerdijk is a sleepy town. Rows of quaint Dutch cottages line 
quiet streets, disturbed only by the occasional cyclist or dog walker. The 
buzziest spot is the local snack bar, a squat building with an oversized fake 
ice cream cone fastened to its roof. This is a place where neighbors look out 
for each other.That may soon change 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-11/dutch-village-faces-demolition-for-power-grid-expansion-in-the-netherlands?cmpid=091126_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260911&utm_content=8793>
.

 * Past a belt of woodland is a stretch of factories run by some of Europe’s 
industrial heavyweights. Shell Plc operates one of its largest chemical 
complexes in this part of the Netherlands. An RWE power plant is also present, 
along with hundreds of facilities owned by the likes of industrial gas giant 
Air Liquide and waste-management company Attero. The region’s energy needs keep 
rising.
 * That’s a problem for Moerdijk. In recent months, the centuries-old village, 
a short drive from Rotterdam, has been fighting for its survival. To 
accommodate the Netherlands’ changing energy system, the Dutch authorities are 
considering a major expansion of infrastructure in the area, potentially 
requiring Moerdijk’s 1,100 or so residents to leave and the village to be razed.
 * “Why does everything have to happen here?” said Jos Schalk, 65, who has 
lived in the town for more than six decades. “Why do we specifically have to 
leave? Couldn’t some of these developments take place somewhere else?”
 * For years, the Netherlands has struggled to square the math on a problem 
playing out across Europe. As the continent accelerates its shift to cleaner 
energy and away from fossil fuels, demand for grid capacity has grown faster 
than networks can be expanded. That’s raising difficult questions about how 
countries can meet power needs, grow their economies and find space for the 
infrastructure required to do both.
