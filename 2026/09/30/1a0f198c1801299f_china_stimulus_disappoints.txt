China is leaving investors wanting more after producing an anticipated but 
narrowly targeted economic stimulus package.

Government agencies unveiled mortgage subsidies and expanded central bank 
support for targeted sectors after markets closed Tuesday. While the moves 
amount to the biggest stimulus effort in two years and make it more likely 
China will meet its annual growth target of 4.5%-5%, the lack of broader 
measures suggest to analysts that the countrywill remain stuck 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/china-s-mini-stimulus-seen-securing-gdp-target-not-much-more?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9525>
 with a two-speed economy.

“It should spur growth enough to hit the lower end” of the range, said Duncan 
Wrigley, chief China economist at Pantheon Macroeconomics. But “it won’t solve 
China’s structural imbalances, with sluggish domestic demand and high reliance 
on exports,” he said — a view shared by many.

Chinese markets reflect the troubled state of the economy, with stocks sinking 
to more than one-year lows this month. A multi-year property downturn, along 
with a gloomy outlook on incomes, has left domestic demand languishing. A 
perceived lack of attractive projects — outside of high-tech and advanced 
manufacturing sectors — has also left investment spending mired in a slump. 
That’s left the nation’s growth primarilyrelying on exports 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-09-26/china-s-economic-regime-shift-may-sow-global-risk-new-economy?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9525>
.

All this to say, China’s divergent growth model appears likely to continue, 
with global consequences in the shape of lackluster demand and competitive 
exports.

—Richard Frost
