Diesel’s Pull

Last week’s US inflation report 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/us-consumer-spending-rises-by-most-in-a-year-core-pce-up-0-2?cmpid=BBD100626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9732>
 offered some encouraging signs that the momentum for core prices was easing as 
of August. But commodity experts are warning not to get too comfortable with 
the idea that the latest cost-of-living strain will soon be over.

The record run-up 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-21/us-diesel-tops-6-50-a-gallon-as-wars-worsen-global-fuels-crunch?cmpid=BBD100626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9732>
 in diesel costs in the US and Europe in recent weeks threatens to provide a 
lasting impulse for inflation unless underlying supply strains are addressed 
soon, analysts say. That’s because the transmission of diesel to the broader 
economy is powerful and also because it operates with a lag.

So wrote Michael Haigh and Jeremy Sellem of Societe Generale in a report 
Monday. “Diesel is woven into almost every layer of the global economy, making 
it one of the most pervasive channels through which an energy shock can feed 
into headline and core inflation.”

The fuel is used in trucks, tractors, construction equipment, heavy industry, 
ships and machinery of many kinds, and in some areas continues to be used asa 
heat source 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/german-government-instructs-energy-company-sefe-to-store-gas?cmpid=BBD100626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9732>
. So a price rise ripples through an economy-wide range of goods and services.

The Group of Seven’s move on Friday to release emergency oil and diesel stocks 
will only have ashort-term impact 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-02/g7-to-release-up-to-100-millions-of-barrels-of-diesel-and-oil?cmpid=BBD100626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9732>
, the SocGen duo argue, and risks just borrowing supply from the future. “The 
lower inventories fall, the smaller the buffer against the next disruption and 
the greater the risk that today’s price relief ultimately gives way to an even 
sharper price response.”

What’s different about the current energy shock compared with 2022 is, not 
only have crude prices soared more than they did then, but refining capacity 
has been knocked out — in the Middle East and Russia, due to the wars underway. 
That’s what’s helped propel diesel prices over a record $6.50 a gallon in the 
US and€2.24 per liter 
<https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/01/no-relief-at-the-pump-eu-diesel-hits-new-record-of-224-a-litre>
 in Europe in recent weeks.

And with American refineries going flat-out, running at 97% capacity 
<https://www.eia.gov/dnav/pet/pet_pnp_unc_dcu_nus_m.htm> as of last count, the 
risk of breakdown rises. Shutdowns for maintenance and repair can be delayed 
but hardly avoided. If underlying supply doesn’t normalize by year-end, then 
the year-on-year inflationary impulse may not fade until the third quarter of 
2027, SocGen said.

They also found a strong correlation between fluctuation in diesel prices and 
US inflation surprises. Their model pointed to next week’s September US 
consumer price index rising 4% year-on-year, versus the current consensus of 
3.6%. While that’s not a forecast, it’s worth bearing in mind after Friday’s 
upside surprise on Septembereuro-area inflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-02/euro-zone-inflation-tops-estimates-to-hit-three-year-high?cmpid=BBD100626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9732>
.

Need-to-Know Research

AI-intensive companies in the euro area are shifting toward capital markets 
over traditional bank financing, according toan ECB blog post 
<https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/date/2026/html/ecb.blog20261006~35bf3c24cb.en.html>
. These firms favor equity over debt, as AI investment centers on intangible 
assets such as software, data, and specialist expertise that are difficult to 
pledge as collateral.

When borrowing, they increasingly opt for market-based instruments such as 
bonds. ECB economists Ana Sofia Cianfarani, Paola Di Casola, José Ramos, and 
Dennis Zander note this trend carries monetary policy implications: 
longer-maturity market debt and lower leverage may reduce these firms’ 
sensitivity to ECB rate changes.

Europe, however, lags the US in converting AI innovation into investment. AI 
patenting has grown substantially but remains well behind American levels, and 
investment responds more strongly to positive AI developments in the US than in 
Europe.

“US firms can turn AI opportunities into funding and growth more easily,” the 
economists wrote. “A deeper and more integratedEuropean capital market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-21/lagarde-urges-europe-to-spur-growth-or-be-held-irresponsible?cmpid=BBD100626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9732>
 could help AI-intensive firms find the funding they need at each stage of 
growth.”
