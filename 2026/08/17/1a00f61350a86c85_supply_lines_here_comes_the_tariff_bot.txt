We are living in an AI summer, bracing for an AI election, and watching as the 
computing needs of artificial intelligence drive global trade andinvestment 
<https://unctad.org/publication/world-investment-report-2026-international-investment-turbulent-era>
. So it’s worth asking: What happens when advances in technology eventually 
mean Tariff Man becomes Tariff Bot and our economic conflicts are driven by our 
new robot overlords?

Someone somewhere is flinching at the very thought. But alongside its launch 
last week of a report decrying shifting trade patterns provoked by US tariffs 
as the “Great Transshipment Scam 
<https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/The-Great-Transshipment-Scam.pdf>
,” the White House quietlyconfirmed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/trump-trade-enforcers-deploy-ai-to-crack-down-on-tariff-dodging?cmpid=BBD081726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7734>
 something Bloomberg News’ Laura Curtis has been tracking for some time: it 
would increase its use of AI tools to crack down on tariff evaders.

Read More: US Customs Ramps Up AI Tools to Sharpen Enforcement 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-25/us-customs-ramps-up-ai-investment-in-push-to-sharpen-enforcement?cmpid=BBD081726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7734>

So the logical next step is to ask when and how — if it isn’t already — the 
power of AI will be driving not just national economic priorities but making 
battlefield decisions in our trade wars.

After all, we already know AI is playing a growing role in coordinating drone 
swarms 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-14/ai-powered-auterion-drone-swarms-are-coming-to-the-battlefield?cmpid=BBD081726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7734>
 and the process of identifying targets.

So what about keeping out Chinese drones 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/trump-imposes-100-tariffs-on-certain-drones-countering-china?cmpid=BBD081726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7734>
 and solar panels from the US market? Orquartz countertops 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-15/quartz-industry-splits-as-trump-tariffs-fuel-affordability-fears?cmpid=BBD081726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7734>
? All of which has been subject to recent tariff actions by the Trump 
administration.

What would a bot commander tell Europe the best approach is to deal with a 
surge 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-22/one-in-three-plug-in-hybrid-cars-sold-in-europe-is-now-chinese?cmpid=BBD081726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7734>
 in Chinese EV imports? Or Canada in itsnegotiations 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-15/us-canada-still-see-sticking-points-before-deadline-for-50-tariffs?cmpid=BBD081726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7734>
 and response to threatened 50% US tariffs on a small but symbolic 5% of its 
exports to its southern neighbor that may take effect this week?

For what it’s worth, this was the answer ChatGPT 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-28/openai-anthropic-staff-share-letter-asking-us-to-help-pace-ai-progress?cmpid=BBD081726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7734>
 offered when asked for a one sentence response to the question of how 
effective tariffs are as a tool to project power (which doesn’t look good for 
Ottawa): “For projecting economic power, tariffs can be effective when a 
country controls access to a large market and the target is more dependent on 
that access than the imposing country is, but they are a blunt instrument whose 
leverage erodes through retaliation, substitution, and domestic economic 
costs,” it offered.

Better Options

It also added thoughtfully that there were better tools like “targeted export 
controls, financial sanctions, investment screening, industrial subsidies, 
standards-setting, and coalition-based trade agreements” that “usually project 
economic power more precisely and at lower domestic cost.”

I’m not willing to hand over the reins just yet. As someone who increasingly 
treats my AI tools as a tireless research assistant juggling multiple questions 
at once, I’m learning that like a confident graduate student well-versed in 
academic debates, my AI bots aren’t very good at dealing with real-world gray 
areas and nuance even if they are able to digest the literature that’s out 
there in remarkable ways. (“How, with today’s real-world political limits and 
priorities, would you resolve global imbalances?” is one that seems to have it 
perplexed.)

To be fair it’s not like the humans are mastering this grand game. But I’m not 
sure what version of AI we would be getting — the well-intentioned professorial 
technocrat or the Machiavellian game player intent on winning at all costs and 
willing to bend and break rules liberally.

I’m curious how, for example, an AI general would tackle the coming month in 
US-China relations. Because if China’s Xi Jinping follows through on plans and 
flies to Washington to sit down with US President Donald Trump on or around
Sept. 24 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-06/trump-says-he-expects-to-host-china-s-xi-in-us-around-sept-24?cmpid=BBD081726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7734>
, it’s hard to imagine any other event topping that in importance this year. 
Which means we are just weeks away from what may for the global economy turn 
out to be the most consequential meeting of the year.

As a result, this summer has generated lots of often dueling academic work on 
the realities of US and Chinese economic and military power (Seehere 
<https://www.foreignaffairs.com/china/fault-lines-chinas-power-ratner-danby> and
here 
<https://www.foreignaffairs.com/china/mirage-chinas-military-edge-dennis-blair>
) and things like whether the US or China – or neither – really holds 
“escalation dominance” (Seethis 
<https://acf.sais.jhu.edu/the-path-to-u-s-china-stability-in-an-era-of-economic-warfare/>
 andthis <https://acf.sais.jhu.edu/chokepoint-statecraft-has-its-limits/>).

Avoid Decoupling

When I asked ChatGPT to come up with a plan for the US to manage the economic 
relationship with China, it took a little over six minutes to draft one built 
around a strategic objective that it nominated on its own. “I would pursue 
competitive coexistence rather than blanket decoupling,” it wrote.

It listed six key challenges and seven potential areas of action to do that. 
The latter included “coalition-based economic statecraft” and the pursuit of “a 
large-market economic-security coalition with Europe, Japan, South Korea, 
Taiwan, Canada, Australia, Mexico and other willing partners.” Among the goals: 
“rules of origin and anti-transshipment enforcement.”

Read More: Rerouted US Imports Avoiding Trump Tariffs Top $300 Billion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-23/rerouted-us-imports-avoiding-trump-s-tariffs-top-300-billion?cmpid=BBD081726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260817&utm_content=7734>

It was a plan worthy of a nerdy think-tank briefing. It closed with a section 
on “What I would not do,” which began with “I would not pursue complete 
economic separation, rely on tariffs alone, subsidize every politically favored 
industry, impose technology controls without allied participation, or judge 
success primarily by the bilateral trade balance.”

Probed a little further, it offered a mantra you would hope that today’s 
economic combatants have thought of already: “Effective leverage is the cost 
you can impose on China minus the cost China can impose on you, adjusted for 
how quickly each side can find alternatives.”

So we got a thoughtful professor. But how long would that last? Vexing 
problems often frustrate players and cause overreactions and miscalculations. 
Surely the win-at-all-costs bot would surface in time. It’s clearly a question 
we need to start considering, though.
