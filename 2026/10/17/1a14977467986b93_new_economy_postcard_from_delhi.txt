Three days isn’t enough time to understand a country of 1.4 billion people. 
But three days at the Bloomberg New Economy Forum in New Delhi, with Prime 
Minister Narendra Modi, other senior ministers and chief executives from some 
of India’s biggest businesses in attendance isn’t a bad place for a crash 
course.

The optimism on India’s prospects from the main stage at the Leela Palace this 
week was palpable. Modi is targetingdeveloped economy status 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-10-10/why-10-000-per-capita-is-india-s-magic-number-new-economy?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
 by 2047 — an objective that requires annual growth averaging upward of 8% a 
year. Recentprotests 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-15/india-top-court-to-hear-election-challenge-delhi-protest-cases?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
 on the streets of Delhi and a Reserve Bank of India (RBI) that’s been 
intervening to prop up the rupee underscore the challenges.

Below, I detail three points of tension that I thought were striking. (And for

My first takeaway on India’s economy is the contrast between structural 
drivers and cyclical drags.

The secret sauce of China’s growth miracle was enormous scale, a can-do 
government and what economists call the “advantage of backwardness.” In other 
words, it had a low starting point, which meant there was space to boost 
productivity by absorbing new technologies, and low costs gave it export 
competitiveness.

India is a different beast, but it has the same advantages. Enormous scale 
means the opportunity to attract capital and build world-beating businesses. 
The Modi government has made development a priority — and delivered significant 
wins likeunifying the tax system 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2025-09-06/india-has-a-silver-lining-inside-trump-s-tariff-storm-new-economy-saturday?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
. GDP per capita below $3,000 means cost competitiveness and opportunities to 
grow.

At the same time, it’s clear that this is a moment of cyclical challenge 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-12/india-shifts-into-crisis-mode-with-fuel-curbs-wider-deficit?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
.

Protests on the streets — the proximate cause being a move to kick some 130 
million voters 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/india-s-voter-roll-revision-what-is-sir-and-why-is-it-controversial?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
 off the electoral rolls, with youth unemployment adding fuel to the fire — are 
a visible marker of domestic stress. The RBI wading into the foreign exchange 
market to defend the rupee at 97 to the dollar shows the strains from the 
US-Israel war with Iran, with high oil prices adding to the import bill.

It’s tough not to be swept up in the enthusiasm for India’s development story. 
Still, frustrated graduates, a weak currency and an underperforming stock 
market are reminders that the challenges are real, and there’s work to do to 
make sure everyone shares in the benefits of growth.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-13/human-intelligence-in-a-hostile-world-video?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
Wondering whatThe Odyssey teaches about the Iran war? Watch Tom Orlik’s 
Bloomberg New Economy Forum presentation, “Human Intelligence in a Hostile 
World.”
A second takeaway is the contrast between the US vision of artificial 
intelligence as a sprint toward the singularity, and India’s more practical and 
inclusive focus.

In Washington, discussion of AI tends toward hyperventilation. If the US 
doesn’t go full speed ahead,China will win 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-29/us-china-ai-race-five-key-flashpoints-explained?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
 the race and we’ll all labor under communism’s digital yoke. If we do go full 
speed ahead, though, some of the AI companies warn the consequences could be
mass unemployment 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/imf-chief-alarmed-at-slow-moving-tsunami-of-ai-for-jobs-taxes?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
 or even — gulp — the extinction of the human race.

In New Delhi, the conversation appeared more optimistic and even-toned. Nandan 
Nilekani, one of the founders of Infosys Ltd. and now a digital policy mover 
and shaker, called the focus on extreme risks “disaster marketing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-13/nandan-nilekani-india-will-lead-in-scaling-ai-video?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
.” The emphasis in India is more on making AI work for everyone — there’s even 
a chatbot to provideadvice for cow farmers 
<https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/amul-goes-ai-savvy-with-sarlaben-to-boost-dairy-farmers-productivity/amp_articleshow/128192155.cms>
.

And there’s optimism that India’s IT champions will have a shaping role. It’s 
a plausible narrative. Making AI work at the enterprise level, as opposed to 
just having everyone with ChatGPT open on their desktop, will require human 
hands. Quite how many human hands, though, is open to question. Shares in 
India’s IT firms rallied in October, but are still markedly down so far in 2026.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-13/nandan-nilekani-india-will-lead-in-scaling-ai-video?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
Nandan NilekaniPhotographer: Dhiraj Singh/Bloomberg
Last but not least, geopolitics.

My prior perspective, heading into the Forum, was that New Delhi’s diplomacy 
is reactive to moves made in Washington. With President Joe Biden in power, 
India was drawninto the US orbit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-11-10/yellen-takes-friendshoring-case-to-india-in-push-against-china?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
, part of an alliance of democratic powers, sharing their technology to 
counterbalance the rise of China. With President Donald Trump pursuing an 
“America first” policy, India has had no choice but to hedge by strengthening
ties with Beijing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-12/xi-sits-down-with-modi-during-first-trip-to-india-in-seven-years?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261017&utm_content=10141>
.

On the stage and in the margins, India’s diplomats — from External Affairs 
Minister Subrahmanyam Jaishankar on down — set out a different view. India 
recognizes that power, and with it priorities, changes hands in Washington 
every few years. India’s diplomacy is based on a dispassionate view of the US, 
and a careful calculation of its interests as a major pole in a multipolar 
world.

The lesson for me — perhaps an obvious one — is that agency in international 
affairs extends beyond Washington and Beijing. The lesson for the US leaders: 
foreign powers gauge reliability based on behavior across administrations, not 
just the ones you run.
