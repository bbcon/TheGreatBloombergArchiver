Mine to nowhere

By Todd Woody 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVl1IPrK6vs/todd-woody?cmpid=BBD091226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8817>

Copperhead Resources Inc. was just another money-losing Canadian gold 
prospector last year when President Donald Trump ordered the US government to
start issuing deep-sea mining licenses 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-07-21/why-trump-s-deep-sea-gold-rush-won-t-materialize-for-years?cmpid=BBD091226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8817>
 to tap the ocean floor’s vast store of critical minerals. But the company 
wasn’t about to miss out on what the administration was calling “the next gold 
rush 
<https://www.noaa.gov/news-release/next-gold-rush-president-trump-unlocks-access-to-critical-deep-seabed-minerals>
.”

In a matter of months, Copperhead rebranded itself as Deep Sea Minerals Corp. 
<https://deepseamineralscorp.com/>, set up a US subsidiary and applied for a 
federal license to prospect for polymetallic nodules in a remote region of the 
Pacific Ocean called the Clarion-Clipperton Zone. The company’s lack of 
deep-sea experience has so far proved no hindrance: In June it said the 
government had deemed its application to be in “substantial compliance” with 
licensing requirements. That month, Deep Sea Minerals had just C$1.6 million 
($1.2 million) in cash on hand, according to Canadian securities filings; it’s 
now trying to raise C$50 million to start exploring. Chief Executive Officer 
James Deckelman acknowledges the sudden corporate transformation is 
opportunistic. The Trump administration, he says, is creating “access to seabed 
polymetallic nodules that didn’t exist before.”
Polymetallic nodules on the ocean floor.Source: NOAA
Deckelman’s company is one of a small but growing group of largely foreign 
entrepreneurs establishing US subsidiaries in anticipation of obtaining 
deep-sea mining licenses, mostly for the Clarion-Clipperton Zone. There, 
several miles below the ocean’s surface,trillions of avocado-size nuggets of 
minerals 
<https://www.usgs.gov/publications/deep-ocean-polymetallic-nodules-and-cobalt-rich-ferromanganese-crusts-global-ocean-new>
 potentially worth hundreds of billions of dollars carpet the seabed, rich in 
the cobalt, copper and nickel used to make electric-car batteries and other 
electronics. But seabed mining remains commercially unproven, and ramping up an 
industry that doesn’t yet exist will cost billions, experts say, casting doubt 
on these would-be marine miners’ wherewithal to realize the president’s dreams 
of establishing “American dominance 
<https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/04/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-unleashes-americas-offshore-critical-minerals-and-resources/>
” in the nascent sector.

In typical Trumpian style, US efforts to dive into the seabed aren’t without 
controversy. TheClarion-Clipperton Zone 
<https://www.usgs.gov/media/images/locations-clarion-clipperton-zone>, which 
lies between Hawaii and Mexico, is controlled by theInternational Seabed 
Authority <https://isa.org.jm/>, a United Nations-affiliated organization. The 
ISA has spent the past two decades painstakingly writingstill-unfinished mining 
regulations 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-03-19/deep-sea-miners-are-set-to-dig-for-critical-minerals-even-if-rules-aren-t-done?cmpid=BBD091226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8817>
 to minimize potentially catastrophic harm to one of the last untouched and 
most fantastically biodiverse places on the planet. In the coming months the 
administration also plans to auction off leases for a chunk of the Pacific 
seabed the size of Colorado that surrounds American Samoa and the Northern 
Mariana Islands. (These US territories oppose strip-mining their marine 
ecosystems.)

Beyond the legal questions are technological challenges. A commercial seabed 
mining operation requires a fleet of giant robots to rumble along the seafloor 
vacuuming up nodules 24/7 under the crushing pressure and frigid temperatures 
of the abyss. The rocks then must travel nearly 3 miles through a pipe that 
connects the nodule collector to a ship on the water’s surface. From there 
another ship must transport them thousands of miles to a refinery to be 
processed. Nodule-refining technology itself still needs to be developed, and 
few nations outside China possess such industrial capacity or expertise.

That’s a lot to contend with for a small company such as Deep Sea Minerals, or 
would-be rivalAmerican Deep Sea Minerals Inc. <https://americandsm.com/>, 
incorporated last August by a San Francisco hedge fund partner namedGraham 
Goulet <https://www.valiantcapital.com/team/>. It had $150,000 cash on hand in 
November when it filed its application. Goulet declined an interview request.
Samples of polymetallic nodules at the Viridian Biometals lab in Pasadena, 
California.Photographer: Alex Welsh/Bloomberg
“Color me very skeptical of companies pivoting into this space without a 
substantial track record behind them,” says Lyle Trytten, a Canadian mining 
consultant and veteran of the metals processing industry. “It feels to me more 
like a gold rush — where the companies selling shovels and wheelbarrows make 
money but most prospectors don’t.” Last year, Trytten and a colleague concluded 
that deep-sea mining is “highly unlikely” to become commercially viable within 
the next 15 years because of “substantial economic and technological 
uncertainties.” A 2025 Rand Corp. study came to asimilar conclusion 
<https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA3560-1.html>, finding that 
“seabed mining companies struggle to attract capital” thanks to a host of 
challenges: the volatile price of metals, pushback from environmental groups 
and mostly untested technology.

Non-emergency

By Leslie Kaufman 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUZ8izNmriE/leslie-kaufman?cmpid=BBD091226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8817>

In a setback to the Trump administration’s push to prevent coal-fired power 
plants from retiring nationwide, a federal appeals court rejected its effort to 
keep an aging Michigan plant running.

The US Court of Appeals for the DC Circuit ruled Friday that the Energy 
Department exceeded its authority when it invoked emergency powers to keep 
Consumers Energy Co.’s J.H. Campbell plant operating past its planned May 2025 
retirement.

The administration has argued that aging coal plants are needed to help meet 
rising electricity demand and prevent power shortages. The latest of six 
successive 90-day emergencyorders 
<https://www.energy.gov/articles/energy-secretary-keeps-coal-fired-generation-operational-midwest?utm_source=chatgpt.com>
 to keep Campbell online was issued Aug. 14, when Energy Secretary Chris Wright 
said the 1,420-megawatt plant was needed to address grid reliability concerns 
in the Midwest. The department has cited projections of tight reserve margins 
and growing electricity demand as grounds for the orders.
The J.H. Campbell coal-fired power plant in West Olive, Michigan on May 31, 
2025.Photographer: Jim West/UCG/Getty Images via Universal Images Group 
Editorial
The court disagreed that the situation qualified as an emergency under the 
Federal Power Act, finding that the department had stretched a narrowly written 
provision beyond what Congress intended.

The ruling matters beyond Michigan. The administration has repeatedly used the 
same authority to keep coal and other fossil fuel-generating units operating or 
available around the country. The Energy Department has taken other steps to 
extend the life of coal plants, includinghundreds of millions of dollars 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-04/trump-approves-700-million-in-bid-to-revive-us-coal-industry?cmpid=BBD091226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8817>
 in federal support.
