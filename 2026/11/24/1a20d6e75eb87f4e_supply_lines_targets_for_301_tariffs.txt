Even though the chip trade wars 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-24/china-halts-critical-minerals-ban-to-maintain-truce-with-us?cmpid=BBD112426_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11745>
 between the US and China are stealing the spotlight these days, old-school 
tariffs from Washington haven’t completely faded away.

US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, speaking Friday 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-20/greer-sees-us-trade-probe-certainly-concluding-by-year-end?cmpid=BBD112426_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11745>
 to Bloomberg TV, said he expects his office’s report on industrial 
overcapacity to be released very soon and “certainly” by year end. (Watch the 
full interviewhere 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-20/ustr-greer-on-chip-curbs-rare-earths-us-china-trade-video?cmpid=BBD112426_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11745>
.)

The timing is tricky politically because announcing a fresh batch of Section 
301 tariffs before the Group of 20leaders meeting <https://g20.org/events> Dec. 
14-15 might sour the mood in Miami.

Related: Trump Says He Expects China to Buy Another 250 Boeing Planes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-23/trump-says-he-expects-china-to-buy-another-250-boeing-planes?cmpid=BBD112426_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11745>

Diane Swonk, chief economist with KPMG, said in an X post 
<https://x.com/DianeSwonk/status/2124846233353109706> Monday that Greer has 
indicated that existing trade deals will be honored, including 15% caps for 
most imports from European Union and Japan.

She noted that the EU is at 10%, “leaving more room to rise,” and Japan’s 
levies start at 12.5%. European officials will want theirTurnberry deal 
<https://commission.europa.eu/topics/trade/eu-us-trade-deal_en> to be 
respected, and other countries with signed agreements will, too.

‘Risks Collide’

Then, Swonk continued, “the risks collide: China could see a stacking of 
tariff rates again, when the truce in escalation lapses Jan. 10.” She said 
China has rare earths for leverage.

Bloomberg News previously reported 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-24/us-eyes-china-overcapacity-tariffs-of-7-5-before-xi-trump-talks?cmpid=BBD112426_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261124&utm_content=11745>
 the administration was eyeing a 7.5% tariff on China based on the overcapacity 
probe, a move that would restore duties on Chinese imports close to 20% — a 
level Beijing has previously said is consistent with its trade truce with 
Washington.

“The Supreme Court ruling and interim tariff regime helped take some pressure 
off effective tariff rates and inflation,” Swonk wrote. “A broader Section 301 
round could put some of that pressure back. Tariffs didn’t disappear. They 
changed legal lanes. Now we are seeing the lanes merge again to lift tariff 
rates.”

The 301 case 
<https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2026/march/ustr-initiates-section-301-investigations-relating-structural-excess-capacity-and-production>
 targets 15 countries and the EU. Inpublic filings 
<https://comments.ustr.gov/s/docket?docketNumber=USTR-2026-0067>, governments 
have already made their cases against their association with factory 
overcapacity.

“The US has enjoyed a consistent trade surplus with Singapore for over 20 
years,” the island city-state’s “exports to the US are not due to any form of 
excess capacity,” its Ministry of Trade and Industry wrote in a letter to USTR.

Vietnam’s submission concludes that “structural excess capacity does not 
exist” in the Asian nation.

Country Responses

Norway’s ministry of foreign affairs filed 118 pages of analysis and data to 
support its case against tariffs.

“No sector shows indications of structural excess capacity that could burden 
US commerce,” Norway’s document states. “To the contrary, in the oil and gas 
sector, Norway plays a strategically valuable role, to the benefit of the 
United States, by ensuring reliable and stable production aligned with global 
demand, through its rules-based, market-driven and transparent licensing 
system.”

India’s government denied USTR’s allegations and called for an end the 
investigation.

“Since India and the United States have initiated a process of negotiating a 
Bilateral Trade Agreement, any trade concerns should be addressed within the 
framework of the trade negotiations and not through unilateral measures,” the 
commerce and industry ministry wrote.

Given the stakes, the trade world is eager to know when the months-long 
process will end. Here’s how four panelists on the latest weeklypodcast 
<https://www.wita.org/ep-70/?_kx=vq8b-9Yacjud4OvCVdvrCUqX6jdegq8AJWpaU9GfNpg.WeKaKa>
 from WITA - The International Trade Association speculated on the timing for 
USTR’s excess capacity report:

 * Ed Gresser, vice president and director for trade and global markets at the 
Progressive Policy Institute: He think’s USTR will wait until after the G-20 
meeting and until they know if the courts will strike down the forced labor 
tariffs “because that will determine what rates to put in for excess capacity.”
 * Peter Harrell, a visiting scholar at the Georgetown Institute for 
International Economic Law: He agrees it’ll be released after the US Court of 
International Trade decision, which could come “any day” and “I bet it will be 
before Christmas.”
 * Brian Janovitz, a partner with DLA Piper US: He thinks it’ll come be after 
the G-20 because “they won’t want the release of this to have an influence on 
the conversations at the G-20.”
