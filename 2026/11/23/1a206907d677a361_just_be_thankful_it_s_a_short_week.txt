What Just Happened?

We have a blissfully short but likely hectic market week ahead before the US 
gives thanks <https://www.history.com/topics/thanksgiving> on Thursday — 
although many traders will want to know exactly what for. They’re now in the 
most significant selloff in some years.

Unlike the other two market breaks under Trump 2.0, caused by the Liberation 
Day tariffs and the attack on Iran, this one lacks an obvious external driver — 
and there’s no clear single measure that could end it. Let’s use the weekend 
breathing space to take stock. This is how the Nasdaq 100 has moved this month, 
with some key news stories marked:

The Nasdaq 100’s instant reaction was to rise on news of the Democrats’ “Blue 
Tsunami” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-04/blue-tsunami-crashes-down-on-donald-trump?cmpid=BBD112326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11708>
 and that Jensen Huang of Nvidia had had an affair with a woman US authorities
believed to be a Chinese intelligence asset 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/spy-chief-says-us-probing-alleged-spy-tied-to-former-nvidia-ceo?cmpid=BBD112326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11708>
. It also gained after strongnon-farm payrolls for October 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-06/us-jobs-report-october-2026-key-takeaways-on-employment-payrolls?cmpid=BBD112326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11708>
 that many thoughtcemented a rate hike for next month 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-18/fed-minutes-show-almost-all-officials-expect-more-rate-hikes?cmpid=BBD112326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11708>
, and afterNvidia’s results 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-17/nvidia-delivers-solid-outlook-in-first-post-huang-earnings?cmpid=BBD112326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11708>
 showed the company was still doing fine. TheAnthropic IPO 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-20/anthropic-ant-ipo-on-nasdaq-overcame-ai-fears-and-warnings?cmpid=BBD112326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11708>
 was as successful as anyone could ask, and then the bottom fell out of the 
market.

All these factors contributed to the selloff; it’s too simple to blame the 
Democrats, or Huang, or the Fed in isolation. And the market isn’t yet 
compellingly cheap. The S&P 500’s earnings yield (the inverse of the 
price/earnings ratio) remains lower than the 10-year Treasury yield:

Measures of risk sentiment show that this is the biggest scare since 
Liberation Day last year (this is true of high-yield spreads, Bloomberg’s 
overall financial conditions index, and the VIX volatility index) but don’t 
suggest anything worse, at least yet:

Time to Buy?

The Nasdaq 100 looks oversold, and is at its lowest compared to its 50-day 
moving average in the last five years, outside a couple of days around the 
Liberation Day selloff:

That means a decent chance of a trading bounce. But there’s a crucial 
difference with last April’s tariffs shock; that was a specific reaction to a 
single stupid policy that could then be reversed. The market rebounded as soon 
as President Donald Trump relented; there’s no similar source of relief this 
time.

For those with a long time horizon, the biggest AI stocks certainly look 
cheap. The market now puts more than double the multiple on Procter & Gamble’s 
future earnings than it does on Nvidia’s:

P&G is a great company; but there is far less chance of organic growth in 
household products than there is in the chips that power AI. And optimism about 
Nvidia’s earnings remains undimmed, at least in official forecasts reported to 
Bloomberg:

So if you have confidence in the long-term future of AI, this is an 
opportunity to start buying.

A Correction Long in the Works

The fact is that momentum toward last week’s selloff had developed since 
midyear, when an extraordinary surge in chip stocks came to an abrupt halt. 
Since then, momentum stocks have taken a pummeling while investors have flocked 
to value, as illustrated by Bloomberg’s Factors To Watch service:

This looks like the bursting of the latest in a series of rolling bubbles. 
Hyperscalers’ shares had gone nowhere for a year while data-center and chip 
stocks picked up the baton. This one has now deflated:

The reversal in the “Long Semiconductors/Short Software” trade, built on the 
belief that AI would render existing software business models redundant, has 
been particularly dramatic:

And tech stocks now, remarkably, lag the rest of the market for the year:

This remains a localized selloff, though with widening collateral damage. The 
equal-weighted S&P 500, measuring the average stock, is down only 7.2% from its 
peak, and still has an 8% gain for the year. That compares to an 11% dive for 
the cap-weighted version, which is now up less than 1% for the year.

It’s still possible to read this as a somewhat drastic correction to a 
localized bubble which has formed in plain sight, and not a major incident that 
could have macroeconomic impact and justify government action. That brings us 
to...

A Parallel That Suggests It’s the Fed’s Fault

In 2018, Trump was in the second year of his term and a new chairman took over 
at the Fed. The Republicans did badly in the midterms, the new chairman 
continued to sound hawkish, and then the market — which had already been 
slowing — went into a free fall that ended amid the reduced liquidity of the 
shortened Christmas Eve trading day:

Back then, Jerome Powell wanted to continue tightening the Fed’s balance sheet 
“on autopilot.” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2018-12-19/markets-revolt-as-steadfast-powell-whistles-past-recent-sell-off?cmpid=BBD112326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11708>
 This time, Kevin Warsh and his colleagues have sounded hawkish about inflation 
that’s running higher than expected. A couple of governors areconsidering the 
possibility of a 50-basis-point “jumbo” hike next month 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-18/fed-s-waller-says-fed-should-consider-half-point-rate-hike?cmpid=BBD112326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11708>
. Does this set us up for a repeat of 2018, with the final massacre brought 
forward to the reduced trading day ahead of Thanksgiving and some cave-in by 
the Fed coming thereafter?

The overlay chart is based on a sample of one. It proves nothing. But the big 
midyear shift in momentum did come as Warsh gave his first press conference as 
chairman and sounded far more hawkish than predicted. And the belief that the 
Fed is planning to overtighten shines from inflation expectations:

Those expectations tumbled as crude prices have fallen this month, thanks to 
fears not being fulfilled that the US and Israel would escalate the war in Iran 
after their elections. The prospective price of crude at the end of next year 
has now tanked. Why have Fed governors taken no account?

That at least is the thinking in markets, where the fear is that the Fed is 
heading for a major mistake. Anna Wu of Floodlines Research in Melbourne speaks 
for many:
The bond market and the Fed are not in full agreement. One of them could be 
making a mistake. In this case, I think it is the Fed, because the market may 
not be strong enough to take it this time. Equities are in a correction. 
Households are under the pump as real wages have been falling since April. Top 
AI spenders are losing market cap fast while, together, spending more cash than 
they bring in. In this environment, that could become a trust issue.
The Fed has its credibility at stake and needs to avoid the impression of a 
“401(k) Put” in which it will always cut rates when equities fall. Inflation is 
rising, even if gasoline is excluded, and the aggregate measures of the economy 
remain strong. So there are limits to how much it can pivot.

But even if some restraint from the Fed might be a necessary condition for 
calming the market, it’s not a sufficient one. The clues are in the gold price.

No Haven for Gold Bulls

Just nine months ago, in February, 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-02-04/gold-holds-near-record-high-as-tariff-confusion-aids-haven-bids?cmpid=BBD112326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11708>
 gold was trading at nearly $5,500 an ounce and bulls thought $6,000 would be 
only a matter of time. Now, the price is approaching $3,500, downmore than 30% 
from its peak 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-02-04/gold-holds-near-record-high-as-tariff-confusion-aids-haven-bids?cmpid=BBD112326_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11708>
, and optimism is nowhere to be found. A rate hike next month precludes any 
bullishness in the near term, while bullion has provided no haven from the 
AI-driven selloff:

Even apart from rising rate expectations, a falling gold price in a market 
selloff is a normal occurrence. When panic sets in, asset allocators sell 
traditional safe havens including gold to raise cash. As Dean Chen of Bitunix 
explains, gold’s failure to provide a hedge reflects a market that is 
prioritizing liquidity and leverage management over capital preservation.

A hawkish Fed contributes to the liquidity problem, but it spreads much wider. 
Mike Howell of Global Liquidity Indexes argues:
The main reason US liquidity weaker is not the Fed but the hoovering up of 
liquidity by new issues, the Treasury and the strong economy. The Fed is 
largely neutral looking at bank reserve levels and so on. Gold, silver and 
Bitcoin are all liquidity sensitive assets and all have entered a bear market 
around three months after the peak in our Global Liquidity Index (GLI) in late 
2025.
Last week’s capitulation selloff came as investors struggled to make space for 
a big slug of Anthropic stock in their portfolios. Global liquidity, Howell 
argues, has been driven as much by China’s anemic economy and continued refusal 
to resort to a big stimulus as by anything in the US. This chart shows his 
measure of combined changes in Federal Reserve and People’s Bank of China 
liquidity, and its lagged effect on the gold price. It’s a tight fit (and 
there’s a similarly strong relationship for cryptocurrencies):

In the short term, a reduction in real rates would help gold, and probably 
requires a more dovish Fed. Real rates drove gold’s very different performances 
across the four most recent major equity drawdowns, as BCA Research’s Roukaya 
Ibrahim observes. It rose during the dot-com bust and the Global Financial 
Crisis, then fell during the Covid crash of 2020 and the equity rout two years 
later:
Source: BCA Research
During the dot-com bust, falling real rates provided a tailwind, while in 
2022, inflationary pressures boosted real rates even as equities fell. In the 
current context, gold is struggling during an initial rush to deleverage, but 
Ibrahim argues that it would come into its own if this turns into a prolonged 
equity selloff, as that would probably force the Fed to cut:
Because AI capex and the wealth effect are concentrated in the US, the US 
economy would be the most vulnerable to an AI equity selloff, pushing nominal 
and real yields lower. Moreover, a reversal of flows into US assets would put 
downward pressure on the dollar.
Indeed, investors are plowing money into gold ETF holdings even as prices 
weaken. That reflects a belief that gold could still be protection if this 
turns into a far worse selloff:

That eventual gold rally must wait for the dollar peak, and for lower rates. A 
more dovish Fed would help — but it’s unwise to think this is a pure repeat of 
2022 and that a “Warsh Pivot” could on its own repair the damage.

—Richard Abbey
