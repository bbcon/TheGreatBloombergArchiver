We’ve spent the past two years exploring the economic and political costs and 
benefits of tariffs. Now it’s time to talk about another economic weapon: 
export controls.

There have been multiple triggers for the AI-related slump that has hit global 
financial markets 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-22/trump-s-ai-chip-crackdown-on-china-after-nvidia-crisis-squeezes-southeast-asia?cmpid=BBD112326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11710>
 in recent weeks. Underlying it all is an increasingly broad concern over 
whether the anticipated trillions of dollars in capital being deployed will pay 
off either in returns to investors or in economic growth.

President Donald Trump on Sunday blamed 
<https://truthsocial.com/@realDonaldTrump/117661238591305263> Democrats for the 
recent tumble in equity prices, though the recalibration of investor attitudes 
had started before the midterm elections. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent on 
Sunday added recent hawkishcomments 
<https://www.cbsnews.com/news/transcript-treasury-secretary-scott-bessent-face-the-nation-with-margaret-brennan-nov-22-2026/>
 from Federal Reserve policymakers to the targets for blame.

Related: South Korea’s Early Exports Gain After $1 Trillion Milestone 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-23/south-korea-s-early-exports-gain-after-1-trillion-milestone?cmpid=BBD112326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11710>

It doesn’t help that AI and its different layers remain a mysterious realm to 
many investors, or that the breadth of possible outcomes offered by even 
industry insiders ranges from Armageddon to Utopia. But one recent contributor 
to the market angst is simple to understand.

Jensen Huang’s downfall 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/spy-chief-says-us-probing-alleged-spy-tied-to-former-nvidia-ceo?cmpid=BBD112326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11710>
 amid his affair with an alleged Chinese spy has raised questions over 
chipmaker Nvidia’s sales of its best chips to Southeast Asian data centers used 
by Chinese tech companies as a way around US export controls. There are also 
indications some of those chips may have been smuggled to China.

From a national security perspective, closing the loophole is a no-brainer. 
The US is in a geopolitical AI race with China. Which is why Washington has 
strict limits on sales of leading chips to Chinese companies. And why the US 
Commerce Department announcednew rules 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/us-moves-to-limit-remote-access-to-advanced-ai-chips?cmpid=BBD112326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11710>
 last week to restrict sales of chips to a short list of countries with data 
centers that offer cross-border access to computing power.

Data Center Tax

Markets tend to be agnostic on national security issues, though. They are more 
interested in the economic effects of policies.

Which is why the new Commerce rules contributed to a slump in the shares of 
Nvidia and other chipmakers last week. As didTrump’s declaration 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-20/trump-threatens-to-tax-companies-building-data-centers-abroad?cmpid=BBD112326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11710>
 on Friday that he would tax any US companies building data centers offshore.

The bottom line for investors is that more restrictions on sales of chips 
means fewer being sold and less revenues. Likewise, fewer offshore data centers 
built by US companies means less business for cloud companies like Amazon and 
Microsoft.

Those specific concerns are layered over ones about the broader economic 
effects of a global slowdown in infrastructure investment related to AI. The 
new Commerce rules woulddisproportionately hit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-22/trump-s-ai-chip-crackdown-on-china-after-nvidia-crisis-squeezes-southeast-asia?cmpid=BBD112326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11710>
 Southeast Asian countries like Malaysia and Indonesia. A slowdown in chip 
production would mean fewer exports from tech-heavy economies like South Korea 
and Taiwan and hit the accumulation of wealth in those countries that has been 
flowinglargely into US equities 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-10-12/how-the-ai-boom-is-fueling-global-financial-risks?cmpid=BBD112326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11710>
.

The concerns surrounding a potential hit to growth are real. In the US, 
estimates show that as much as a full percentage point of growth is the result 
of investment in AI-related infrastructure. In last month’s World Economic 
Outlook, the International Monetary Fund said a slowdown in AI investment 
combined with a correction in global financial markets would take as much as 
1.1% offglobal growth by 2028 
<https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/weo/2026/october/english/text.pdf>
.

There are certainly arguments to be made that letting a little air out of an 
AI bubble may be a good thing. Slowing down the AI race may also have value for 
humanity and governments and societies still grappling with the implications of 
this technological revolution.

But what if pricking the bubble leads to a financial crisis?

Not so long ago the main discussion about export controls was over their 
effectiveness. They did not stop China developing its own models like DeepSeek 
and contributed to a surge in Chinese investment in its own chipmakers.

Rise of Geoeconomics

Now it’s worth having a frank discussion about the potential costs. Economic 
security is national security is the mantra embraced in Washington over the 
past decade. But what if prioritizing national security hurts economic security?

There have been lots of attempts in recent years to break down analytical and 
policy silos. A whole field of geoeconomics has emerged in which economists and 
national security types analyze the sources of dependence and potential choke 
points. The policymakers’ view in the wake of the pandemic being that national 
security and resilience matter more than economic efficiency or corporate 
profits.

But the new discipline hasn’t really been tested. Little analysis has focused 
on what economic costs countries — and their citizens — are willing to pay in 
the name of geopolitical competition. More importantly, the discussion so far 
has been conducted without an economic crisis as the context.

The recent US electoral experience with tariffs indicates that voters aren’t 
willing toabsorb much of a cost 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/jamieson-greer-on-being-tasked-with-rebuilding-trump-s-unpopular-tariff-wall?cmpid=BBD112326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11710>
. Trump’s tariffs have become associated with higher prices and by some 
measures have a lower approval rating than the president does. It is probably a 
stretch given all the other forces involved in the new AI turmoil. But what 
would happen to export controls if they contributed to the onset of a financial 
crisis?

Economic statecraft and geoeconomics have been increasingly important in 
setting the agenda in Washington in recent years. We may be about to explore 
some of the economic costs of that.

Charted Territory

China’s factory reach 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-19/china-s-industrial-parks-abroad-help-beat-trade-barriers?cmpid=BBD112326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11710>
 | Morocco’s Tanger Tech is one of at least 145 industrial zones sites funded 
by China worldwide, with dozens more being planned, according to the Council on 
Foreign Relations. Chinese companies have plenty of reasons to leave home, 
where competition can be ferocious. Building abroad can lower costs and put 
factories closer to raw materials and customers. US and European tariffs have 
made it more expensive to sell goods made in China. And customs authorities are 
paying closer attention to the ruse of routing products through a third country 
to dodge duties.
