Let’s be honest. There are very few documents in international economics less 
meaningful than a Group of 20 communique unless a crisis is raging and the 
world is in dire need of joint action. So the lack of a unified declaration 
from last week’s G20 trade ministers’ meeting in Milwaukee shouldn’t be cause 
for alarm.

But there was a clear signal in the outcomes of the gathering. And it wasn’t a 
ringing endorsement of the American hosts.

The US went into the G20 talks touting President Donald Trump’s project to 
remake the global trading system.

“We gather as possible architects of a new international economic order,” 
Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said in anopening statement 
<https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/speeches-and-remarks/2026/september/ambassador-greers-opening-remarks-g20-trade-ministerial>
 that took delegates on a trip through American and Milwaukee history, with 
nods to famed architects including Antoni Gaudi and Frank Lloyd Wright.

Related: US, India Officials Signal Trade Deal Talks at an Impasse 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-05/us-india-officials-signal-trade-deal-negotiations-at-an-impasse?cmpid=BBD100526_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9701>

Greer wanted to focus on four areas: the weaponization of food in economic 
disputes; the tolerance of forced labor’s use to make traded goods; cracking 
down on structural excess industrial capacity; and reforming the most favored 
nation (MFN) tariff system, a pillar of global trade rules since the General 
Agreement on Tariffs and Trade went into effect in 1947.

The end result? 

A consensus among G20 ministers that using food as a weapon was beyond the 
acceptable bounds. And, well, not much else toward the host’s four workstreams.

The US got only Argentina and Mexico to sign on to a statement vowing 
<https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/Press/Releases/Statement%20on%20Eliminating%20Forced%20Labor%20in%20Global%20Supply%20Chains_final.pdf>
 to do more to eliminate forced labor from supply chains. There was no 
consensus on the need for action on structural excess industrial production 
with “a handful of members” objecting. On the matter of reforming the MFN 
system, a closing chair’s statement drafted by the US declared that the meeting 
“marked the beginning of a serious and necessary deliberation.”

The big tell in the closing statement 
<https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/Press/Releases/G20%20Chair's%20Statement.pdf>
 coming out of Milwaukee was that the US was its lone signatory. When G20 
finance ministers met last month in Asheville, North Carolina, the equivalent
statement <https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0620> was backed by 
everyone but China, which objected to just four of its 17 paragraphs.

There’s an obvious reason for the reluctance. 

The US’s interest in forced labor is tied to the tariffs of 10%-12.5% it 
slapped on dozens of trading partners (including all of the G20) over the 
summer as part of its efforts to rebuild Trump’s tariff wall after the Supreme 
Court declared his original duties illegal. Those new tariffs are also being 
challenged and faced tough questionsduring a hearing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/trump-s-latest-global-tariffs-face-tough-questions-from-us-court?cmpid=BBD100526_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9701>
 last week.

China and Overcapacity

The US push on excess industrial capacity came amid growing concerns around 
the world about surging Chinese exports. It also, however, came as Washington 
threatens to impose yet more tariffs as part of an investigation into 16 
trading partners. Those targets include many of the very countries concerned 
about Chinese exports. Which means the US is targeting many of the very 
counties it could rally to the cause.

There is also widespread suspicion outside the US of the loose definition of 
excess capacity Washington is throwing around. Which seems to be based on the 
idea that producing more of anything than is needed domestically is an economic 
crime.

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-10/europe-is-trying-to-gauge-how-much-pain-it-can-take-from-china?cmpid=BBD100526_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9701>

We are still waiting for the result of the investigations launched earlier 
this year. But as Christopher Padilla, who served at USTR and in the Commerce 
Department during the administration of President George W. Bush, put it in a
satirical post 
<https://christopherpadilla1.substack.com/p/too-much-tequila?r=zlx1&utm_medium=ios>
 over the weekend, the presumption appears to be “everyone is guilty.”

There is a constituency eager to talk about how to respond to China’s 
export-led economic policies and the distortions produced by its industrial 
policies. But when the US is treating many of those potential allies like 
economic cheaters for selling into a global market, why would they sign on?

Again, no one should get overly exercised about any G20 statement. The signal 
coming out of Milwaukee, however, was yet another sign of a growing skepticism 
of Trump’s trade project at home and abroad.

Trump’s bid to remake the global trading system is well underway. At home, 
though, it faces legal challenges and the uncomfortable political reality that 
his tariffs are unpopular with voters heading to the polls in a month. And out 
in the rest of the world, the project is facing open defiance fromhistorical 
allies like Canada 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-19/great-lakes-bind-us-canada-trade-imperiled-by-trump-tariffs?cmpid=BBD100526_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9701>
 and a quieter resistance in diplomatic forums. Something’s not going to plan.
