Inflation, the Next Installment

The biggest macroeconomic number of the month is due on Wednesday. The US 
labor market appears to be broadly in equilibrium, according to the latest 
download of data. That provides little justification for shifting monetary 
policy in either direction, and prompted overnight index swaps to push back 
their projected timing for the next hike in the fed funds rate.

However, the market still remains braced for a couple of hikes to come through 
eventually, and that is largely because of inflation. The successive shocks of 
tariffs and the Iran war before price rises could return to normal after the 
post-pandemic surge have left it above target, and with opinion widely 
dispersed.

The next moves from the Federal Reserve, which has kept short-term target 
rates on hold all year, and from longer-term Treasury yields, which have been 
steadily rising and threatening to break out into new post-crisis highs, depend 
on inflation. Core CPI was startlingly low in June, less than even the lowest 
estimate in Bloomberg’s survey of economists. The expectation now is that this 
will continue and further ease the pressure for rate hikes, but there will be 
relief when and if this is confirmed:

The balance of comments by senior officials since the last Federal Open Market 
Committee meeting has been hawkish, with Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack 
saying Monday that “some number” of rate hikes may be needed 
<https://bloom.bg/45SuX0p>. So further falls toward the 2% inflation target 
would be helpful.

Despite optimism about consumer prices, there are signs of pressure in the 
pipeline. Raw materials prices are increasing, and not just oil. The Commodity 
Research Board’s RIND (Raw Industrials) index, which covers industrial products 
that aren’t quoted on futures markets, is rising sharply, as is Bloomberg’s 
index of the major industrial metals:

One of the greatest issues facing the Fed comes from the stock market. 
Earnings are climbing at a rate that in the past has virtually ensured rising 
10-year Treasury yields:

The stock market is similarly positioned for higher inflation. To some extent, 
it is natural that big increases in earnings and revenues (of which more later) 
likely mean an expanding economic cycle, bringing price rises with it. But it 
also emerges clearly from stockpickers’ bottom-up decisions.

Of late, the market has been dominated by cyclical stocks that tend to be 
positively correlated with inflation. For years since it was introduced in 
2020, Societe Generale SA’s stock market inflation proxy, based on developed 
market stocks most correlated with inflation, moved in line with the MSCI World 
index. Over the last year, it has far outperformed:

That looks like a strong warning that central bankers should be alert for 
inflation. To quote SocGen’s Andrew Lapthorne:

Markets are no longer expecting somewhat contradictory strong profit growth 
and interest rates cuts, but such strong profit growth usually merits rates 
rises. Little wonder our equity-based inflation proxy index is up so strongly 
(+71%) over the last 12 months.

It’s certainly hard to argue that monetary policy is at present restricting 
the corporate sector. Another spotless inflation report would alleviate some 
concerns — but the evidence from second-quarter earnings is still quite 
startling.

Welcome to the Party

If earnings growth has been a party dominated exclusively by Big Tech, the 
guest list expanded in the second quarter. Nearly everyone else is joining in 
as tech giants relinquish their advantage. With all S&P 500 companies now 
having reported their earnings, non-technology groups are on course for their 
second-best quarter since the debut of ChatGPT in late 2022 — though they’re 
still far behind the remarkable overall earnings growth recorded by the 
Magnificent Seven leading the artificial intelligence buildout:

This is quite unmistakably a huge cyclical boom. Deutsche Bank’s Binky Chadha 
notes that overall S&P 500 profits growth accelerated to 34%, from 25% in the 
first quarter, one of the highest figures ever outside of recoveries from 
recessions. All sectors are on track to deliver growth, with seven of the 11 in 
double digits. While tech is still the lead driver, its contribution to 
aggregate growth is “only” 55% from about 90% a year ago:
Source: Bloomberg Finance LP, Deutsche Bank Asset Allocation 
 Questions are mounting <https://bloom.bg/4qbtifU> over how quickly the capex 
plowing into AI will translate into profitability, but tech’s earnings growth 
is still impressive — it’s just that the cost of that investment is 
increasingly hard to ignore. Their free cash flow is deteriorating at an 
unprecedented pace as spending on data centers gobbles it up, even as the rest 
of the corporate sector generates more cash:

The numbers for the overall S&P 500, as shown by Bloomberg’s Graph 
Fundamentals function, confirm a protracted run in which earnings per share 
have risen much faster than free cash flow. This started after companies had 
made big pandemic cost cuts, and then went into overdrive after the arrival of 
ChatGPT:

As meaningful as depressed free cash flows may be, they are far from 
conclusive. Cloud revenues at Alphabet Inc., Microsoft Corp. and Amazon.com 
Inc. all grew by more than 40%. This, Juan Manuel Correa of BCA Research 
argues, shows that hyperscalers are beginning to justify the mega spending:

The evidence suggests that, contrary to the consensus view, the return on 
investment on AI capex is showing up. This development, along with broader 
earnings strength, should provide a green light for the bull market over the 
rest of the year.

Semiconductor groups, meanwhile, are more concerned about what happens when 
that spending eventually slows. Chipmakers have been the biggest beneficiaries 
of the AI buildout, with earnings and free cash flow rising speedily and in 
line with each other. The sector has always been cyclical, which makes it 
difficult to know how much longer the upswing can last. Any reversal could be 
substantial, depending on whether tech groups decided to pull back on their 
investments:

Even if earnings are broadening, Apollo Global Management’s Torsten Slok 
points out that non-tech companies’ profit margins show little evidence that 
they’ve yet managed to use AI to make themselves more profitable:

Societe Generale’s Andrew Lapthorne offers another illustration of this. 
Nearly 90% of S&P 500 companies reported increased sales. The number improving 
their margins, however, was barely half that:
Source: Societe Generale 
The longer it takes the S&P 493 to generate ROI, Slok points out, the bigger 
the downside risks to an economy and a market this concentrated in the AI trade.

Still, the way the market has handled these earnings is in one way 
exceptionally encouraging. Earnings multiples are falling. Over history, a 
rerating like this is almost always achieved by prices falling. This time 
around, earnings have grown to justify the previous generous multiples:

If there’s reason for worry, it’s that companies are “all in”; they’ve spent 
their cash to generate a remarkable profits growth cycle, and in the process 
made themselves more vulnerable to a subsequent downswing if they run into 
funding difficulties.

The most encouraging sign for the market is that while the AI boom is getting 
bigger, it’s no longer doing all the heavy lifting. Earnings growth spreading 
beyond the technology giants will ultimately widen the market's profit base and 
reduce dependence on a handful of companies. The AI trade may yet turn earnings 
growth into a story bigger than the technology itself.

—Richard Abbey


A Yen for Intervention

A full week has passed since Japan’s Ministry of Finance joined with the US 
Treasury to buy yen in an attempt to shock the currency higher. The results 
were dramatically effective when the waves of purchases were made, on July 30 
and 31, but there was immediate skepticism that they would have lasting impact 
without follow-through in tighter monetary and fiscal policy.

At present, that skepticism begins to look justified. The yen opened the week 
by steadily depreciating through Monday trading, and is now close to crossing 
160 against the dollar again, once regarded as a line in the sand:

It looks like the authorities have had their bluff called. If the yen weakens 
past 160, the assumption will be no more interventions. But will they really 
give up without a fight?


Straits of No Hope

If no ships pass through a strait, but there’s nobody there to watch, does it 
really matter? Prospects of a resumption of traffic through the Strait of 
Hormuz have taken another reversal, with President Donald Trump now saying that 
compensation by Iran will be essential to any agreement. It is vanishingly 
unlikely that the Iranians will accept this this.

The president also said that the Strait is already open. Bloomberg’s monitor 
of tanker traffic suggests very few ships are taking the opportunity. Oil 
prices are rising again, with gasoline in the US still costing on average more 
than $4 per gallon:

This had little obvious impact on Wall Street, where the S&P 500 closed Monday 
virtually flat (down 0.06%). That appears to be because companies have adjusted 
to assume that Hormuz will be disrupted for the foreseeable future. A majority 
of executives questioned by Oxford Economics expect this to continue throughout 
next year, while almost a fifth think it will drag on at least another year 
after that:

Companies and supply chains will find ways to adapt, or maybe they’ll put up 
with paying tolls to Iran and Oman. It’s not great, but at least in market 
perceptions, the fate of the waterway is no longer as critical as it was. And 
as this relieves pressure on all sides to reach a deal, that in itself makes a 
settlement less likely.
