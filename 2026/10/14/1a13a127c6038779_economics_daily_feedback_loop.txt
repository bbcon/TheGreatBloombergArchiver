Feedback Loop

The International Monetary Fund has been hammering all week on the twin risks 
of outsize debts andsoaring bond yields 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/surging-bond-yields-are-top-risk-to-financial-system-imf-says?cmpid=BBD101426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10057>
. Today it described how they could combine into a feedback loop, and trap the 
world’s most indebted governments.

High debt makes government finances more vulnerable to rising interest rates, 
while investors are also becoming more sensitive to fiscal risks, Era 
Dabla-Norris and Rodrigo Valdés wrote in anIMF blog post 
<https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2026/10/13/rising-sovereign-yields-raise-the-fiscal-stakes>
 published Wednesday.

That can create a cycle in which higher rates make the debt burden heavier — 
prompting markets to demand evenhigher yields 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/treasuries-gain-on-haven-demand-as-trading-resumes-after-holiday?cmpid=BBD101426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10057>
.

The danger is acute right now, with global public debt projected to exceed 
100% of gross domestic product by 2030. Sticky inflation led by energy prices 
could make things worse, because monetary policy will likely have to tighten up 
to contain it.

Central banks may need to keep rates higher for longer 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/ecb-will-probably-have-to-hike-interest-rates-again-wunsch-says?cmpid=BBD101426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10057>
, worsening debt dynamics, while fiscal concerns can themselves push market 
rates higher, the IMF said.

One way this is all playing out in several advanced economies is that 
governments are refinancing more debt at shorter maturities. That means when 
central banks hike rates, it passes through to sovereign borrowing costs more 
quickly.

“High debt and shorter maturities can therefore make restoring price stability 
more difficult and more costly for the public finances,” the IMF said.

These pressures are already showing up in government budgets. The effective 
average interest cost of public borrowing globally is projected to increase to 
3.7% by 2031, from 2.3% in 2021.

In the US, interest payments are forecast to reach 5.7% of GDP in 2031. That 
would roughly match projectedbudget spending 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-11/us-2026-budget-gap-hits-1-96-trillion-with-a-month-left-to-go?cmpid=BBD101426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10057>
 on Social Security, the biggest line item in the national budget.

Not for the first time, the fund has this week been urging the debt-laden 
economies of the developed world to start getting their house in order. In 
IMF-speak, they need credible medium-term fiscal plans.

Delaying the adjustment will make things worse, the IMF says. Because of the 
feedback-loop dynamics described above, interest burdens and fiscal 
vulnerabilities will only build – making the ultimate fix even more painful.

Need-to-Know Research

Many expect the diffusion of artificial intelligence will stoke productivity. 
Perhaps few are as bullish on this prospect as the Japan Center for Economic 
Research, whose president recently unveiled projections for mammoth increases 
in major economies in the 2030s.

Total factor productivity — that’s the added output per working hour that 
comes not from increasing machinery or removing people, but from learning how 
to do things better — will see added gains of about 2 percentage points a year 
for China and the US, and 1.5 points for Europe and Japan, during the 2030s, 
Kazumasa Iwata wrote in arecent note 
<https://www.jcer.or.jp/english/financial-policy-to-prepare-for-alien-ai>.

Productivity is one thing. How about economic growth 
<https://www.jcer.or.jp/jcer_download_log.php?f=eyJwb3N0X2lkIjoxMjkxMTIsImZpbGVfcG9zdF9pZCI6MTI5MTk3fQ==&post_id=129112&file_post_id=129197>
 itself? Iwata highlights two scenarios cited for the US, where one sees GDP 
gains continuing around 2% and another where AI’s impact is remarkable enough 
to take growth up to some 10%. Ultimately, the answer will be “somewhere in 
between.” It will depend on the size of the business areas “where AI 
application is lagging and the assessment of how much those areas will impact 
the overall economy.”
