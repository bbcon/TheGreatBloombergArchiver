Whither the Trump Trade

The greatest waves are forced by the confluence of contradictory factors. At 
theCortes Bank <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lz97moE41Xo&t=20s>, 100 miles 
off California, an underwater island beneath where tides meet combines to 
create massive pillars of surf for adrenaline junkies to ride. Similar factors 
create monster waves off Tahiti, orNazare 
<https://www.facebook.com/bigwavesnazare/videos/nazar%C3%A9-portugal-the-biggest-waves-in-the-world-nazare-bigwaves-bigwavesnazare-wa/1455352983191576/>
 in Portugal.

In markets, contradictory factors also create the greatest waves — and unlike 
ocean waves, the adrenaline addicts surfing them can affect how high they rise. 
As big-wave pioneerBuzzy Trent 
<https://www.eos.surf/videos/video-buzzy-trent-filmed-by-bud-browne> once said: 
“Waves are not measured in feet and inches, they are measured in increments of 
fear.”

At present, we have the separate but related bullish trade in artificial 
intelligence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-25/ai-spending-fears-threaten-to-swamp-big-tech-s-strong-earnings?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
, now suffering an intense bout of skepticism, and a bearish trend in bonds. 
The two affect each other, with booming AI heightening the case for interest 
rates to rise, while prohibitive long-term rates prompt doubts about AI groups’ 
access to financing.

The result, viewed by the performance of the exchange-traded funds tracking 
the Nasdaq 100 and Treasuries of 20 years or longer, has been a spectacular 
wave that crested in the last month. This isn’t like the self-inflicted 
selloffs of Trump 2.0 after last year’s Liberation Day tariffs, and then the 
attack on Iran. But for now, the grand trend of rising tech stocks and falling 
bonds is intact:

Can this continue? Much depends on the financials of the companies at the 
center of AI 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-25/ai-spending-fears-threaten-to-swamp-big-tech-s-strong-earnings?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
. Results from Alphabet, Amazon.com and Microsoft this week could restore 
confidence, as could a successfulIPO for Anthropic 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-23/anthropic-ipo-why-a-2-trillion-valuation-faces-major-risks?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
 PBC, makers of Claude, next month. Bad outcomes would have the opposite effect.

Beneath this wave, oil is the undertow 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRVfAzE9lBs>. Next month, once US and Israeli 
elections are over, hostilities in the Gulf could re-escalate. That would raise 
the risk of permanent damage to production facilities, and thus an ongoing hit 
to supply, and that is showing up in renewed bets that higher oil prices are 
going to be around for a long time.

This chart shows the rolling contract for the price of Brent 12 months 
forward, which hasn’t been this high since the immediate aftermath of Russia’s 
2022 invasion of Ukraine, and the contract for November 2027, which has never 
been this high. Optimism that the war and the interruption to supply that it 
causes will soon be over has drained away:

If oil keeps rising like this, it will attack the presumptions of growth, and 
possibly accelerate the re-found tendency to use Treasuries as a haven in times 
of peril — and bring the AI/bonds wave crashing down. It will also create a 
dilemma for the Federal Reserve...

The Uses and Abuses of Purdah

The UK’s budget is due on Wednesday 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-23/healey-s-first-budget-to-push-uk-s-hard-choices-to-another-day?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
. It used to come shrouded in “Purdah” — thetraditional Indian word 
<https://www.britannica.com/topic/purdah> for secluding women behind veils. 
Nobody on the chancellor’s team was supposed to breathe a word to anyone in the 
weeks before it was unveiled to the House of Commons.

Like Purdah itself, that concept is taken less seriously now, but the Fed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-23/federal-reserve-interest-rate-decision-faces-challenges-from-ai-inflation-war?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
 adheres to its own version. Leading US central bank officials go silent in 
public for the last week and a half before each Federal Open Market Committee 
meeting. Under the new regime of Kevin Warsh, who is keen to ditch “forward 
guidance” to markets about the Fed’s intentions, that shouldn’t make much 
difference. But the period from the last meeting until the Fed’s Purdah started 
10 days ago was nothing like that. This is how the chance of an October rate 
hike moved over that period, as gauged by the Bloomberg World Interest Rate 
Probability function:

There is a repeating pattern. New data, notably September consumer price 
inflation that showed a disquieting rise across the board, prompted traders to 
mark up the odds on a hike. Then a speech by a Fedster would bring those odds 
right back down again.

John Williams of the New York Fed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/fed-s-williams-interview-with-bloomberg-news-full-transcript?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
 talked down the sense that there was urgency to hike soon.Warsh himself 
suggested 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-16/warsh-boosts-market-expectations-of-fed-hold-in-october?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
 that he was in no hurry in a speech to the International Monetary Fund on the 
last day before Purdah. Politics add to the reasons to hold, as the Fed will 
want to avoid controversy a week before the midterms.

It looks like Warsh has already joined the ranks of Fed chairmen swiftly 
forced into retreat by markets once they take over. Instead of forward 
guidance, championed most famouslyby Ben Bernanke 
<https://www.econlib.org/bernanke-on-forward-guidance/>, he favors a kind of 
random walk in response to data. Afteralarmed market reactions to his first 
press conferences 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-07-30/warsh-needs-to-do-better-than-i-won-t-tell-you?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
, it looks mightily as though he’s given up on this.

Meanwhile, something is moving rate expectations 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-25/warsh-tells-traders-to-watch-the-data-they-still-follow-the-fed?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
 beyond Fedspeak or the data: the bond market. Rising long yields are a 
problem, and are perceived to force the central bank to demonstrate itsbona 
fides by clamping down in the present. Of late, the 10-year yield and the 
market forecast for fed funds a year hence, have moved in alignment:

But what is the direction of causation? Gavekal’s Anatole Kaletsky 
<https://research.gavekal.com/author/anatole-kaletsky/> suggests that the Fed 
is driving the bond market, for two reasons:
The first is that many bond investors are so confident about the Fed’s ability 
and willingness to hit its inflation target that they dismiss short-term 
fluctuations in data on inflation, fiscal policy, GDP and so on as irrelevant 
noise... A second reason... is the changing structure of the bond market. Bond 
trading today is dominated by hedge funds, algos and other short-term 
trend-followers for whom forecasts of inflation, or GDP or fiscal conditions 10 
years ahead, or even one year ahead, are entirely irrelevant except insofar as 
they may influence the Fed’s decisions on short-term policy rates.
If Kaletsky is right, and rising yields continue to alarm the administration 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/bessent-says-buyback-move-was-aimed-at-quelling-market-fever?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
, then the pressure on Warsh to guide the market, whether veiled by Purdah or 
not, will only intensify.

A Biotech Supplement

Some sectors, as Apollo’s Torsten Slok has pointed out 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-25/ai-provides-zero-payoff-to-s-p-493-outside-tech-apollo-economist-says?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
, have missed out completely from the benefits of artificial intelligence. 
Biotechnology is not one of them. Investors’ bet that AI will boost 
productivity is especially evident in drug discovery, where the technology is 
accelerating research and lowering development costs. The offshoots for biotech 
stocks, though not completely immune from the broader AI market selloff of 
recent weeks, are encouraging:

Also, look at dealmaking. In a market increasingly hostile to deals beyond 
mega-cap tech, five transactions this month reiterated investors’ appetite for 
biotech. Bloomberg Intelligence’s Jennie Li says this reflects a broader search 
to combine defensive earnings with innovation-driven growth. Decades ago, 
healthcare’s investment case rested largely on blockbuster drugs and stable 
cash flows:
Today, GLP-1 therapies, gene editing, AI-powered drug discovery, smarter 
clinical trials, antibody platforms, radiopharmaceuticals, advanced 
diagnostics, robotic surgery, and precision medicine are reshaping that case.
These have made healthcare a big AI beneficiary, and biotech deals show 
excitement about its growth potential:

Healthcare stocks are up about 5% over the past month, even as the AI tech 
stocks sold off. This resilience has rewarded investors in companies like Eli 
Lilly, reinforcing the appeal of businesses that pair resilient earnings with 
innovation in areas such as obesity, immunology and oncology:

Meanwhile, Washington is pushing to restrict US investment 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-22/us-proposes-curbs-on-investment-in-chinese-biotech-firms?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
 in Chinese biotech. The scrutiny by the Treasury is rooted in the 
Comprehensive Outbound Investment National Security Act, enacted last year, 
which provides a basis for restricting US investments in countries of concern — 
most importantly China.

Both administrative action and legislative pressure lie behind the move, which 
raises the risks for drugmakers involved with China. Bloomberg Intelligence’s 
Duane Wright said:
Though the law didn’t explicitly cover biotechnology, Treasury’s proposed 
implementing rule would add the sector and prohibit covered investments 
involving a broad range of pharmaceutical technologies, including small 
molecules, biologics and cell and gene therapies.
With the Treasury seeking feedback 
<https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0667>, the final rule might be 
scaled back. For now, the proposal targets joint ventures and similar 
transactions rather than instantly prohibiting conventional pharma licensing 
deals, limiting the immediate risk to drugmakers sourcing Chinese assets, 
Wright says.

But the proposal adds to the regulatory uncertainty hanging over the biotech 
sector for investors, and weighs on earnings potential. Any additional clarity 
in the final rule, coupled with the sector’s outperformance, could provide an 
additional catalyst for earnings:

The strength of any such recovery remains uncertain. Research and development 
costs, which temper future EPS growth, patent cliffs, and US drug pricing 
reforms all cast doubt on revenue projections — but potential efficiency gains 
from AI should at least offset higher R&D costs.

Additionally, AI research and drug discovery should help offset some of the 
revenue lost to expiring patents, with an estimated $290 billion to $350 
billion in global branded-drug revenue at risk of losing exclusivity between 
2025 and 2030 — roughly one-sixth of annual industry revenue.

—Richard Abbey

Survival Tips

So, I saw the Elizabeth Holmes movie You Can See Everything 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/nathan-fielder-movie-you-can-see-everything-businessweek-review?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
 this weekend; it’s a bizarre, unsettling and immersive experience, and there’s 
good reason why everyone is talking about it. It won’t tell you much about the
Theranos fraud <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIqF95qiQSs> itself. For that 
you should read John Carreyrou’sBad Blood 
<https://www.amazon.com/dp/152473165X?lv=shuf&channelId=500&plpRedirect=mhFallback>
, a gripping tale of how a journalist rumbled what was going on.

Instead, it’s a take on metaphysics and epistemology — what do we really know 
about anyone, and what is the truth anyway? Could Holmes really be a 
self-deluded dreamer, manipulated by the men in her life, who genuinely doesn’t 
understand that what she’s saying isn’t true — or is she a brilliant scammer 
who keeps up her hypnotic act right up to the moment she goes to jail? And must 
we all prejudice the truth whenever we construct a narrative? For evidence that 
Holmes’ convoluted language always showed she was hiding the truth, trythis 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2021-09-17/complex-language-on-earnings-calls-is-a-warning-to-investors?cmpid=BBD102626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10550>
.

The movie is maybe 20 minutes too long, but worth your time. Seeing it in a 
theater, and hearing the gasps in response to revelations, adds to the 
experience. Have a good week everyone.
