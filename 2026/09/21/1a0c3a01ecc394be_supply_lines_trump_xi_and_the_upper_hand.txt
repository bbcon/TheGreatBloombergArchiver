Here’s a prediction going into this week’s Washington summit between Xi 
Jinping and Donald Trump: Both leaders will enter the room convinced they have 
a stronger hand than they really do.

Expectations of actual meaningful outcomes from Thursday’s day of pageantry 
are low. But both sides’ belief in their own strength could be the biggest 
factor in determining where things go next. Because if we’ve learned anything 
from the past decade of trade wars it’s that overconfidence can eventually 
backfire spectacularly in an economic conflict.

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Sunday that his meeting with his 
Chinese counterpart, Vice Premier He Lifeng, had yielded an agreement to create 
abilateral dialogue 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-21/bessent-hails-very-successful-china-talks-on-ai-threats-trade?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>
 to discuss AI issues. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer added that the 
two sides had “operationalized” a board of trade. Working talks are due to 
continue on Monday.

That means that by the time the week is done, the expectation is the two sides 
will have agreed to talk more about the risks presented by AI, possiblytweaked 
some tariffs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/china-and-us-discuss-cutting-tariffs-on-agriculture-and-energy?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>
, and discussed at least extending a trade truce.

All would be good results for the global economy. Its two largest players are 
talking and seem to want to avoid full-blown conflict. Yet it also seems clear 
that neither side is fully embracing peace and that seems to be a function of 
how both sides may be misreading their own standing.
“Both sides want to avoid another flare-up and relative stability in the 
relationship is likely to continue,” Nicole Gorton-Caratelli, Adam Farrar and 
Maeva Cousin of Bloomberg Economics wrote in aresearch note <>.
Xi is coming to Washington as the leader of what to much of the rest of the 
world is a daunting industrial powerhouse. China, as it made clear last year, 
also controls vital industrial inputs like rare earth magnets without which 
many of the world’s factories would grind to a halt.

Trump leads the world’s largest economy and, for now, its lone military 
superpower. He has successfully managed to raise a tariff wall around the US 
and proven his willingness to employ economic tools none of his predecessors 
would as he finds new ways to assert American power.

Domestic Headwinds

Yet both face problems at home and abroad that are hard to ignore.

Xi’s export-driven model is increasingly meeting resistance 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-10/europe-is-trying-to-gauge-how-much-pain-it-can-take-from-china?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>
 in major destination economies with the European Union leading that pack.

The threat of a new China Shock much like the one that contributed to the 
collapse of factory towns in the US in the 1990s and early 2000s is causing 
governments that once embraced trade to at least consider rolling out American 
style protectionism.

Which is understandable when Germany’s auto industry has been announcing mass 
layoffs in theface of competition 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/vw-board-gives-ceo-blume-unanimous-backing-to-overhaul-carmaker?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>
 from new Chinese EVs. Or if you are digesting recent election results in 
Germany and thepotential collapse 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-20/merz-s-cdu-posts-worst-state-result-in-postwar-german-history?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>
 of Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s government.

China’s domestic economy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/china-s-consumption-and-investment-wilt-as-factories-power-ahead?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>
, meanwhile, isn’t exactly humming. Consumer spending and investment were down 
in August. So too was fiscal spending even as plenty of economists are calling 
formore stimulus 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-18/china-s-fiscal-pullback-stretches-into-august-as-spending-slumps?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>
 rather than less.

Trump, meanwhile, is finding that the world is increasingly less prone to bend 
to his will whether it is in Canada or Iran.

In the US, Trump’s approval rating sits near historic lows ahead with a 
midterm election just weeks away that seems likely to hand control of at least 
one house of Congress to opposition Democrats. All as voters aren’t convinced 
he’s delivering the economic miracle he promised andtaking a dim view 
<https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/09/18/most-michigan-voters-disapprove-trumps-tariffs-canada-poll-shows/>
 of new initiatives like tariffs on Canadian imports.

Diesel Prices

Trump’s biggest domestic problem is that the stubborn and unpopular inflation 
caused by both his tariffs and the economic fallout from the war he launched in 
Iran isn’t going away. And the ways it is manifesting itself are metastasizing.

The Federal Reserve chairman he explicitly appointed to lower interest rates 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-17/trump-denied-rate-cut-as-federal-reserve-targets-war-stoked-inflation?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>
 just oversaw the first hike in rates instead with inflation the main target. 
Farmersare grousing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-16/midterm-elections-rising-us-diesel-heating-oil-prices-hit-voters-in-key-states?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>
 about high diesel and fertilizer prices and also angry about his plan to 
import beef to lower prices. Restaurants are reworking hamburger recipes to use 
less meat because of thehigh cost 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-18/restaurants-serving-burgers-steaks-adjust-menu-offerings-with-high-beef-prices?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>
 of the core ingredient.

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-21/us-diesel-tops-6-50-a-gallon-as-wars-worsen-global-fuels-crunch?cmpid=BBD092126_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260921&utm_content=9114>

Meanwhile, the AI investment boom that’s fueled much of the growth in the 
economy is prompting both voter revolts and fears about the unacceptably high 
odds its own creators are putting on the potential for it to cause the 
Apocalypse.

Both leaders also face a common problem. Their main source of strength — the 
ability to weaponize the dependence each economy has on the other — faces an 
expiry date. As Paul Krugmantold 
<https://paulkrugman.substack.com/p/economic-size-and-economic-power> a 
European Central Bank conference last week: “Everybody has market power, 
although it’s a wasting asset if you try to use it.”

Because countries eventually adapt and find alternatives. And no matter how 
powerful, no country is immune to economic consequences. No one ever has the 
upper hand for very long. Which is something Xi and Trump are starting to 
confront.
