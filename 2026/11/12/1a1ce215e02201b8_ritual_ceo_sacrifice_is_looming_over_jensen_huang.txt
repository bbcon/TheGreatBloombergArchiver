The Honey-Trap Discount

The revelations keep coming, and they make it ever clearer that Nvidia Corp. 
had badly underestimated thekey-man risk 
<https://www.linkedin.com/top-content/business-strategy/understanding-business-risks/how-to-assess-key-man-risk-in-business/>
 it was taking in the person of its hugely powerful and respected CEO Jensen 
Huang. It’s possible, however, that the market is overstating quite how much 
peril is rolled up in him.

A bigger and more difficult question is whether Huang was also a key man for 
the entireartificial intelligence industry 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-08/nvidia-board-to-probe-spying-case-after-ceo-jensen-huang-admits-infidelity?cmpid=BBD111226_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11301>
. So far, the bet seems to be that he isn’t. This is what’s happened to shares 
in Nvidia and the main exchange-traded fund tracking the Nasdaq 100 since the 
start of trading last Friday:

After the weekend’s extraordinary revelation of a relationship with an alleged 
Chinese spy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/alice-huang-suspected-china-spy-spent-years-in-nvidia-s-orbit?cmpid=BBD111226_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11301>
 (who has now reappeared inBeijing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-12/alleged-chinese-spy-at-center-of-nvidia-firestorm-is-in-beijing?cmpid=BBD111226_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11301>
), the news that he also had an affair withKimberly Powell 
<https://www.ft.com/content/d82f357a-89f6-41e4-930c-00b52a2a5df9?syn-25a6b1a6=1>
, who heads Nvidia’s efforts to introduce AI to healthcare and reports directly 
to him, might well have been the final straw. It’s hard to see how any 
executive could survive after transgressions like this.

At the time of writing, Nvidia’s board was meeting 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-11/nvidia-working-with-outside-firm-to-identify-ceo-candidates?cmpid=BBD111226_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11301>
 andappeared highly likely to dismiss him 
<https://www.wsj.com/business/nvidia-is-preparing-to-oust-nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-30f421ad>
.

Huang had been 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-11-10/jensen-huang-affair-nvidia-ceo-should-have-known-better?cmpid=BBD111226_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11301>
 a hugely successful leader. However, with no succession plan in place, all 
this is bad news and reason to sell off. But even before the revelations of the 
second affair, traders had taken down Nvidia’s valuation to the point where its 
forward earnings multiple, on a blended 12-month basis, was only 66% that of 
the S&P 500 as a whole. Three years ago, it was more than 200% more expensive 
than the market:

The slide predates the scandal, but still seems remarkable. Nvidia’s 
prodigious earnings, combined with mediocre stock market performance by the 
other members of tech’s Magnificent Seven, mean that it hasn’t lost that much 
ground. Nvidia surrendered its position as the largest company by market cap to 
Apple Inc. last month; it’s still bigger than any of the other Magnificents:

What’s perhaps most surprising is how bland all of their performances have 
been. There’s much talk of a bubble, and fear that the market has grown 
overconcentrated, but none of the Magnificents have looked like either an 
inflating or a bursting bubble over the last 12 hectic months. Broadly, they’ve 
gone sideways.

And despite all the heavy trading and swings, the overall index remains 
extremely narrow. Concentration has reduced since the S&P 500 topped five weeks 
ago, but not by that much. On Oct. 6, the top 10 stocks accounted for 40.8% of 
the S&P 500. That’s now down to 38.7% — still historically high. (Note that 
both numbers might slightly understate the issue because the S&P 500 does not 
yet include SpaceX, which would be among the top 10.)

So it “feels” like a bubble is bursting, and stocks have reshuffled manically 
all year, but the overall result looks nothing like that. The reason may lie in 
the nature of investment in AI. Speculative bubbles are inevitable during the 
adoption of any radical new technology where gains are far in the future and 
uncertain. From railroads to the internet, past great innovations have 
transformed the economy after losing some people’s shirts along the way.

What’s different about AI is what Viktor Shvets of Macquarie calls a “rolling 
bubble”:
There is no way to hedge the “AI trade,” as it is everywhere. Instead, 
embracing AI is a better way forward: diversify across a wide range of themes 
shaped by AI... identify new “bubbles” while discarding past winners that are 
likely to derate rapidly; broaden exposures outside indices, both listed and 
private. Our world is not one of a single AI bubble but multiple rolling 
bubbles.
New research from the index provider MSCI shows how this process has worked 
out. They divided the AI phenomenon into a series of sectors that would benefit 
in different ways. Most recently, physical infrastructure (chips and 
data-center providers, shown in turquoise on the chart) formed what looks like 
a classic bubble that now appears to be bursting. Previously, the lead belonged 
to digital infrastructure:

The veteran investor Jim Paulsen suggests that there has been a series of 
“Busted Bulls.” It’s a strange phenomenon with few analogues, and it’s still 
unclear whether this is fundamentally negative:
Is a bull market which chronically suffers from “Busted Bulls” one which is 
constantly refreshing itself allowing the bull market to continue as new 
bullish themes emerge? Or, as each bull theme cracks, is the overall bull 
market weakened and made more vulnerable increasing the possibility that a bear 
market does eventually conquer? Maybe the answer is neither?
If this is truly a rolling bubble, then maybe the instant judgment that the 
Huang scandal will do terrible damage to Nvidia without hurting the market 
could make sense. But we can expect it to be tested — and if this story doesn’t 
force everyone to take corporate governance more seriously when they invest, 
then nothing will.

Silver Anniversary for China and the WTO

China’s admission to the World Trade Organization traces back to 1972, when 
then-US PresidentRichard Nixon flew to Beijing 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cqKZIzZPLgg> to meet Mao Zedong, setting in 
motion a historic rapprochement 
<https://history.state.gov/milestones/1969-1976/rapprochement-china>. The 
gradual normalization was punctuated by disputes over trade practices and human 
rights abuses. The key moment came almost three decades later on Nov. 10, 2001, 
when the WTO’s trade ministers approvedChina’s accession at a Doha conference 
<https://ustr.gov/archive/Document_Library/Fact_Sheets/2001/Background_Information_on_China's_Accession_to_the_World_Trade_Organization.html>
. Beijing became the organization’s 143rd member a month later.

The significance in the aftermath of the 9/11 terrorist attacks 
<https://www.911memorial.org/911-faqs> was missed at the time, and it’s now 
overshadowed by President Donald Trump’ssweeping efforts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2025-04-03/liberation-day-tariffs-leave-us-economy-adrift?cmpid=BBD111226_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11301>
 to reshape global trade and erode the WTO’s influence. But even though China’s 
trade surplus with the US has been under pressure since Trump began a tariff 
war between the countries in 2018, its surplus with the rest of the world 
continues to reach new highs:

China’s stock market, however, is a different story. Notoriously volatile, it 
has had three separate bubbles in the last quarter-century, in 2007, 2015 and 
2021. All burst, largely because of crude government interventions — but only 
in the last year did it fall behind emerging markets and the world as a whole:

What is going on? The trajectory of China’s trade surplus 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/chinese-trade-surplus-surpasses-1-trillion-for-second-time-ever?cmpid=BBD111226_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11301>
 is unsettling for many on the receiving end. Opposition to Chinese imports is 
growing, but it’s unclear whether this can derail the export growth on which 
Beijing now relies as it attempts to fix the imploded property market. Last 
month,exports 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-10/china-trade-surplus?cmpid=BBD111226_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11301>
 surged by more than 35% year-on-year, underpinned by technology shipments. The 
trend is upward:

Concerns over sustaining this growth are harder to ignore as producer 
inflation accelerated 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/chinese-producer-inflation-soars-to-5-7-for-post-covid-era-high?cmpid=BBD111226_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261112&utm_content=11301>
 to its highest since 2022. Prices at the factory gate climbed 5.7% in October 
from a year earlier after a gain of 5% in September, according to the National 
Bureau of Statistics:

Surging producer prices contrast with subdued consumer inflation, which 
slipped more than forecast to 0.7% in October. Macquarie’s Shvets attributes 
this divergence to persistent and growing overcapacity, which in turn results 
in disinflationary pressures and lower returns. This limits the possibilities 
to drive growth domestically:
Outside of computers, capacity utilization rates are falling across the board. 
This is especially true of the automotive sector (66% vs 81% in 2018), but is 
also visible across most industrial pursuits (e.g. chemicals, coal, electronic 
equipment). Inevitably, this leads to high unemployment, especially among 
younger cohorts.
The energy shock for Chinese manufacturers is driving costs higher elsewhere. 
Unless business owners can absorb some of the rising costs, higher inflation 
will weigh on global demand for Chinese imports. It’s hard to gauge how 
meaningful this will be, but October’s exports, which fell below estimates, 
could signal that it’s already happening.

Beyond price-induced pressures, Macquarie’s Larry Hu and Yuxiao Zhang argue 
that export growth will slow down. The extent will be shaped largely by the 
tech cycle, which will in turn determine how much domestic stimulus Beijing 
needs to provide to meet the GDP target. Whatever new mix results might just 
revive Chinese equities, which have now given up almost all of their gains 
since the underwhelming policy pivot in late 2024:

China’s balancing act is difficult, but not impossible. WTO accession unlocked 
an era of export-led growth, but that has provoked a reaction and stock markets 
are running out of patience. The challenge now is to find a new source of 
momentum at home, before limits on the old model are imposed from abroad.

—Richard Abbey
