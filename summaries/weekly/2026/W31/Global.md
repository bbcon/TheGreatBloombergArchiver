**Oil Markets and the Strait of Hormuz**
Monday's ceasefire-driven oil decline proved short-lived. Wednesday's resumption of US strikes on Iranian military targets following Iranian ballistic missile attacks on a US base in Jordan pushed Brent back above $91. The conflict's impact on energy markets is best understood as disruption and dislocation rather than simple supply destruction: rerouted cargoes are widening regional price spreads, Goldman Sachs identifies diesel as the supply squeeze epicenter, and energy majors with diversified trading and refining capabilities are capturing dislocation value — Shell more than doubled quarterly profit despite losing a third of gas production. Washington has ruled out further Strategic Petroleum Reserve releases beyond the 172 million barrels announced in March, with the SPR at its lowest since the early 1980s. Bloomberg New Energy Finance's base case for European gas storage assumes Hormuz reopening by mid-September — a scenario that cannot be treated as given.

**Monetary Policy Convergence and Divergence**
The simultaneous hawkish lean across the Fed, ECB, Bank of England, and Bank of Japan — each confronting energy-driven inflation from different starting points — represents a notable moment of G7 policy convergence. Japan's BOJ held at 1% Friday but signaled every future meeting is live; the ECB is pricing at least one more hike; the Bank of England split its vote. The common thread is oil: each institution must weigh supply-shock inflation against slowing growth, a tension that structural interest rate divergence complicates for currency markets. The Rhine River approaching a 36-year low adds a fourth simultaneous logistics stress point alongside the Strait of Hormuz, Red Sea, and Panama Canal.

**Geopolitics and AI Competition**
Iran has launched approximately 2,000 ballistic missiles since February, eroding interceptor stockpiles and demonstrating asymmetric offense-versus-defense cost dynamics that are reshaping defense procurement globally. NATO committed $50 billion toward long-range strike capabilities, with six European nations, South Korea, Japan, and the US all expanding ballistic missile programs — a structural shift in defense spending with multi-decade fiscal implications. Separately, China's release of a new large-scale AI model and CXMT's landmark Shanghai debut underscore that American export controls and Chinese technological advancement are proceeding in an escalating cycle. The US-China technology competition, not exchange rate coordination, appears to be Washington's primary bilateral framing ahead of a planned September Trump-Xi summit.
