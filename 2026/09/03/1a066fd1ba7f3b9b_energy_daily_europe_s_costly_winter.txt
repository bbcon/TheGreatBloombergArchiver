Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Priscila Azevedo Rocha looks at Europe’s winter energy risks.

European policymakers have good reason to worry about a tough winter: With 
just weeks to go,energy prices are soaring 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-02/energy-prices-surge-as-politicians-sound-alarm-ahead-of-winter?cmpid=BBBXT090326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8488>
 and the region is ill-prepared for demand spikes.

One of the clearest signs of Europe’s vulnerability is its gas storage 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/europe-s-gas-gamble-risks-higher-prices-in-global-supply-fight?cmpid=BBBXT090326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8488>
. Inventories are at their lowest for this time of year in data going back to 
2009, leaving a thin winter cushion. Those reserves typically meet about a 
third of demand during colder months, providing extra fuel when consumption 
surges.

Rebuilding that buffer is only getting harder. As the Iran war disrupts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/-we-are-stalled-us-iran-conflict-stuck-with-no-end-in-sight?cmpid=BBBXT090326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8488>
 global supplies, Europe will have to compete more aggressively with buyers 
elsewhere for liquefied natural gas, meaning prices may need to stay elevated 
to attract cargoes.

They’ve already surged to a three-year high 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/european-gas-futures-rise-to-highest-since-2023-on-iran-war?cmpid=BBBXT090326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8488>
 this week after a new wave of fighting in the Middle East, raising the 
prospect that poorer countries will be priced out.

The first signs of fallout are emerging in Pakistan, which scrapped an 
emergency LNG tender 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-02/pakistan-faces-further-blackouts-after-rejecting-pricey-lng?cmpid=BBBXT090326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8488>
 after receiving only one very expensive offer. Without immediate supply, the 
nation may need to continue rolling blackouts.

Bangladesh, meanwhile, paid the most for LNG 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/bangladesh-pays-most-for-gas-since-2022-to-ease-power-blackouts?cmpid=BBBXT090326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8488>
 in about four years as it scrambles to contain power disruptions.

In Europe, the situation is particularly acute in Germany, where tanks are 
just over half full. Italy and others have made more progress, while some 
officials in Berlin saythere’s still time <bbg://news/stories/TKS2ZWT96OSG> to 
fill storages. But Germany’s sheer size means a late dash for fuel could shake 
the region, driving up prices and internal competition.

There’s been little incentive to act sooner, with summer gas more expensive 
than winter contracts and many observers initially expecting the Iran war to 
conclude sooner.

And gas isn’t the only pressure point. Diesel prices in Europe 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/europe-s-diesel-crunch-spurs-rare-12-000-mile-journey-from-korea?cmpid=BBBXT090326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8488>
 are climbing much faster than crude as Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries 
and the Iran war squeeze global supplies.

All of that’s making inflation concerns 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-02/energy-prices-surge-as-politicians-sound-alarm-ahead-of-winter?cmpid=BBBXT090326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8488>
 hard for policymakers to ignore and forcing many of the world’s most important 
central banks to contemplate hiking interest rates.

The lesson for Europe may be that waiting comes with a high price. What looks 
manageable today can quickly become harder and much more expensive to fix once 
winter demand kicks in.

—Priscila Azevedo Rocha, Bloomberg News

Chart of the day

A cargo of South Korean diesel is heading to western Europe this month in a 
rare trade 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/europe-s-diesel-crunch-spurs-rare-12-000-mile-journey-from-korea?cmpid=BBBXT090326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8488>
, underscoring efforts to shore up supplies ahead of the Northern Hemisphere 
winter. Europe has limited refining capacity and is heavily reliant on diesel 
imports. South Korea typically exports fuel to countries in the Asia-Pacific 
region, but much higher prices for diesel in Europe help explain a voyage that 
would typically make little economic sense.
