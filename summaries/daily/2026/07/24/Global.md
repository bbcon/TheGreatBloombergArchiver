**Energy and Middle East Escalation**
The partial closure of the Strait of Hormuz following renewed hostilities has produced a sustained oil price shock that is now embedding itself in global financial conditions. Brent crude near $100 per barrel is simultaneously a supply disruption signal and an inflation input, complicating the task of central banks across developed and emerging markets. Trump's threat of a "massive attack" on Iran — described as larger than any previous strike — introduces a scenario tail that markets are beginning to price but cannot yet fully assess. The Strait carries approximately 20% of global seaborne oil trade; a further escalation that extends or deepens its effective closure would represent a material supply shock for which there is limited short-term substitute routing. Gulf sovereign bond issuance at record levels partly reflects the capital required to build bypass infrastructure, a signal that regional governments do not expect rapid normalization.

**Monetary Policy Divergence**
The global rates picture is growing more complex. The ECB is privately leaning toward a September hike driven by energy-price inflation. The Bank of Japan is on track for further normalization. The Federal Reserve faces a market now pricing a one-in-three probability of a hike next week, while its economist consensus still points to the next move being a cut. Russia's central bank, by contrast, extended its easing cycle. The divergence reflects fundamentally different domestic inflation dynamics layered on top of a shared energy shock — a configuration that typically produces meaningful currency volatility and cross-border capital flow realignment.
