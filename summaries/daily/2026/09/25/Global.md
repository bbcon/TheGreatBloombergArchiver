• Brent crude fell toward $105/barrel and WTI to ~$93 (–1.6%) on reports that US and Iranian negotiators are exploring a phased deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz.
• Global government bond yields remain at multi-decade highs; the average yield on sovereign debt worldwide is near 4%, the highest since 2007.
• US retail diesel prices exceeded $6.50/gallon for the first time, driven by refinery capacity losses in both the Middle East and Russia; Trump advisers are studying a short-term diesel export ban.
• France pledged to send troops to defend Saudi oil infrastructure as Houthi forces claimed responsibility for ballistic missile strikes on Yanbu and Taif.

**Strait of Hormuz and Energy Markets**

The potential US-Iran framework being discussed on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly represents the most significant near-term catalyst for energy markets. A phased agreement reopening the Strait would materially relieve the oil price pressure that has been a primary driver of the global bond selloff. Treasury Secretary Bessent's thesis — that yields will decline once the Iran conflict resolves — would gain credibility under such a scenario. However, simultaneous Houthi attacks on Saudi oil infrastructure and France's decision to deploy troops to defend those facilities underscore that regional instability extends well beyond the bilateral US-Iran dimension. Any Hormuz deal, if achieved, remains fragile.

**Global Bond Regime Shift**

Beyond the energy shock, a structural argument for sustainably higher rates is gaining traction. The convergence of elevated sovereign debt loads, AI-driven capital expenditure, fiscal deficits across major economies, and central banks constrained by above-target inflation has pushed the global cost of capital to levels not seen in nearly two decades. Whether this represents a cyclical peak or a durable regime shift — as Vanguard and others now contend — remains genuinely uncertain, and the resolution of the Hormuz situation will be an important near-term test of that thesis.
