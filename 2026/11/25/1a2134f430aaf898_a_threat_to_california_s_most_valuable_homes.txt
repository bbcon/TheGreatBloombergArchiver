King tides revive debate

By Todd Woody 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVl1IPrK6vs/todd-woody?cmpid=BBD112526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11833>

Record high tides 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-24/san-francisco-faces-onslaught-of-tidal-floods-juiced-by-el-nino?cmpid=BBD112526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11833>
 are hitting the California coast this week, prompting Marin County officials 
to install yellow barriers and pumps to protect the sole road to Seadrift, a 
gated enclave of multimillion-dollar homes wedged between the Pacific Ocean and 
a lagoon near San Francisco.

With sea levels 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-24/highest-ever-ocean-temperature-measured-as-super-el-nino-forms?cmpid=BBD112526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11833>
 magnified by extreme ocean temperatures and an approaching storm, the local 
fire department also plans to station personnel there. If the road becomes 
impassable, residents could be evacuated by dune buggy, helicopter or even by 
raft in a medical emergency.
Waves from Tropical Storm Rachel crash into the boardwalk and homes in Long 
Beach, California, on Oct. 11.Photographer: David Swanson/AFP/Getty Images
Across the Golden State, the price 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-26/california-faces-billions-in-flood-losses-as-el-nino-strengthens?cmpid=BBD112526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11833>
 of protecting wealthy homeowners and their beach houses is rising with the 
ocean as an El Niño amplified by climate change accelerates coastal erosion. 
Down the coast near Los Angeles, $5 million Dana Point and Long Beach homes 
battered by giant wavescollapsed into the ocean 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-10-16/malibu-mansions-face-el-nino-waves-after-palisades-fire?cmpid=BBD112526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11833>
 earlier this fall as the surf washed out foundations and opened up a huge 
sinkhole at actor Nicolas Cage’s Malibu mansion.

The coastal destruction is escalating a fight over the costs of saving, or at 
least buying time for, some of the state’s most valuable homes. At its heart is 
who gets protected as the ocean advances. Seawalls and other barriers can 
shield pricey homes from rising seas, but they also speed up the erosion of 
public beaches thatgenerate billions of dollars 
<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0964569111002067> in 
revenue.

Across the lagoon from Seadrift in Bolinas, a hippie surf town favored by San 
Francisco’s tech elite, an extensive seawall is under construction in front of
billionaire Mark Pincus 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-06-25/consumer-side-of-ai-currently-underweighted-mark-pincus-video?cmpid=BBD112526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11833>
’ beachfront house. As a pile driver planted steel beams into the sediment in 
October, the beach had disappeared under waves that crashed against the 
construction site.

“It raises questions about whether the public as a whole should be financing 
the protection of private property through the loss of beach resources,” says
Charles Lester 
<https://msi.ucsb.edu/people/principal-investigators/charles-lester>, director 
of the Ocean and Coastal Policy Center at the University of California at Santa 
Barbara.
Water filled barriers along Calle del Arroyo road in Stinson Beach, California.
Photographer: David Paul Morris/Bloomberg
Time to adapt is running out as climate impacts accelerate. Disputes over 
armoring beachfront dwellings are hashed out one building permit at a time but 
climate change is now threatening huge swathes of the coast at once as erosion 
intensifies. As much as $10 billion in coastal property “is likely to be 
underwater” by 2050 with another $10 billion at risk from high tides, according 
to a statereport 
<https://lao.ca.gov/reports/2020/4261/sea-level-rise-081020.pdf>.

“We’re beginning to see what could well be catastrophic losses along the 
coastline,” saysAlbert Lin <https://law.ucdavis.edu/people/albert-lin>, 
co-director of the California Environmental Law and Policy Center at the 
University of California at Davis. “That could be a jolt to the system but so 
far it’s been easier to put our heads in the sand.”

In Stinson Beach, the community that includes Seadrift, Marin County pegged 
the price 
<https://www.marincounty.gov/departments/cda/planning/researchreports/stinson-beach-adaptation-and-resilience-collaboration-arc>
 of sea level rise adaptation at $1.2 billion to avoid $1.3 billion in property 
losses in the coming decades. The town has a population of 541 and most 
properties are vacation homes.

Elevating those homes, raising roads and bridges and protecting the beach 
would cost an average $255,000 per property by 2030, rising to more than $1 
million through 2050. Still, the county acknowledged in a 2025 study that 
mitigation measures recommended for the next 15 years “appear unachievable” due 
to a lack of funding. Homeowners argue that failure to protect their homes 
through such measures violates their property rights.

“The cost of houses here is insane,” says Seadrift resident Vicki Sebastiani, 
standing outside her beachfront house that flooded during the 2023 El Niño, as 
workers board up the neighboring $10 million house.

Answers in Antarctica

By Ishika Mookerjee 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUJMYcOXODs/ishika-mookerjee?cmpid=BBD112526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11833>
 andMary Hui 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXDwzJNHmiM/mary-hui?cmpid=BBD112526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11833>

Scientists will deploy technology in Antarctica that was used to detect the 
coronavirus <https://www.resrchintl.com/covid-19> in Singapore at the height of 
the pandemic, as they seek to better understand the effects of a changing 
climate.
Benjamin Horton during a research trip to Antarctica in February 2023.Source: 
Benjamin P. Horton
Researchers will use portable canisters to capture airborne microbes, which 
can act as natural tracers and biological indicators of environmental change. 
Sampling will be conducted in parts of western Antarctica for the first time, 
helping tofill data gaps <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13569471/> 
and provide a more detailed picture of global warming.

“It’s all about using these microbes to better understand wind or humidity 
patterns or weather conditions,” saidBenjamin Horton 
<https://www.cityu.edu.hk/see/people/prof-benjamin-horton>, dean of the School 
of Energy and Environment at the City University of Hong Kong, who will lead 
the researchers on the trip. “We know that what happens in Antarctica has a 
significant influence on many people in the Southern Hemisphere.”

Changing weather in Antarctica has been linked 
<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0921818125003029> to 
extreme temperatures and droughts in the Southern Hemisphere, and understanding 
shifts in polar regions is vital because the poles are crucial regulators of 
the world’s climate and serve as early-warning indicators.
