On days when she’s cooped up in her residence, inspiration sometimes strikes 
Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi to post on social media. She might 
detail her government’s inflation measures, or wax lyricalabout cockroaches 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-24/japan-pm-takaichi-defends-habit-of-staying-at-official-residence?cmpid=BBD093026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9528>
 and reincarnation.

Lately, immigration has been on her mind 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/japan-s-takaichi-touts-sweeping-changes-in-immigration-rules?cmpid=BBD093026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9528>
.

In several lengthy weekend posts, Takaichi outlined government efforts to 
crack down on people residing in Japan illegally. Visa applications are going 
through more rigorous screening processes, she added.
Takaichi at the UN General Assembly in New York on Sept. 22.Photographer: John 
Lamparski/Bloomberg
Those stricter rules ride a rising wave of angst against foreign nationals in 
Japan, sometimes dubbed Japan’s “foreigner problem.” One study shows those in 
their 30s or youngerparticularly set against foreigners 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-02/what-s-driving-the-far-right-sanseito-voters-who-shook-japanese-politics?cmpid=BBD093026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9528>
.

The number of non-Japanese people residing in the country topped 4 million in 
late 2025, up 9.5% from the previous year to a record, according to the Justice 
Ministry.

The ratio of foreign nationals in Japan — forecast to rise to 10.8% by 2070 
from just 2.2% in 2020 — is still a fraction of that in other countries.

Takaichi has talked of a minority of foreigners whose actions “deviate from 
our laws and rules,” giving rise to “feelings of anxiety or unfairness among 
the public.”

But sentiment against foreign residents may be more of a symptom than a source 
of such anxieties. One recent study noted a pronounced uptick in foreigner 
angst among those mostpessimistic about Japan’s future 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-30/japan-pm-takaichi-faces-biggest-test-yet-as-yen-sinks-approval-ratings-drop?cmpid=BBD093026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9528>
.
Takaichi with Self-Defense Forces senior commanders at the Defense Ministry in 
Tokyo on Sept. 2.Photographer: Kiyoshi Ota/Bloomberg
Takaichi may be trying to change that negative narrative as she sets 
unprecedented investment goals and lays out plans to grow Japan’s economy. 
Those measures may depend to some degree onattracting foreign labor 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-31/japan-s-firms-hire-foreigners-even-as-takaichi-tightens-rules?cmpid=BBD093026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9528>
.

Takaichi says her government is now putting plans into action.

“There is no future for a country that doesn’t take on challenges,” she said 
this month, dismissing politics “focused on self-preservation.”

That’s a tough circle to square for any Japanese leader. — Sakura Murakami 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXPm08xOzlY/sakura-murakami?cmpid=BBD093026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9528>
