Outliers become the norm

By Laura Millan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD090526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8571>

The giant wave of rock, ice and debris that swept through 100 kilometers (62 
miles) of Nepalese valleyslast week 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-nepal-flash-flood/?cmpid=BBD090526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8571>
 is the deadliest illustration yet of how climate change is fueling mountain 
disasters of epic proportions.

Scientists have long predicted 
<https://www.unep.org/resources/report/ipcc-special-report-ocean-and-cryosphere-changing-climate>
 quicker melting of snow and ice and accelerated thawing of permafrost in high 
mountain areas that are warming twice as fast as the rest of the planet. For 
years they have documented the results: more frequent rockfalls, avalanches, 
lake outbursts, flash floods and landslides.

Even so, the sheer scale of the disaster in Nepal shocked experts. “There are 
things that we’re completely unprepared for,” saidNoah Diffenbaugh 
<https://sustainability.stanford.edu/people/noah-diffenbaugh>, a climate 
scientist at Stanford University. The floods fall into the category of “things 
that are just completely outside of our experience.”

The event was triggered on Aug. 26 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-27/nepal-flash-floods-what-causes-them-and-why-the-country-is-so-vulnerable?cmpid=BBD090526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8571>
 when the bedrock gave way below a glacier near the Langtang Lirung peak in the 
Nepalese Himalayas. A huge section of the glacier, measuring between 1 and 1.3 
kilometers wide, detached from the mountain. The fast-moving deluge that ensued 
raised water levels in rivers downstream by as much as 9 meters within just 
half an hour.
Women view the newly widened Trishuli River following the flash floods in 
Devighat, Nepal.Photographer: Ezra Acayan/Getty Images AsiaPac
It’s among the catastrophic mountain incidents that climate scientists would 
have classed as outliers not long ago, but that are now happening more 
regularly. Last year alone, three glacial lakes burst in Nepal, flooding 
valleys downstream, with one occurringon the same river 
<https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/31/world/asia/china-nepal-tibet-flood-himalayas-risk.html>
 as last week’s mega-event. In 2024, the village of Thame in Nepal’s Everest 
region wasflooded <https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/26/4131/2026/>, while 
in 2023 India’s Sikkim and Himachal Pradesh states were deluged. All told, 
hundreds of people died in these disasters, which werereported 
<https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-asia-india-67678440> in foreign media 
<https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/oct/05/india-floods-death-toll-lhonak-lake-injuries-missing-sikkim>
 but did not capture the attention of the world.

Since 2014, Kristen Cook, a geomorphologist at IRD, France’s National Research 
Institute for Sustainable Development, has spent several months each year in 
Nepal. She travels to places affected by big floods to study how water, debris 
and mud move down mountain valleys and impact the geography downstream. Her 
work is part of an effort to find more effective ways to alert and shield 
people and infrastructure from these devastating events.

While she witnessed landslides and glacial lake outbursts during her first 
years in Nepal, their frequency has increased notably since 2021, she said. 
This year’s tragedy killed at least 1,250 people and left 4,200 missing, but 
will also help Cook and other researchers better understand the mechanics of 
large mountain floods.

The sheer scale of the disaster gives researchers more data to understand how 
these floods behave. “I want to know what water does to the valley, whether it 
erodes the sides of the valley, triggers landslides, picks out sediments,” Cook 
said. “I want to know how all that affects the properties of the flow, which in 
turn affects its magnitude and how much further downstream it can travel.”

Not unlike detectives investigating a crime, Cook and her team start by asking 
locals what they remember about the disaster. Sounds and even smells can reveal 
what caused the flood and where it came from. They take measurements, pictures 
and, when allowed, samples. Occasionally, they conduct expeditions to the 
source of the disaster, hiking for days to reach remote glaciers and high 
mountain slopes. There is a lot at stake on these expeditions.

“This frequency of events we have seen since 2021 or so is a lot,” Cook said. 
“We’re going to continue to see these disasters and more of them.”

Further rollbacks

The Trump administration on Friday proposed a new rule 
<https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2026-09/wotus_snprm_9-2026_0.pdf> 
that would significantly decrease how many US streams and wetlands would be 
protected from pollution and industrial impacts, part of a larger push by 
officials to roll backenvironmental regulations 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-12/why-trump-s-epa-cuts-will-outlast-him-this-time-around?cmpid=BBD090526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8571>
 for air, water and land.

The changes, environmental experts said, would result in more dramatic 
reductions in the number of protected water bodies nationwide than an earlier 
plan from November.

“This is a humongous rollback and it’s a bigger rollback than their initial 
proposal,” said Jon Devine, who leads the Natural Resources Defense Council’s 
federal water policy team.
Wetlands near Candelaria, Texas.Photographer: John Moore/Getty Images via 
Getty Images North America
This week, in response to 220,000 public comments on the first proposal, the 
Environmental Protection Agency and US Army Corps of Engineersmade a 
supplemental one 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TKUEWMR24U9G?cmpid=BBD090526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260905&utm_content=8571>
 that provides some alternatives.

“Landowners, farmers, ranchers, energy producers, developers, and small 
businesses deserve greater regulatory clarity and predictability from EPA, and 
we are committed to delivering on that while adhering to the Clean Water Act 
and the Supreme Court’s direction,” said EPA press secretary Brigit Hirsch.

Under the updated plan, wetlands and other bodies of water whose flow has been 
temporarily interrupted by drought would also only be protected for up to five 
years. If the drought lasts longer, then the affected bodies of water would 
lose those protections.

Elizabeth Southerland, a former top EPA water official who resigned under the 
first Trump administration 
<https://www.eenews.net/articles/senior-official-quits-decrying-triumph-of-myth-over-truth/>
, said that provision would “ensure more wetlands and streams lose jurisdiction 
as climate change intensifies.”
