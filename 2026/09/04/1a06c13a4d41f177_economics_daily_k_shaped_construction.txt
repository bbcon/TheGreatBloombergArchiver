K-Shaped Construction

When I was a reporter and there were embargoed lock-ups for media to prepare 
stories on US indicators, I was excited one morning when a Commerce Department 
official confirmed for me that the latest construction spending figure had hit 
a record high. Until, that is, a Bloomberg colleague piped up.

“Dude, it hits a record almost every month.” Indeed, as a nominal number — 
with no adjustments for inflation — it would be unusual for spending to see an 
outright drop. Population growth means more residences need to be built. 
Economic growth means more construction for businesses. It was a lesson.

So when Commerce reported this week that construction spending fell, it caught 
my eye. All the more so since thedata-center boom 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-02/data-center-spending-to-reach-31-6-trillion-by-2050-on-ai-boom?cmpid=BBD090426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260904&utm_content=8524>
 was supposed to be propelling the industry. In fact, July marked the 12th 
straight month of year-on-year declines. Spending is down almost 5% from its 
peak in February last year.

Construction is running at the weakest pace in more than three years. And when 
you strip out the data-center building surge, spending is contracting at its 
fastest since the end of the housing boom in 2006, according to calculations by 
Patrick Perret-Green, a longtime strategist who now heads PPG Macro.

“For all the noise, data centers only account for 3.5% of construction 
spending,” Green wrote in a note this week. “They are not going to save the 
industry.” He also said that the labor intensity of this type of building is 
“much less than that required for residential housing.”

That challenges the Trump administration’s logic that AI-related construction 
will serve as a big stimulus for blue-collar jobs. As for what’s depressing 
residential construction, there’s no secret there, as this newsletternoted 
recently 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-19/us-housing-is-a-buyer-s-market-trouble-is-there-aren-t-many-buyers?cmpid=BBD090426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260904&utm_content=8524>
: high borrowing costs. With a selloff in the bond market, 30-year mortgage 
rates are creeping back uptoward 7% 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/us-mortgage-rates-climb-to-6-71-highest-since-july-2025?cmpid=BBD090426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260904&utm_content=8524>
.

But it’s not just homebuilding that’s in decline. “Strip out data centers and 
the power to feed them and nonresidential construction contracts,” Yelena 
Maleyev, a senior economist at KPMG, wrote in anote 
<https://kpmg.com/us/en/articles/2026/july-2026-construction-spending.html>. 
Manufacturing construction is downsome 21%  
<https://www.census.gov/construction/c30/pdf/release.pdf>year-on-year.

It all adds up to “K-shaped US construction,” Green wrote. In other words, one 
section going up and another heading down.

Need-to-Know Research

For years after the global financial crisis, policymakers from Tokyo 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2013-06-19/japan-inc-sits-on-italy-sized-cash-pile-as-abe-urges-investment?cmpid=BBD090426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260904&utm_content=8524>
 to Washington were frustrated by a reluctance of companies to invest, and a 
preference by many toward simply accumulating cash, or spending it on stock 
buybacks. The Biden administration even introduced atax on buybacks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-10-03/corporations-care-little-about-democrats-stock-buyback-tax?cmpid=BBD090426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260904&utm_content=8524>
, and the Trump team hascracked down <> on defense companies doing them.

So what’s happening with cash? Societe Generale’s global quantitative strategy 
team, led by Andrew Lapthorne, has taken a look. Going through a universe of 
1,500 listed nonfinancial firms, they find that, in aggregate, companies aren’t 
returning as much cash as they used to. They’ve moderated buybacks, and instead 
are issuing more debt and equity as they ramp up investment.

“The largest AI spenders sit at the center of this shift. They’ve recorded the 
biggest increase in investment spending and have raised substantial cash 
through debt issuance in turn reducing buybacks,” the SocGen team wrote this 
week. What’s also evident is that despite increases in capital spending, 
corporate cash piles are back up at record highs — meaning there’s firepower 
for more investment — they wrote.
