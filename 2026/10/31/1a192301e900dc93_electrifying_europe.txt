Economic insulation

By Akshat Rathi 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUdOobfNzkQ/akshat-rathi?cmpid=BBD103126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10781>

Europeans have been lurching from one crisis to another in recent years, and 
are left with high living costs and declining industries. Protests in France 
and Germany are just the latest signs ofpublic discontent 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-10-30/france-protests-who-is-on-the-streets-and-what-do-they-want?cmpid=BBD103126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10781>
, stoked by inflation and employment fears.

Wars in Europe and the Middle East have laid bare a core cause: an 
uncomfortable reliance on fossil fuel imports. One way to tackle the problems 
for the continent home to more than 700 million is more electrification.

“Electricity is the solution for our green aspirations as well as for our 
security equation,” Kristian Ruby, secretary general of Eurelectric, which 
represents more than 3,500 utilities in 32 countries, said on theZero podcast 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/electrification-works-for-security-and-the-environment-how-about-cost?cmpid=BBD103126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10781>
.

Oil and gas make up for more than 60% of energy consumed by industry, 
buildings and transport, while fossil fuels make upless than a third 
<https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/european-electricity-review-2025/> of 
Europe’s electricity mix.

“Europe has finally gotten the memo that electricity is our way of insulating 
our economies against these roller coaster prices of fossil fuels and against 
the wars in geographies that we don’t control,” Ruby said.
Workers board up a store ahead of a demonstration in Frankfurt on Oct. 30. 
Protests over high living costs are expected in France, Germany and Italy this 
weekend.Photographer: Alex Kraus/Bloomberg
And it’s not just a solution for Europe. If the world pursues electrification 
seriously, it cancut its total energy use in half 
<https://fullycharged.show/podcasts/podcast-361-why-electrification-could-cut-global-energy-use-in-half/>
 while producing all the same services that the current system provides. That’s 
according to Oxford University Professor Jan Rosenow, who was also interviewed 
on theZero podcast as part of an upcoming series on electrification (subscribe 
here 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/green-zero-emissions-podcast?sref=jjXJRDFv&cmpid=BBD103126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10781>
 for updates).

Devices powered by electricity are vastly more efficient than those that run 
on fossil fuels: electric cars go three times the distance of their gasoline 
counterparts for the same amount of energy consumed and heat pumps provide four 
times the heating output of a natural-gas boiler.

Taking advantage of that efficiency, however, will not be easy. While 
electrification will lower energy costs over the next few decades, it will 
require big upfront investments first to build out the infrastructure to 
generate, distribute and consume that power. For example, in 2023, the European 
Commissionestimated 
<https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX:52023DC0757> 
that it will cost €584 billion ($651 billion) by the end of this decade in grid 
investments alone if the bloc were to meet its climate goals.

However, if the EU succeeds in securing those investments and doubles its 
share of energy use coming from electricity by 2040, theElectrification Action 
Plan 
<https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/plan-make-europe-first-electro-continent-2026-07-17_en>
 published in July estimates the bloc could save as much as €260 billion in 
avoided fossil-fuel imports every year.

Early electrification comes from converting gasoline cars to electric ones and 
replacing natural-gas boilers with heat pumps. These technologies are 
mainstream and, with the right policies, places like Norway and Finland have 
shown that high adoption rates can be achieved.

Electrification of other sectors of the economy are harder. Consider the 
industrial sector: “Almost all industrial processes can be electrified,” said 
Rosenow. “The problem is the further up you go in the temperature range, the 
more difficult it gets because technologies become less advanced.”

Industrial heat pumps, far larger than the ones designed for homes, can 
provide steam at 200C. Electric boilers can provide heat of up to 400C. Then 
resistance heaters or plasma torches can get well beyond 500C, while 
electric-arc furnaces can go beyond 1500C. But the hotter the temperature, the 
less tested the technology and the higher the cost of using it.
An Octopus employee works at the installation of a heat pump outside a home in 
Reading, UK.Photographer: Carlotta Cardana / Bloomberg
While the challenges of finance and technology adoption are real, one 
shouldn’t overstate them, said Ruby. Europe does not have many fossil-fuel 
reserves it can exploit, but it does have plenty of potential for deploying 
renewables, a strong workforce to execute on its energy plans and the 
industrial base ready to adopt new technologies.

“What we need to do is really to stack it up against the alternative, which is 
continued dependence on supplies of fossil fuels from the outside that we have 
no control over,” Ruby said.

Voting over power bills

By Linda Poon 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUc4draqlcE/linda-poon?cmpid=BBD103126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10781>

In an Oct. 16 Instagram video from Jane Fonda 
<https://www.instagram.com/p/De2K-n9P3Dc/?hl=en>, the actress and climate 
activist addressed the people of Georgia, urging them to vote two Democrats 
into an obscure agency known as the public service commission.

“Not many people have heard about it,” Fonda says to the camera. But “this is 
more important than you could imagine.”

While the state’s most-watched races have been for governor and Senate, these 
two down-ballot contests are also top of mind for some Georgia Democrats trying 
to flip the state and for voters fed up with soaring power bills and new data 
centers.

The public service commission regulates investor-owned utilities — including 
gas and electric — most of which operate as monopolies in their regions. The 
agency has final say in how much residents pay on their utility bills.
The Vogtle nuclear plant in Waynesboro, Georgia. (Photo by Pallava 
Bagla/Corbis via Getty Images)
For electricity providers like Georgia Power, the agency determines what grid 
upgrades companies make, how much of the costs they can pass to customers and 
how much profit they make from their investments. It also reviews the terms of 
agreements over power usage by data centers and oversees clean energy goals.

“They’re the Supreme Court justices of energy,” said Charles Hua, founder and 
executive director of the nonprofit PowerLines, whose mission is to demystify 
the utility regulatory system for consumers. Nationwide, about 200 people serve 
on public service commissions, which also go by names like public utility 
commission and corporation commission. Together, they’ll oversee$1.1 trillion 
<https://www.eei.org/en/news/news/all/electric-companies-to-invest-nearly-%24208b-in-2025-to-strengthen-grid-and-drive-economic-growth>
 in projected electric utility spending through 2029.

While most commissions are nonpartisan, those that are elected have in recent 
decades been Republican-run and incumbents have largely defended their seats 
against Democrats and challengers from their own party. But frustration over 
energy unaffordability has begun to reshape the landscape in some states: 
Across the 15 public service commissioner races in nine states this year, only 
five incumbents are running. Others have lost their primaries, reached their 
term limits, resigned, or chosen not to run for reelection.

The race in Georgia shows what’s at stake for voters. After two Democrats won 
commission seats last year, the board could now flip to majority-Democrat, 
reversing two decades of an all-Republican board.

The next commissioners will help oversee more than $54 billion by 2030 
<https://powerlinesdashboard.github.io/PUC-Dashboard/src/factsheet.html?state=GA>
 in proposed capital expenditures from Georgia Power for upgrades that could 
include things like new power plants, transmission lines and distribution poles 
and wires. The cost is likely to trickle down to customers.

Cautious optimism

By Alastair Marsh 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQvVwWuhxcQ/alastair-marsh?cmpid=BBD103126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10781>

Countries meeting at the United Nations’ COP17 summit in Yerevan, Armenia, 
concluded two weeks of discussions on how to protect biodiversity with a pledge 
to redouble their efforts to meet nature targets.

At the outset of the two-week event, a global review 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-15/un-says-biodiversity-protections-are-off-track-ahead-of-summithttps://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-15/un-says-biodiversity-protections-are-off-track-ahead-of-summit?cmpid=BBD103126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10781>
 published by the UN found that not enough concrete action has been made to 
meet the 2030 goals of a landmark agreement signed by nearly 200 nations in 
2022. Countries meeting acknowledged thataction 
<https://www.cbd.int/doc/c/a1ad/0628/41987af10d6a81059f6b3e90/cop-17-l-15-en.pdfhttps://www.cbd.int/doc/c/a1ad/0628/41987af10d6a81059f6b3e90/cop-17-l-15-en.pdf>
 has been insufficient.

Observers attending the event expressed cautious optimism 
<https://wwf.panda.org/wwf_news/press_releases/?16511466/WWF-COP17-countries-agree-to-scale-action-on-2030-nature-targets>
 that the plan of action to accelerate nature targets agreed today would yield 
results. Still, concerns remain that inability to finance efforts would remain 
a stumbling block.

“Institutional investors need clear, consistent and credible policy signals, 
alongside investable opportunities, if substantially more capital is going to 
flow towards nature,” Laura Hillis, managing director for responsible 
investment at the £3.5 billion Church of England Pensions Board, said in an 
email. “As an asset owner we see the decline of nature as a long-term risk to 
portfolio returns, so this is a particularly key issue for us.”
