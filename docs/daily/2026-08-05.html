Daily Brief

Wednesday, August 05, 2026

US

  • The S&P 500 reached a new all-time high on Tuesday, gaining 1.8%, its first record in two months, driven by strong tech earnings and Hormuz deal optimism.
  • The US trade deficit narrowed 5.6% in June to $73.3 billion, better than the prior month, as imports fell for the first time since the start of the year — though the gap with China widened.
  • The Nasdaq 100 has surged 9.3% over four days, recovering $3.5 trillion in market value following a prior correction concentrated in AI and semiconductor names.
  • Gasoline prices averaging above $4 per gallon are generating political pressure on Republicans ahead of November midterms, with Trump publicly criticizing ExxonMobil and Chevron's combined $29 billion quarterly profit.

Trade and Macro Data

The June trade deficit came in at $73.3 billion, narrowing from the prior month as imports declined broadly for the first time this year — a likely reflection of front-loading effects fading rather than durable demand weakness. The shortfall with China widened, underscoring persistent bilateral trade imbalances despite supply-chain diversification. Goldman Sachs economists estimate 2026 US AI capital expenditure at just under $600 billion, or approximately 1.8% of GDP, rising to 2.5% by 2027, providing a meaningful offset to the energy-price drag stemming from the Iran conflict.

Equities and Earnings

Blowout second-quarter results from Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, and Nvidia triggered the Nasdaq 100's four-day, $3.5 trillion recovery, erasing most of the prior month's correction. Evidence points to over-leveraged hedge funds as a proximate cause of that selloff: the Goldman Sachs hedge fund VIP ETF appears to have led both the decline and the rebound. AMD fell 8.4% on an underwhelming sales outlook, while SpaceX dropped 11% after disclosing higher-than-expected AI spending. Inflation swaps have declined materially over two months, with expectations now pointing below the Fed's 2% target — reducing near-term rate-hike concerns and providing additional support to equities. ---

Asia

  • The Reserve Bank of India held its benchmark rate unchanged for the fourth consecutive meeting, trimming its inflation forecast while nudging up its growth outlook.
  • China's services PMI grew at its weakest pace in two years, while Beijing tightened export controls on drones to the US in retaliation for Washington's tech curbs.
  • The South Korea KOSPI rose 3.8% on the session, with full-year earnings growth expected at 220%, the highest of any major market.
  • US Treasury Secretary Bessent coordinated the first joint US-Japan yen intervention in nearly three decades, signaling more action may follow.

Central Banks and Currencies

The RBI's decision to hold rates reflects a calculated bet that inflation pressures from the Iran conflict are transitory rather than structural. Governor Malhotra's slight upward revision to the growth outlook reinforces India's status as a resilient growth anchor. Separately, Bessent's orchestrated yen support — which included deliberately revealing a notepad referencing a $10 billion purchase — marks a departure from conventional Treasury practice. The intervention reflects Washington's concern that sustained yen weakness could trigger competitive devaluations across Asia, compounding regional inflationary pressures.

Equities and Capital Markets

Emerging market equities, when weighted by full market capitalization including Taiwan and South Korea, have risen more than 34% year-to-date — one of the strongest starts on record — driven almost entirely by the semiconductor supply chain's exposure to AI infrastructure. South Korean earnings growth is forecast at 220%, with shipbuilders and chipmakers both benefiting from geopolitical fragmentation. Singapore's GIC is seeking to sell approximately $1 billion in private equity stakes in the secondaries market, a signal that even long-horizon sovereign funds are actively rebalancing portfolios as private asset exits remain slow. ---

Europe

  • Rhine water levels have fallen to the lowest on record ahead of peak summer dry season, with conditions expected to worsen; ING estimates a 0.3 percentage point drag on German GDP growth this year.
  • Danube drought conditions are threatening nuclear power generation in Romania and Hungary, with Romanian authorities deploying military explosives to redirect river flow to the Cernavoda nuclear plant.
  • Romania's caretaker government recalled parliament in emergency session to pass reforms needed to unlock more than €3 billion in EU funding amid an ongoing political deadlock.
  • Italian Finance Minister Giorgetti announced plans to use EU-authorized extra budget leeway for energy-related measures.

Drought and Industrial Impact

Europe's inland waterway crisis represents a compounding supply shock for an industrial base already contending with elevated energy costs and trade disruptions. Bloomberg Economics estimates that one month of severely restricted Rhine navigation reduces German industrial production by up to 1% and adds inflationary pressure through higher freight costs. The Rhine corridor connects Rotterdam — Europe's busiest seaport — with the Rhine-Ruhr steel belt, Frankfurt's financial hub, and the Ludwigshafen chemical complex. BASF and other large users have invested in low-draft barges and inventory buffers since prior droughts, which may moderate the near-term impact, though a prolonged dry spell could still cause significant damage.

Romania's Overlapping Crises

Romania's situation is the most acute: historic drought, double-digit inflation, a stagnant economy, and a months-long government formation impasse are converging simultaneously. The country's nuclear plant, which normally supplies 20% of national electricity, required unprecedented military intervention this week to maintain cooling water flows. The political vacuum strengthens the far right, which already leads in polls. As a NATO member hosting US logistical assets tied to the Iran conflict, Romania's instability carries implications beyond its borders. ---

LatAm

  • Brazil entered a diplomatic dispute with the US and Argentina over alleged interference in its October presidential election, with Lula facing a tight contest against Flávio Bolsonaro.
  • El Niño is disrupting Peru's anchovy fishery — worth up to $1.8 billion in exports — as warming Pacific waters keep fishing grounds closed, directly slowing economic growth.
  • Brazilian central bank officials were reported to be considering an interest rate cut at their Wednesday meeting.

Political Economy

Brazil's accusation that Washington and Buenos Aires are interfering in its October election adds geopolitical complexity to an already contested race between President Lula and right-wing Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, who has cultivated ties with Trump and Milei. The dispute risks complicating Brazil's trade and diplomatic relationships at a sensitive moment, coming as the country navigates elevated inflation and the potential for monetary easing. If Bolsonaro prevails, it would represent the most consequential shift in Latin American political alignment in years.

El Niño and Commodity Exposure

For the broader region, a strengthening El Niño — potentially the most powerful in 76 years — poses a material risk to agricultural output and energy systems. Peru's anchovy fishery disruption is an early and tangible economic cost. Colombia faces compounding pressure on energy infrastructure and food prices from both El Niño-related weather disruption and elevated oil import costs. Bloomberg Economics research suggests El Niño drought conditions could raise consumer prices by nearly two percentage points in the most exposed economies, adding to inflation already elevated by the Iran conflict. ---

Global

  • Brent crude fell 11% from Monday's open on growing optimism over a Strait of Hormuz deal, though the Houthis issued fresh shipping threats, partially reversing the move.
  • A strengthening El Niño is on track to become the most powerful in 76 years, posing compounding risks to economies already strained by the Iran conflict.
  • Former senior officials from the UK, France, Germany, and Russia met in Vienna last month to explore conditions for Ukraine peace talks.

Hormuz and Energy Markets

The emerging framework — in which Iran and Oman would reopen the Strait under fees framed as ecological and management charges rather than tolls — appears close to formal announcement. The US pulled back from a planned military offensive over the weekend, which analysts interpret as a signal that Washington lacks the political will to escalate further, particularly as November midterms approach. Analysts describe the arrangement as strategically costly but practically payable: it effectively monetizes a critical global chokepoint without US military action, while reducing the uncertainty that has weighed most heavily on markets. Inflation swaps have declined sharply since the deal began taking shape, and the S&P 500 has returned to all-time highs. Whether the arrangement holds — and whether Iran can be deterred from escalating its demands — remains genuinely uncertain.

El Niño and Climate Risk

The convergence of a record-strength El Niño with a global economy already stressed by conflict-related supply disruptions represents a meaningful tail risk. Emerging markets face the most direct exposure, with food security, hydropower generation, and inflation all vulnerable. Europe's own climate stress is evident in record-low Rhine and Danube levels, which are simultaneously constraining industrial output and threatening nuclear power generation. These events are not isolated — they reflect a structural shift in climate conditions that is broadening the range of economies and supply chains exposed to weather-related disruption.
