Postcard From Buenos Aires <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EWLSychTU4Y>

Greetings from the hushed Argentine capital, which has come to a halt to 
welcome Pope Leo XIV <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5HkDwlgrk_E>. The pontiff 
wasgreeted on Sunday <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZKgNj5dGA8w> by the 
hobbit-like President Javier Milei, who has been in office since late 2023 and 
faces the electorate a year from now.

Argentina has a reputation for economic and financial drama, which Milei 
accentuated during thechainsaw-wielding campaign 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2024-08-16/javier-milei-s-popularity-survives-his-chainsaw-cuts-in-argentina?cmpid=BBD111026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11137>
 that brought him to power. His actual time in office has been oddly calm by 
such standards, and the 2027 election looks finely poised. Prediction markets 
saw his reelection chances at slightly better than 50% for most of this year, 
and now put the odds slightly against him:

The latest unwelcome news, before the country took a national holiday to 
welcome the pontiff, came from the US, where President Donald Trump has been a 
keen supporter of a kindred unconventional right-winger. Last year’s midterms 
in Argentina swung in Milei’s favor after US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent 
announceda package of $20 billion to support Argentina’s bonds and currency 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-24/bessent-says-us-negotiating-20-billion-swap-line-with-argentina?cmpid=BBD111026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11137>
 — providing the president’s party won the elections. It brought yields down 
and delivered a surprise victory.

A Democratic Congress in Washington can probably thwart such aid next year — 
and that has sent Argentine bond yields sharply higher:

The deterioration in bonds this year has been matched by a further steady 
devaluation of the peso, which tends to drive up inflation:

That’s bad news, but doesn’t rob Milei of his chance of a second term. He just 
needs to succeed in hisprimary task of reining in inflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-23/why-javier-milei-s-economic-strategy-has-worked-in-argentina?cmpid=BBD111026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11137>
. It’s currently running at 34%, which counts as a success by Argentine 
standards. That’s lower than the rate at the end of 2019, when the previous 
right-wing reformist government under President Mauricio Macri was voted out of 
office, and it’s also below the rate Milei inherited from his Peronist 
predecessor, Alberto Fernandez. The nation has survived theshock that his 
austerity policies 
<https://apnews.com/article/argentina-javier-milei-trump-disabilities-austerity-6d98fb3cecc91fe4f3e3f8800826275e>
 was always going to induce, prompting arebuke from the pope 
<https://www.dw.com/en/pope-urges-argentinas-milei-to-put-people-before-economy/a-79894779>
 on Sunday. Now, he must rein in price rises a little more:

As for his impact on investors, Milei has presided over a very un-Argentine 
boring period. In dollar terms, the benchmark Merval index is almost exactly 
where it was three years ago. That’s way behind the rest of the developing 
world, but impressive given the bitter medicine Milei has administered:

All depends on the economy over the next 12 months. My unscientific survey in 
the last few days suggests most would take the “over” — people think he can 
probably hold on. But they’re not as excited by Mr. Chainsaw as they are by 
Pope Leo, or bythe extraordinary 95th-minute goal 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rcAlpC5oKW4> with which Buenos Aires team Boca 
Juniors <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XKvnqFgoHsc> snatched the league 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OITwA6Ws7Iw> this weekend.

Another DeepSeek Moment

Shocking news from China can damage Nvidia Corp., the dominant 
semiconductor-maker. We learned that last year when word broke thatDeepSeek 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2025-01-28/deepseek-there-s-a-disruption-whale-in-nvidia-s-moat?cmpid=BBD111026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11137>
 had produced a competitive large language model. Nvidia far underperformed the 
main Nasdaq 100 that day — which turned out to be a buying opportunity for chip 
stocks:

Monday brought Nvidia’s worst performance since then, as it failed to join in 
the Nasdaq 100’s 3.4% rally 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-08/stock-market-today-dow-s-p-live-updates?cmpid=BBD111026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11137>
. The news this time was that CEO Jensen Huang hadhad an affair 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/geopolitics-take-nvidia-ceo-s-affair-to-new-level-of-scandal?cmpid=BBD111026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11137>
 with asuspected Chinese spy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/spy-themed-short-seller-bet-on-nvidia-s-china-problem-months-ago?cmpid=BBD111026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11137>
. The instant market reaction was that it was terrible news for Nvidia, but 
without wider repercussions:

Compared to the S&P 500 Equal-Weight index, Nvidia is remarkably lagging over 
the two years since the last presidential election. The fall before the 
allegations was already precipitous:

Nvidia’s market cap is now down more than $1.5 trillion in one month. It’s 
also stunningly cheap. On a blended forward 12-month basis, its price/earnings 
ratio is just above 10, its lowest since 2013:

For comparison, the S&P 500 trades at 17.7, and consumer staples at 23.6. If 
what was recently the hottest growth juggernaut on earth now trades at less 
than half the multiple of makers of diapers or toasters, it’s worth asking if 
this is overdone.

The allegations are obviously terrible for Huang himself. But it’s driven a 
huge reassessment of the prior certainty that Nvidia stood to be the clearest 
AI winner.

How can the honey-trap scandal on its own drive such a shift? Huang is a key 
man 
<https://www.forbes.com/councils/forbesbusinesscouncil/2024/01/10/key-person-risk-what-is-it-costing-your-business/>
. He may well have to resign, and has no clear succession plan. Market pricing 
implies that Nvidia will be helpless without him. If that’s true, it shouldn’t 
be — no individual should be that important to an enterprise of Nvidia’s scale.

If this story has a broader impact, it concerns governance. The G in ESG 
(environmental, social and governance) investing has fallen out of favor as the 
entire concept has been subsumed into political culture wars. It was memorably 
dismissed by failed Ohio governorship candidate Vivek Ramaswamy as“Woke, Inc.” 
<https://www.amazon.es/Woke-Inc-Inside-Social-Justice/dp/1800750676> and he had 
a point. But whatever your views on environmentalism, worrying about corporate 
governance is not woke. There’s a really strong case for avoiding companies 
with problematic governance, such as requiring excessive trust in one, or a 
few, key individuals.

Massive IPOs are due in the next few months in which the founders hope to 
avoid surrendering much control. The potential merger of Tesla Inc. and SpaceX 
will involve huge trust in Elon Musk, possiblythe world’s most controversial 
human 
<https://theweek.com/elon-musk/1022182/elon-musks-most-controversial-moments>. 
This issue has been ignored for too long.

Woke 1.0 went too far, as its practitioners now accept, and there are huge 
flaws in the way ESG has been applied. But Ramaswamy’selection defeat 
<https://www.firstpost.com/explainers/ohio-election-results-why-vivek-ramaswamy-lost-governor-race-to-amy-acton-14057734.html>
 last week suggests the anti-woke backlash also went too far. It’s time to 
re-establish a disciplined focus on corporate governance.



Affordability and Expectations

Where does the affordability problem come from? Naturally prices are an 
important component, but so are incomes. Inflation at 3% would be much less of 
a problem if people expected their pay to go up by more. But they don’t.

The latest survey of consumer expectations by the New York Federal Reserve, 
largely conducted before last week’s sharp fall in oil prices, revealed 
expected inflation for the next 12 months of 3.9% — too high for the central 
bank’s comfort. But just as significant, particularly in explaining last week’s 
election results, is their expectation for wage increases of only 2.6%:

Outside of the post-pandemic bulge in prices, this is the greatest gap between 
the two since 2013. People seem to have given up on any hope that their pay can 
keep up with inflation. If there’s a key to the affordability crisis, it lies 
in changing that.

Emerging Disadvantage

Even as US markets rebounded, emerging markets are still reeling from fears 
that aDemocratic sweep 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TO0GG1KGZAJQ?cmpid=BBD111026_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11137>
 will lead to tighter oversight of artificial intelligence. As EM equities are 
dominated by the likes of Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co., Samsung 
Electronics Co. and SK Hynix Inc., the global selloff is understandable.

It’s not clear that US tech stocks’ recovery will drive a comparable rebound 
in emerging markets. Efforts by Taiwan’s market regulator to restrict 
short-selling show that idiosyncratic responses could jump-start investor 
confidence, as they coincided with Monday’s rally.

The broader EM index excluding China is up by about 22% year-to-date — but 
still has a long way to go before recapturing the heights attained in June. 
With AI regulation risks looming in Washington, the rebound could be hard to 
sustain. HSBC’s Alastair Pinder argues that the reaction to the midterms so far 
has been overdone. Even in a worst-case scenario, he argues, tighter oversight 
would slow AI capital expenditure rather than completely derail it. That should 
limit the damage to emerging markets.

However, Oxford Economics’ Sergi Lanau argues that EM’s growing tech-related 
exports are a major source of vulnerability. Exports reversed following the 
dot-com bust, and another sudden unwinding in tech stocks could trigger a 
similar reckoning. Lanau bases his doomsday scenario on analysis showing that 
the rise in AI-related imports dwarfs the boom a quarter-century ago, even 
after adjusting for differences in gross domestic product:

Any such bust would be sharp, judging by the 55% drop in US IT imports after 
the dot-com bubble, while the rise in AI-related trade has been rapid:
EM domestic value added embedded in US AI-related imports is rising, and the 
experience in 2001-2002 suggests that it could contract quickly. As in the 
dot-com bubble, permanent increases in domestic value added tied to the US AI 
industry appear feasible, but sharp short-term swings are unavoidable if the AI 
cycle turns.
Against this, the midterms have also brought hopes of a ceasefire in the 
US-Iran conflict and a sustained decline in oil prices. That would be very 
welcome, and Brent crude dropped a further 5.5% on Monday. But oil-importing 
emerging markets like India have been here before. Investors will need more 
assurances before venturing back.

—Richard Abbey
