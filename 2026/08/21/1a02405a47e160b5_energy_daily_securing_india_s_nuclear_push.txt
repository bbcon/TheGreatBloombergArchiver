Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
India-based energy reporter Rajesh Kumar Singh explores why the liberalization 
of the country’s nuclear power sector is raising security questions — and 
what’s being done to address them.

India is opening up 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-12-18/india-overhauls-nuclear-law-to-allow-private-investment?cmpid=BBBXT082126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8032>
 its nuclear industry to private firms, ending a long-held state monopoly in 
pursuit of greater energy security. In his recent Independence Day speech, 
Prime Minister Narendra Modilaid out plans 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-15/india-sets-draft-rules-to-open-nuclear-power-to-private-firms?cmpid=BBBXT082126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8032>
 to start five reactors within six to seven years.

While safety is front of mind for any nuclear power plant, close inspection of 
draft guidelines 
<https://cdnbbsr.s3waas.gov.in/s35b8e4fd39d9786228649a8a8bec4e008/uploads/2026/08/202608141057146106.pdf>
 published last week — a 93-page document — reveals a conspicuous focus on 
managing risk. That emphasis may offer clues to how the country will prioritize 
the industry’s growth.

Any private company with ambitions to build a reactor should deliver a system 
capable of handling any emergency, the guidelines say.

Access to every facility must be restricted, with a no-fly zone in the skies 
above, while trucks carrying nuclear fuel should be tracked at all times. The 
draft also emphasizes the need for cybersecurity (Kudankulam Nuclear Power 
Plant, the country’s largest, was subject to amalware attack 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2019-10-31/india-says-nuclear-power-plant-was-affected-by-computer-malware?cmpid=BBBXT082126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8032>
 in 2019).

This focus on security 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-19/hype-around-small-nuclear-is-overblown-indian-official-says?cmpid=BBBXT082126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8032>
 seeks to address concerns that inexperienced operators, or a piecemeal 
approach to expansion, could leave facilities vulnerable to accidents, natural 
disasters or evendeliberate attacks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-18/how-drone-strike-near-uae-s-barakah-plant-shows-nuclear-sites-vulnerability?cmpid=BBBXT082126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8032>
 such as those seen inUkraine 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-30/tanker-catches-fire-in-drone-attack-on-russia-s-taganrog-port?cmpid=BBBXT082126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8032>
, Iran and theUnited Arab Emirates 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-05/nuclear-watchdog-eyes-stronger-reactor-defenses-after-uae-attack?cmpid=BBBXT082126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8032>
.
The Kudankulam facility in Tamil Nadu state is India’s largest nuclear power 
plant.Photographer: Prashanth Vishwanathan/Bloomberg
That’s turned the spotlight on small modular reactors that can be made in 
India andinstalled <bbg://news/stories/SU5A7VDWX2PS> on existing industrial 
premises to provide captive power.

The catch is that smaller facilities are perceived as less secure because they 
lack the formidable exclusion zones that divide state-run plants from the 
outside world. Emphasizing this risk, prominent nuclear scientist Ravi B. Grover
said 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-19/hype-around-small-nuclear-is-overblown-indian-official-says?cmpid=BBBXT082126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8032>
 in November that India “cannot scatter our nuclear assets all around the 
country.”

BloombergNEF nuclear analyst Chris Gadomski <bbg://people/profile/16601286> 
notes the physical security of any asset comes at a cost. “The smaller the 
nuclear plant becomes, the bigger the cost of protecting it gets in proportion 
to its energy output,” he said on the sidelines of theBNEF Summit New Delhi 
<https://about.bnef.com/events/summit-agenda-new-delhi-2026/>.

But security worries won’t spell the end for SMRs in India. On the contrary, 
the government expects these reactors to supplyaround a 10th 
<https://cea.nic.in/wp-content/uploads/notification/2025/10/Roadmap_Final_30062025.pdf>
 of its 100-gigawatt target by 2047.

Comparatively low labor costs will help to keep expenses under control, 
Gadomski said. Developers will just have a high bar to clear when it comes to 
safety.

—Rajesh Kumar Singh, Bloomberg News
