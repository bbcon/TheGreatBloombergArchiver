Inhuman Bondage

Another day, another landmark in the bond market 
<https://www.ft.com/content/8ea6e6f2-1cfb-11dc-9b58-000b5df10621>. The 10-year 
Treasury now yields 5.12%, after leaping 14 basis points Wednesday, while the 
five-year version also now yields above 5% for the first time since 2007:

The catalyst was clear: S&P Global publishes purchasing manager indexes, which 
came out Wednesday. The composite number for the US, combining the 
manufacturing and services sector, was the best since the post-pandemic boom 
and way ahead of expectations:

With business activity rising like that, a recession seems a minimal concern, 
while last week’s contentious decision by the Federal Reserve to raise 
overnight rates looks vindicated. More to the point, the case for further 
tightening ahead looks stronger. At the start of this year, fed funds futures 
put the likely terminal rate (which can be proxied by the implicit rate for 
October next year) at about 3%. It’s now jumped to 4.75%:

The rise in bond yields 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/biggest-long-bond-etf-hits-record-low-as-traders-dump-treasuries?cmpid=BBD092426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260924&utm_content=9239>
 looks alarming and has had a horrible effect on US mortgages, with 30-year 
fixed rates back above 7%. But the evidence is that the overall rise in yields 
is more a response to a “good” rise in growth, rather than to other widely 
cited potential causes. Consumers’ inflation concerns have risen since the Iran 
war drove up oil prices nearly seven months ago, but longer-term market-based 
inflation expectations have barely budged. Yields can be broken into real and 
inflation-driven components using the gap between fixed income and 
inflation-protected bonds (TIPS). Inflation breakevens barely budged over the 
last five years; real yields are now higher and account for all of the rise of 
the last five years:

To illustrate that differently, this chart shows that negative real yields 
drove the exceptionally easy financing conditions of 2021. That’s now totally 
reversed:

Rising bond yields directly raise government borrowing costs, and the US 
fiscal deficit topped $40 trillion last month. But judging by the amount of 
press coverage from all sources appearing on the Bloomberg terminal, concerns 
about the issue aren’t particularly elevated:

The Institute of International Finance in its annual global debt monitor 
<https://www.iif.com/Products/Global-Debt-Monitor> — coincidentally published 
Wednesday — cast some light on how the calm has continued. Critically, 
inflation has been governments’ friend, chipping away at the level of 
outstanding debt as a proportion of gross domestic product, even as overall 
nominal debt levels hit new records:

The IIF also dismissed several other theories behind the backup in Treasury 
yields. First, foreign demand for US corporate debt and equities remains very 
strong. Purchases of Treasuries have been more lackluster, but there’s been no 
great exodus:

There’s also surprisingly little evidence that the phenomenal debt issuance to 
fund the artificial intelligence buildout has crowded out Treasuries. The IIF 
illustrates that extra issuance has been massive, dominated by US groups:

Vishwas Patkar, head of US credit strategy at Morgan Stanley, argued that AI 
issuance would move toward chip financing, which tends to come from a different 
segment of the credit market as it generally amortizes more quickly than 
government finance. For now, issuance was “largely coming from mega caps” that 
generate plentiful cash and can issue a lot more without harming their credit 
ratings. “The problem is more market capacity; can all this issuance be 
handled?”

This doesn’t mean that government yields can keep rising without creating 
problems. Guneet Dhingra, head of US rates strategy at BNP Paribas, argues:
Contrary to a popular narrative, fiscal deficits have played almost no part at 
all. If fiscal concerns re-enter market discussion (and we think they are about 
to), higher term premiums could drive the next leg higher for long-end yields.
For now, he said that conversations with clients revealed “an apathy to 
deficits as a ‘known’ and ‘priced’ story.” The problem, however, is that the 
actual government debt burden will grow directly as a result of the Fed’s 
tightening cycle, as the Treasury is increasingly borrowing via short-term 
T-bills. For the IIF, interest expense has risen far more sharply in the 
developed markets than for emerging markets or China:

A further issue comes from US politics. The conventional wisdom is that bond 
markets like political gridlock, as it tends to limit governments from doing 
anything irresponsible. A Democratic-controlled Congress is unlikely to allow 
any more tax cuts. But the risk is that policymaking could grow incoherent 
after a Blue Wave, bringing with it a risk of worsening deficits. Prediction 
markets have moved sharply toward pricing a “blue wave” sweep of both chambers 
in the last week:

A further problem is that while yields have been driven up by strong growth — 
which makes it easy for the US to finance — those higher borrowing costs will 
make life much harder for countries that already have heavy indebtedness. 
Japanese markets were closed for holidays, but 10-year Japanese government bond 
yields leapt 8.8 basis points to a new 30-year high high of 3.04% as soon as 
Thursday trading started.

Wednesday’s moves in peripheral European economies suggested a reprise of the 
sovereign debt crisis 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2025-08-22/who-lost-europe-hint-it-s-not-trump-s-fault?cmpid=BBD092426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260924&utm_content=9239>
 that rocked the euro zone from 2010 to 2012. French 10-year yields jumped 15.4 
basis points Wednesday, putting government finances to the most difficult test 
since that crisis:

Meanwhile 10-year yields in Italy and Greece rose 14.7 and 14.8 basis points 
respectively. That has very unpleasant echoes.

Despite appearances, the backup in US yields so far is not due to anything 
sinister. But it could yet have very negative effects on anyone whose fiscal 
house is not in order.

UnEMphatic Display

One more consequence of higher Treasury yields: Emerging markets’ rainy days 
are here. The Fed’s return to policy tightening will now test the resilience of 
EM central banks, and has already caused emergingcurrencies to sink 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-09-23/em-currencies-extend-decline-on-us-data-strong-dollar-video?cmpid=BBD092426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260924&utm_content=9239>
 to their lowest since March.

This pain is likely to prod central banks into action. So far, South Africa’s 
Reserve Bank 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/south-africa-central-bank-hikes-rates-as-iran-war-fans-inflation?cmpid=BBD092426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260924&utm_content=9239>
 has followed the Fed’s lead, noting rising price pressures had led to an 
upward adjustment of its inflation target. Whether they need to go further, and 
whether other central banks join in, will largely depend on the pain they can 
tolerate from the Middle East fallout:

With Wednesday’s leap in yields pushing the dollar higher, while Brent crude 
surged back above $100 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-sept-24?cmpid=BBD092426_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260924&utm_content=9239>
 per barrel, rate hikes in the developing world look like only a matter of 
time. Until a breakthrough in peace talks, elevated oil prices will weigh on 
net energy importers. The question is how much the rate outlook will impact 
emerging markets bonds. So far this year, their higher yields have kept the 
total returns on emerging government debt very competitive:

In recent months, EM hard-currency debt has been the standout performer in 
global fixed income, holding up through tariff shocks and the Middle East 
conflict. But the latest upward adjustment in rate expectations and bond yields 
presents a tougher test: not through spreads, which are still tightening, but 
through the strengthening dollar, which often inflicts pain on holders of EM 
local currency debt. A recent survey by HSBC, conducted before the Fed’s 
announcement last week, showed continuing optimism:

Investors have not (yet) soured on the asset class. Instead, HSBC’s Ali 
Cakiroglu argues that softening sentiment is coinciding with a greater 
willingness to take risks:
The combination of stronger risk appetite and greater dry powder could support 
EM if the right catalysts emerge. The macro backdrop looks more supportive, 
although inflation concerns remain elevated.
Robeco’s Anton Eser takes a similar position, arguing that a base-case of 
global expansion and investment-led growth supports sovereign credit quality. 
Current spreads, however, leave little room for disappointment.

EM equities, which typically benefit from a weaker greenback, could be 
particularly threatened by higher US rates — but Eser contends that developing 
markets might actually benefit as the rest of the world attempts to reduce 
dependence on the US:
In a world in which globalization does not disappear but reorganizes around 
security, particularly energy security, emerging markets could continue to 
outperform, in our view. Our trade rewiring metric shows that a high degree of 
ex-ante trade fragmentation points to lower US excess returns and stronger 
ex-US equity returns in the next five years, notably for emerging markets.
For all the talk about EM equities’ resilience, it’s notable that the 
performance is largely concentrated in a handful of countries benefiting from 
semiconductor manufacturing or higher commodity prices, particularly for oil 
and base metals. Exclude Asia, and EM largely matches developed markets (and 
the US when tech is omitted), but no more:

These idiosyncratic drivers are useful in masking the broader challenges — 
including political risk, heavy commodity dependence, and weak consumer demand 
— hiding in plain sight.

—Richard Abbey
