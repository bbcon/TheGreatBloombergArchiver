Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporters Peter Millard and Mariana Durao look at what Brazil’s presidential 
election means for Petrobras.

Flávio Bolsonaro’s surprise lead 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/bolsonaro-leads-lula-in-brazil-runoff-after-first-round-surprise?cmpid=BBBXT101526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10132>
 in the first round of Brazil’s presidential election raises the prospect that 
the nation’s state-controlled oil company could embark on a sweeping asset sale.

The right-wing senator is widely expected to push for more private-sector 
involvement in Brazil’s oil industry and revivepolicies pursued 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2021-09-27/bolsonaro-minister-pledges-petrobras-privatization-in-10-years?cmpid=BBBXT101526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10132>
 when his father, Jair Bolsonaro, was president. That’s apt to include selling 
refineries and aging oil fields held by Petroleo Brasileiro SA.

The big question is whether Petrobras will try to sell its controlling stake 
in Braskem SA, Latin America’s largest petrochemical producer, which has a 
current market value of around $250 million. The business has been left deeply 
indebted by a global downturn in the industry and an environmental disaster 
caused by itssalt mines 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-12-21/brazil-police-sparks-probe-on-braskem-as-mine-risks-to-collapse?cmpid=BBBXT101526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10132>
 in northeastern Brazil.

Braskem is in the midst of negotiations with creditors, who have been pushing 
Petrobras to commit to injecting capital into the company to help avoid 
bankruptcy.
The Braskem SA petrochemical plant in Duque de Caxias, Rio de Janeiro state.
Photographer: Dado Galdieri/Bloomberg
“What I would expect from a Flávio government in the oil sector is a focus on 
profitability” and “the divestment of non-core assets,” said Décio Oddone, who 
had a stint running Brazil’s oil regulator under Jair Bolsonaro. “That would 
put Petrobras back on the course it was on.”

Oddone, who is also a former chief executive officer of Rio de Janeiro-based 
Brava Energia, said the Bahia cluster of oil assets in the Recôncavo Basin and 
the Urucu field in the Amazon rainforest are among those likely to be on the 
block.

Possible buyers are independent Brazilian producers including PRIO SA and 
PetroReconcavo SA. Both snatched up assets during the elder Bolsonaro’s 
presidency, when Petrobras sold oil fields, pipelines and refineries worth 
billions of dollars.

It’s more complicated to make an educated guess about who might be interested 
in Petrobras’s Braskem stake. It took the Brazilian industrial conglomerate 
Novonor SA more than a decade to find a buyer before it finallysold its 38% 
stake 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-20/braskem-majority-stake-sale-by-novonor-advances-with-signed-deal?cmpid=BBBXT101526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10132>
 in the chemical producer to private equity firm IG4 earlier this year.

Brazilian presidential terms, meanwhile, are four years.

—Peter Millard and Mariana Durao, Bloomberg News
