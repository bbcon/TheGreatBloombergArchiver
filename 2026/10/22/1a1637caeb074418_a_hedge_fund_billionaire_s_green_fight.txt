A storm over a port

By Devon Pendleton 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQdUWe1dz2M/devon-pendleton?cmpid=BBD102226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10398>
 andFabiano Maisonnave 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXmFWA_HFBY/fabiano-maisonnave?cmpid=BBD102226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10398>

To hedge-fund billionaire Louis Bacon, the crystalline waters and densely 
forested riverways of the Gulf of Chiriqui in western Panama are an ecological 
oasis. To developer John McKeown, the gulf is a conduit to potential markets 
for the region’s prized produce and a boon for its economy.

Those clashing visions are at the heart of a bitter dispute between the two 
wealthy Americans, who both say they’re championing Panama’s best interests.
The Chorcha River near the Gulf of Chiriqui, close to the site of the planned 
port.Photographer: Tarina Rodriguez/Bloomberg
The feud centers on a $260 million port McKeown is seeking to develop on the 
country’s Pacific coast, just south of the city of David. Ocean Pacific 
Investments, the company McKeown leads, describes the project, Puerto Baru, as 
Panama’s first “green” port that will help relieve pressure on trading hubs in 
Panama City and integrate with trade routes to North America and Asia. 
Chiriqui, with a population of around 500,000, is Panama’s breadbasket, 
cultivating foods including coffee, papayas and shrimp.

The battle has escalated all the way to Panama’s Supreme Court, which is 
weighing the port’s environmental approval after a group of local nonprofits 
challenged it. Already, the fight has involved civil and criminal complaints 
against some of those NGOs. The outcome will dictate the fate of an unspoiled 
pocket of Panama that’s seen little of the wealth that flows through its 
high-flying capital.

To Bacon, the project poses an unacceptable risk.

“I am concerned that the principals behind this project have no experience 
developing or operating commercial ports, yet they are proposing a major port 
in one of the most environmentally sensitive areas in Panama,” he wrote in a 
statement to Bloomberg News. “A simple look at the map would tell you this is 
not a suitable development site.”

Ocean Pacific, for its part, said that it has addressed potential 
environmental impacts and that the campaign to stop the port may be motivated 
by personal interests.

“If I believed Puerto Baru would cause significant harm to a part of Panama, a 
country I not only love but call home, I would never have committed myself or 
my resources to developing this project,” McKeown said in a statement.

The site at issue is nested among the serpentine channels that hold a quarter 
of Panama’s mangrove forests. Mangrove trees and their dense root systems 
fortify coastlines, shelter sea life and suck up enormous amounts of carbon.

Ocean Pacific envisages a 124-hectare (306-acre) commercial port. It would be 
the biggest private investment in Chiriqui Province’s history, Ocean Pacific 
says, and the area would benefit not just from an increase in industry but also 
more tourism, since there will be a dock for leisure vessels. An economic 
assessment commissioned by the company in 2021 estimated that the project could 
create upwards of 1,100 jobs.

Six Panamanian environmental groups say the facility, and the resulting 
cargo-ship traffic, would irreversibly damage the local ecosystem through 
pollution, water churn and blocked sunlight. Juan Carlos Monterrey Gómez, chief 
executive officer of environmental consultancy VANGLabs and Panama’s former 
special representative for climate change, said some impact on the local 
environment from a project of this size is inevitable. (Monterrey isn’t 
affiliated with any of the groups opposing it.)

A spokesman for Ocean Pacific said the company “strongly disagrees” that the 
port will cause irreparable damage and said potential impacts were “studied 
extensively and addressed in detail” as part of the environmental review, which 
was approved two years ago and was comparable, he said, to reviews conducted 
for US infrastructure projects. The analysis took four years, according to 
Ocean Pacific, and included a well-attended public hearing. A website for the 
project describes it as designed to meet “high sustainability standards,” with 
solar panels for all its power needs and dedicated conservation zones.

Texas floods ruling

By Steven Church 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AOY9reFKwsk/steven-church?cmpid=BBD102226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10398>

Camp Mystic, the Texas girls camp where 28 people were killed in flash floods 
last summer, must have a new management team with the power to settle lawsuits 
filed against the company, replacing the family blamed for the deaths, a judge 
ruled.

US Bankruptcy Judge Christopher Lopez said that if the family of Richard 
Eastland, who co-owned and ran the company before he died in the flood, does 
not agree to give power to settle the lawsuits to an “independent fiduciary” 
the court will impose a government-approved trustee to run Camp Mystic.
Camp Mystic in Hunt, Texas.Photographer: Ashley Landis/AP Photo
Lopez rejected two competing proposals for how to manage the 
conflicts-of-interest involving Eastland family members who manage the camp. 
The Eastland side had proposed a new independent director who had limited 
authority over whether to settle lawsuits filed against the company. Creditors 
had asked for the Eastlands to be replaced by a so-called Chapter 11 Trustee.

Lopez gave the Eastland family until Oct. 28 to consider the court’s 
requirements for a new independent director. If they don’t agree, he said he 
will appoint a trustee
