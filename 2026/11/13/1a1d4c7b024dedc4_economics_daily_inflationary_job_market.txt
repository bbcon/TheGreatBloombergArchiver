Jobs and Inflation

Fed policymakers have, for some time now, viewed the US job market as not 
something they needed to worry about in terms of inflationary pressures. But 
data suggest that may change, making this a potential new risk for 2027.

Chairman Kevin Warsh has repeatedly described the job market as balanced. In 
his keynote Jackson Holespeech 
<https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/warsh20260828a.htm> in 
August, he observed that, “when labor supply is barely growing, monthly job 
gains are naturally going to run low.”

At the time, the three-month average for payroll increases was running at just 
20,000 — a fraction of the 122,000 clocked back in 2024. But the October jobs 
report brought this metric up tosome 137,000 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-06/us-adds-165-000-jobs-above-all-forecasts-as-unemployment-falls?cmpid=BBD111326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261113&utm_content=11324>
 — well over double the figure economists generally agree is needed to match 
the rise in labor supply.

Another report this month showed that the “quits rate,” which former Fed Chair 
Janet Yellen favored as ameasure of confidence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-03/us-job-openings-jump-to-highest-level-since-may-layoffs-fall?cmpid=BBD111326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261113&utm_content=11324>
 in the labor market, had climbed to 2.1% in September. This is the share of 
workers voluntarily leaving their jobs. It’s now back to the 2024 average, 
suggesting that angst over federal government job cuts and AI may be waning.

On Thursday, New York Fed President John Williams recognized this improvement 
in the data. “The US economy not only continues to grow, but actuallyseems to 
be strengthening 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-13/fed-s-williams-says-inflation-far-too-high-still-work-to-do?cmpid=BBD111326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261113&utm_content=11324>
 — we’re seeing that also in the labor market,” he said. Williams even sought 
to reassure: “we’re not seeing signs of the economy overheating.” Which itself 
shows how the debate may be shifting.

Warsh pointed out that labor supply hasn’t been growing much. That’s the 
consequence, of course, of Trump’s crackdown on immigration, along with subdued 
gains in the native-born population. Assuming that doesn’t change, the current 
pace of hiring would steadily cause the job market to tighten.

“Few idle folks are available to put to work to make more GDP,” as Carl 
Weinberg, chief economist at High Frequency Economics, put it earlier this 
month. The October data suggest “employment demand growth is pressing even 
harder against an immutable labor force that is shrinking. Pay increases have 
to follow. The Fed should be watching this!”

Need-to-Know Research

The US isn’t the only place where electricity is in high demand. In Southeast 
Asia, it has little to do with AI, and is more a reflection of urbanization and 
the demands from the rising number of middle-class households. The Asian 
Development Bank this week put a giant price tag on what’s needed.

The region’s infrastructure needs have more than doubled to $7.1 trillion for 
2026-2040, the ADB said. Power accounts for half of that, followed by transport 
at 35%. “The energy and transport systems that underpin production come first, 
with digital and water services keeping pace with the growth those systems 
support,” ADB economists wrote in apaper 
<http://adb.org/sites/default/files/publication/1189626/adb-brief-418-7-trillion-challenge-building-tomorrow-asean.pdf>
 this week.

A power build-out — including diversified supply, renewable generation, 
transmission, storage and cross-border trade — is crucial not just to meet 
Southeast Asia’s growing demand but also ensure security. The disruptions to 
energy supplies in the wake of the Iran war have underlined how quickly 
infrastructure gaps can become economic threats, the ADB said.
