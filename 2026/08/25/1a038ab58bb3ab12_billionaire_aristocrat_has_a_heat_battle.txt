Luxury London’s heat challenge

By Olivia Rudgard 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWBnLoYN_tg/olivia-rudgard?cmpid=BBD082526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260825&utm_content=8144>

Grosvenor Group Holdings Ltd. says the combination of rising temperatures and 
antiquated planning regulations threatens to dent the appeal of itsvast 
portfolio of properties 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-25/billionaire-duke-s-firm-has-heat-warning-for-london-luxury-homes?cmpid=BBD082526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260825&utm_content=8144>
 across Mayfair and Belgravia.

The group, which is owned by billionaire Hugh Grosvenor, the 7th Duke of 
Westminster and Britain’s richest aristocrat, is trying to persuade the 
government to relax what the firm views as unnecessarilyrigid planning 
requirements for listed properties 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-25/billionaire-duke-s-firm-has-heat-warning-for-london-luxury-homes?cmpid=BBD082526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260825&utm_content=8144>
. That’s as homeowners in some of Britain’s most coveted postcodes struggle to 
get approvals to add basic cooling features such as awnings and shutters.
Eaton Square, part of the Grosvenor property portfolio.Source: Thomas Graham
“If we don’t adapt our buildings and the planning system doesn’t adapt, then 
people aren’t going to want to live and spend time in these buildings,” said 
Tor Burrows, Grosvenor’s chief sustainability officer.

A desire to protect the look of Britain’s listed buildings risks colliding 
with the need to make those same properties fit for a hotter climate. The UK’s 
Met Office said in July that the country faces more frequent and intense heat 
waves, with temperatures as high as 40C (104F) now a much stronger possibility. 
It’s a prognosis that carries profound implications for a housing stock 
designed for a cooler climate.

In Kensington and Chelsea, for example, adding a new awning if there’s no 
awning box on the outside of a building, or adding shutters, can only be done 
with the council’s permission. “Applying for planning permission ensures that 
what is installed is appropriate for the building and does not harm the 
character or appearance of conservation areas,” the council said in a statement.

Grosvenor won some concessions for its Eaton Square properties earlier this 
year, after the Westminster City Council agreed to create a more streamlined 
process for homeowners looking to install cooling features. The details of that 
change are still being worked out and it will likely be implemented later this 
year. But the group says this doesn’t go nearly far enough.
Terraced residential properties in the Mayfair district in London.
Photographer: Chris Ratcliffe/Bloomberg
Burrows points to research from 2022 showing that 87% of historic building 
owners “see the planning system as a major barrier” to adding features that 
would help them cope with rising temperatures or reduce carbon emissions. The 
result is that many now assume there’s no point in trying, she said.

“People have maybe dipped their toe in or tried to experience it and then 
found it so complicated that then they sort of give up trying to adapt the 
building,” Burrows said.

Grosvenor is now seeking planning concessions from the national government for 
its listed properties. The changes would apply to Mayfair and Belgravia, where 
many residents have lived through recent heat waves without access to built-in 
air conditioning or awnings to shield them from the sun, as well as to listed 
buildings across England.

A spokesperson for the UK’s ministry for housing, communities and local 
government said that “appropriate shading measures in listed buildings can be 
supported providing they do not disproportionately harm the building.”

The new ways to fight fire

By Matthew Hart 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXe4QBnIUqk/matthew-hart?cmpid=BBD082526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260825&utm_content=8144>

Max Brodie was 12 years old when a lightning strike near his hometown of 
Kelowna, British Columbia, ignited a wildfire that a thousand firefighters and 
a fleet of waterbombers couldn’t stop. As hot ash filled the sky, the boy 
scrambled up a ladder to help his father anchor soaker hoses to the roof to 
keep it wet. The house survived, but 33,000 residents, including Brodie, were 
evacuated during that2003 firestorm 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-24/wildfire-fighting-technology-evolves-with-driverless-drones-and-ai-tools?cmpid=BBD082526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260825&utm_content=8144>
.

Fifteen years later, he was living in San Francisco and wondering what to do 
after selling his second software startup when, 180 miles to the north, another 
monster burst from the forest. That blaze killed 85 people, reduced Paradise, 
California, to ashes, and awakened his boyhood trauma. “I thought, ‘Oh, my 
gosh, nothing’s changed,’” he says. Now he knewwhat to do 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-24/wildfire-fighting-technology-evolves-with-driverless-drones-and-ai-tools?cmpid=BBD082526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260825&utm_content=8144>
.
Brodie, center, during a demonstration. Source: TJ Romero, Courtesy Rain
The next year, Brodie and two partners formed Rain Industries Inc. to develop 
the software an autonomous aircraft would need to find a fire and put it out 
before it spread. Four years of research and development and a couple of 
funding rounds later, Rain teamed up with the Sikorsky unit of Lockheed Martin 
Corp., the world’s largest defense contractor, to help turn a Black Hawk 
helicopter into an autonomous firefighting machine, using artificial 
intelligence to deliver the quick response that is firefighting’s Holy Grail.
