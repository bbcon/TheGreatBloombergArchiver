Trump’s new methane rollback

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD102726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10611>
 andZahra Hirji 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVypnhGtqak/zahra-hirji?cmpid=BBD102726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10611>

President Donald Trump is set to unveil a plan to dial back federal 
requirements designed to slash methane emissions from oil and gas operations, 
casting the proposal as a way to encourage more domestic energy production and 
lower costs.

Trump is expected to announce the proposed rule during a campaign rally in 
Lubbock, Texas, on Tuesday, as he tries to bolster the prospects of Republican 
Senate candidate Ken Paxton in next week’s midterm elections, according to an 
administration official who asked not to be named before a formal announcement.
Pumpjacks in the Belridge oil field near McKittrick, California, in November 
2021.Photographer: Mario Tama/Getty Images North America
Texas is the No. 1 oil-producing state in the US — and the petroleum industry 
has lobbied the White House to ease some of the mandates meant to keep a lid on 
releases of methane, a particularly potent greenhouse gas.

The proposal comes as Trump seeks to assuage voter concerns about higher costs 
for oil, gasoline and diesel stoked by Russia’s war on Ukraine and the US war 
against Iran. During a pre-election campaign blitz to keep Republican control 
of Congress, Trump has also championed initiatives tailor-made for his rally 
audiences — from plans forVolkswagen AG 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/VOW:GR?cmpid=BBD102726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10611>
 to build a $4.2 billion Audi plant in Georgia to an $8 billion fuel-relief 
program pitched to farmers in Kansas.

The Environmental Protection Agency proposal takes aim at Biden administration 
requirements 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-12-02/biden-delivers-cop28-crackdown-on-methane-leaks-from-oil-wells?cmpid=BBD102726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10611>
 that forced energy companies to replace leak-prone equipment and regularly 
search for escaping gas from wells and other hardware. EPA Administrator Lee 
Zeldin, at an industry conference in Santa Fe earlier this month,said 
<https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/administrator-zeldin-announces-epa-days-away-unveiling-next-oil-gas-proposal?utm_source=chatgpt.com>
 the planned measure would lift “unnecessary burdens” constraining oil and gas 
production and make it less costly for Americans to heat their homes and drive 
their cars.

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/trump-epa-readies-bid-to-weaken-methane-controls-for-oil-gas-companies?cmpid=BBD102726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10611>

In a major shift, the agency will propose to effectively single out so-called 
marginal, low-output wells for additional regulatory relief. The treatment 
would extend to wells that produce no more than 15 barrels of oil equivalent 
per day.

The approach responds to pleas from some smaller, independent oil producers 
that say the cost of complying with the requirements can overwhelm the 
economics of operating older wells, effectively forcing them to shut production 
at the sites.

The bulk of the roughly 1 million oil and gas wells in the US — about 79% — 
are considered marginal because of their relatively low production. But former 
EPA officials and environmental activists have warned that wells with low oil 
and gas production can be a major source of methane — and any retreat from the 
federal requirements would translate to more of that potent planet-warming 
pollution at a time whenclimate impacts are intensifying

Potential savings

$45 billion

The EPA estimates the proposal overall would yield some $45 billion in savings 
while enabling production of an additional 210 million barrels of oil and 1.41 
trillion cubic feet of natural gas between 2027 and 2050.

A pre-COP31 global report card

By Jennifer A. Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD102726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10611>

A series of new United Nations reports delivers a mixed verdict on the state 
of climate diplomacy and the world’s work to combat global warming less than 
two weeks before annual negotiations begin in Turkey.

The assessments published Tuesday ahead of the COP31 summit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/latest/cop-climate-summit?cmpid=BBD102726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10611>
 show that the architecture of the landmark 2015 Paris Agreement is working — 
driving countries to implement emissions-cutting policies, report on their 
progress and embed climate considerations into broader decision making.

That’s helped bend the emissions curve, with UN Climate Change projecting that 
if countries fulfill their existing pollution-reducing pledges, greenhouse gas 
releases will be pared roughly 6% by 2035, from 2019.

And yet, none of that is going far — or fast — enough, given the scale of the 
threat.

The projected emissions reduction by 2035 is an order of magnitude below the 
60% drop the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change said was actually needed 
to keep planetary warming below 1.5C — the most ambitious target of the Paris 
accord, and one that the UN Environment Programme acknowledged last week is no 
longer achievable.

“This year’s reports demonstrate again that collective progress remains 
clearly insufficient, that global transitions are very uneven, and that many 
countries face substantial barriers to delivering on their targets and 
pledges,” said UN Climate Change Executive Secretary Simon Stiell.

UNEP’s assessment last week concluded 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/un-warns-major-emissions-cuts-needed-to-limit-warming-overshoot?cmpid=BBD102726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261027&utm_content=10611>
 the world will overshoot 1.5C and stressed the focus now must be on limiting 
the scope and duration of the period of warming above that level.
COP31 signage near the Antalya Expo venue in Antalya, Turkey on Sept. 30.
Photographer: Goksel Yapar/AFP/Getty Images
The new analyses from the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change include an 
assessment of biennial transparency reports that reflects what happened from 
2020 to 2022, a study of what countries have pledged to do under their 
nationally determined contributions and an adaptation plan progress report that 
highlights the difficulties in financing projects that can make a difference.

Taken together, they affirm the feedback loop of the Paris Agreement — with 
pledges for action followed by reports on progress and then collective 
assessments, that in turn drive a cycle of increased national efforts.

However, they also lay out the gap between the degree of progress that’s being 
made and the scale of action that’s necessary.

That underscores a theme raised by officials ahead of the climate talks 
opening Nov. 9 in the resort city of Antalya — that rather than debate the gap 
in ambition, an issue that’s long been a lament of the annual summits, 
attendees need to zero in on the implementation and delivery gap instead.

A few key takeaways from the Tuesday reports:

 * Nationally determined contributions are becoming bigger and better: The 
UNFCCC assessed 127 new Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) spanning 
about 70% of global greenhouse gas emissions. They’re more comprehensive, and 
reflect more than just top-down government action, instead showing how climate 
action is being embedded across societies and economies.

 * The Paris process works: Some 90% of parties said the “global stocktake” — 
an assessment of worldwide progress on climate action — informed their pledges 
on emissions reduction. After the first stocktake concluded in 2023, a second 
multiyear review will begin at COP31.

 * Potential for more progress: Current pledges would cut global emissions 
around 6% by 2035 below 2019 levels. Yet, that could be as much as 9% if those 
existing commitments are fully implemented — including conditional promises. 
For countries that aren’t Paris Agreement parties, including the US, global 
emissions calculations reflected modeling of existing policies.

 * The adaptation challenge: Some 81 developing country parties submitted 
national adaptation plans by Oct. 20. But the main bottleneck in implementing 
those blueprints is moving from a plan to a fundable proposal, according to 
UNFCCC.
