Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
senior oil reporter Alex Longley explores the possible repercussions of a US 
ban on diesel exports.

President Donald Trump has confirmed what the oil industry has been quietly 
fretting about for weeks: He’spushing for a ban 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-22/trump-says-he-s-pushed-for-fuel-export-ban-amid-price-crunch?cmpid=BBBXT092326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9182>
 on US diesel exports.

If such curbs came into effect — and at this stage that’s still a big if — the 
ramifications for global and US fuel markets would be enormous.

America is the world’s biggest exporter of middle distillates, a category of 
fuels that includes diesel and jet fuel.

Rewind to before the Iran war, and a ranking of other major exporters shows 
why the global market now faces the issues it does, with Saudi Arabia, Russia, 
Kuwait and the United Arab Emirates all in the top 10.

All of those last four countries have seen their shipments hit by conflict 
this year, whether that’sattacks on refineries 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-18/iran-war-drives-bigger-diesel-supply-loss-despite-trump-s-claims?cmpid=BBBXT092326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9182>
 in the Middle East and blockades of the Strait of Hormuz, or Ukrainian strikes 
on Russia.
Smoke rises from the area of an oil refinery on the outskirts of Moscow on 
June 18, 2026.Photographer: AFP via Getty Images
In the past year, the three biggest buyers of American supplies were Mexico, 
Chile and Brazil, Kpler data show. But an export ban would also be felt in 
Europe, a major importing region where the US is the single largest supplier.

Again, look down the list at backup options from previous years and you have 
Russia, Saudi Arabia and Kuwait.

It goes without saying that a ban would starve the world outside the US of a 
vital fuel at a time when it’s most needed. But there’s no certainty it would 
solve all of theproblems domestically 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-21/us-diesel-tops-6-50-a-gallon-as-wars-worsen-global-fuels-crunch?cmpid=BBBXT092326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9182>
, either.

With America holding on to its fuel, storage would fill up along the Gulf 
Coast. Refineries would start scaling back production, affecting other fuels 
besides diesel and ultimately spurring higher prices across the board, analysts 
say.

None of this is good news for economies worldwide. Central banks are already 
closely watching fuel costs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-09/oil-s-crack-spread-captivates-central-bankers-fretting-inflation?cmpid=BBBXT092326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9182>
 and ballooning crack spreads — the premium of refined products over crude — as 
they grapple to tame inflation.

And it won’t be welcomed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-22/refining-stocks-rally-stalls-as-talk-of-diesel-export-ban-swirls?cmpid=BBBXT092326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9182>
 by the industry either.

“Restricting exports is not a solution to high prices,” the American Petroleum 
Institute says. “Removing US diesel from the market could instead result in 
reduced refinery runs, global economic damage and even higher US prices.”

—Alex Longley, Bloomberg News

Liquefied natural gas deliveries into mainland China more than doubled last 
week, reaching the highest in almost two years, according toBloombergNEF 
<https://about.bnef.com/>. The rebound may partly reflect demand for

Russia was the largest supplier, followed by Australia and Malaysia.
