Diesel prices have soared globally amid disruptions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-oct-7?cmpid=BBD100726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9712>
 in the Strait of Hormuz and Ukrainian attacks onRussian refineries 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/russia-s-oil-export-boom-undercut-by-payouts-diesel-sales-ban?cmpid=BBD100726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9712>
, hitting the agriculture industry especially hard from Victoria, Australia, to 
Alberta, Canada.

Farmers in some regions face fuel costs that are more than 50% higher than 
last year. Diesel a major expense especially when planters and harvesters head 
into the fields. There’s growing uncertainty about how tight energy supplies 
might get during this production season and next.

The squeeze is threatening to stoke a new wave of food inflation — kicking off 
a cycle that sustains higher prices if production costs get too expensive and 
output slumps, Bloomberg Newsreports in-depth 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-10-07/diesel-price-surge-hits-farmers-raising-food-inflation-risk?cmpid=BBD100726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9712>
 on Wednesday.

Less than a month before US midterm congressional elections, the Trump 
administration is scrambling to find ways to ease the crunch, announcing a move 
this week to temporarily allow tax-free, off-road diesel to be used in 
18-wheelers and other highway vehicles.

 the White House’s order won’t do much to immediately lower fuel prices.

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-27/trump-says-he-s-very-seriously-looking-at-diesel-export-ban?cmpid=BBD100726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9712>

 that import a lot of US supplies.

Inflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-11/us-core-cpi-rises-more-than-forecast-bolstering-case-for-hike?cmpid=BBD100726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9712>

a political burden for Trump’s Republicans.Research published 
<https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/10/how-fast-do-tariffs-pass-through-into-consumer-prices/>
 Tuesday by economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York won’t help them 
deflect the blame for the elevated cost of living.

The report seeks to answer the question of how long it takes for Trump’s 
tariffs to hit consumers’ wallets.

Explainer: Why the US Economy Looks Strong But Feels Bad to Many 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/why-americans-are-unhappy-with-an-economy-that-looks-strong?cmpid=BBD100726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9712>

“We find that for every percentage point increase in average tariffs, consumer 
goods prices increase by about a quarter of a percent after one year,” wrote 
Fed researchers Mary Amiti and Sebastian Heise, and Columbia University 
economics professor David Weinstein.

“Roughly two-thirds of the effect arises because tariffs raise the prices of 
imported consumer goods,” they said. “The remaining third arises indirectly: 
tariffs increase the prices of goods made in the US because producers pay more 
for imported parts and materials, and because producers raise their markups 
when competing imports cost more.”

The authors of the latest post include three of the four economists who drew 
the ire 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-18/hassett-attacks-ny-fed-for-study-on-tariffs-hurting-us-companies-mls3r5yi?cmpid=BBD100726_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9712>
 of White House economic adviser Kevin Hassett back in February — about a week 
before Trump lost his tariff case before the Supreme Court — forwork explaining 
<https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/02/who-is-paying-for-the-2025-u-s-tariffs/?ftag=MSFd61514f>
 that nearly 90% of Trump’s 2025 tariff burden fell on American companies and 
consumers.

White House officials have argued that tariffs aren’t inflationary.
