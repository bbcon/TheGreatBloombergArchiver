Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, the 
China oil team looks at the extent to which the country’s recovering demand 
will affect global prices.

China’s oil imports are rebounding quickly 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-19/china-oil-imports-rebound-as-beijing-turns-back-to-stockpiling?cmpid=BBBXT111926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11609>
 as the year draws to a close. They’re now back to about 10 million barrels a 
day, up from just over 7 million in June.

The big question is how much impact this will have on global prices as the 
international market swings back to a surplus. The answer boils down to two 
crucial factors: stockpiling and fuel-export policy.

Chinese state-owned refiners have good reason to want to rebuild inventories 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/china-refiners-to-refill-oil-stocks-from-november-eurasia-says?cmpid=BBBXT111926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11609>
 after eating into them during the early months of the US-Iran war. The 
independent processors, known as teapots, also need more crude to produce 
fuels, especially after they lost access tocheap Iranian supply 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/china-s-teapot-refiners-turn-to-trading-giants-as-iran-oil-ebbs?cmpid=BBBXT111926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11609>
.

Just how aggressive the Chinese buyers will become depends on price. Brent 
crude below $80 a barrel would encourage more substantial purchases, according 
to analysts surveyed by Bloomberg. While January futures for the international 
benchmark are currently trading around $92, later-dated contracts are closer to 
that level.

Refiners’ appetite for crude also depends on the amount of fuel that 
authorities permit them to export. After issuing bigger-than-expected 
allowances in October, Beijing was initially reluctant to approve higher 
monthly shipments due to signs oftight diesel 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-06/china-to-cut-diesel-exports-on-tight-stocks-energy-aspects-says?cmpid=BBBXT111926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11609>
 inventories.

That constraint may now be easing, with one gauge of diesel stockpiles 
recently starting to increase after months of drawdowns. That would give 
refiners more scope to ship product overseas and capitalize on healthy export 
margins, which would in turn support more crude imports.

The global market looks to be increasingly in need of extra demand from China. 
Persian Gulf exports are almost back to prewar levels as producers adapt to 
Iran’s threats to shipping. That’s prompted the International Energy Agency and 
Goldman Sachs Group Inc. to forecast areturn to surplus. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/goldman-sachs-predicts-sizeable-surplus-for-oil-market-in-2027?cmpid=BBBXT111926_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261119&utm_content=11609>

Yet China’s transport fleet is rapidly electrifying and its economy is still 
battling deflationary pressures. So any increase in the country’s crude demand 
is unlikely to be primarily driven by domestic consumption.

That’s why, heading into 2027, stockpiling and fuel-export policy will 
determine whether Chinese demand regains its influence over the global oil 
market.
