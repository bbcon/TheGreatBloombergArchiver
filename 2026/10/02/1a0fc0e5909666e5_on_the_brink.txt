Northern Ireland is on the brink again.

Fresh developments concerning a British unionist parade that Irish 
nationalists don’t want to proceed may be thelatest trigger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-29/northern-ireland-powersharing-at-risk-as-march-tensions-simmer?cmpid=BBD100226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9644>
 that causes the regional government to collapse.

The Belfast assembly is already highly tenuous. It’s broken down several times 
since being established under a landmark 1990s peace agreement that largely 
ended deadly sectarian violence.

Another breakdown could damage the arrangement that has just about managed to 
hold the fragile peace for almost 30 years.

Under that deal, pro-UK unionists and Irish nationalists agreed to share power 
in a devolved parliament, with either side able to bring it down if they see 
cause.

An impasse in Portadown, a traditionally unionist town, might be the next 
reason.
An Orange Order parade in Portadown, Northern Ireland, on Sunday.Photographer: 
Charles McQuillan/Getty Images
A court in Belfast upheld a ruling by the Parades Commission that the unionist 
Orange Order could march through a nationalist enclave for the first time since 
the route was banned after it became a deadly flash point in 1998. Hundreds of 
Irish nationalist protesters have been blocking the route.

The UK government is urging compromise 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-27/uk-pm-calls-for-calm-amid-standoff-over-northern-ireland-march?cmpid=BBD100226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9644>
, with Secretary of State Chris Bryant having held talks with both sides in an 
attempt to resolve the deadlock.

The last time the Northern Ireland Assembly collapsed was over unionist 
concerns related to the UK’s decision to quit the European Union, and a 
seemingly arbitrary border drawn in the Irish Sea to ensure trade flows.

All that would be resolved if Prime Minister Andy Burnham took the UK back 
into the EU as he floated this week.

But the Portadown protests point to deeper undercurrents that could complicate 
his plans in Northern Ireland and may be even harder to fix than Brexit.

Even though polls suggest a majority of people in the region want to remain in 
the UK, fewer identify solely as British, whilesupport for Irish unity 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-14/nationalist-wins-in-uk-lift-case-for-irish-unity-sinn-fein-says?cmpid=BBD100226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9644>
 is growing, especially among the young. There is also a desire to remain in 
the EU; a majority in the region voted against Brexit.

Only the British government can call a vote on the matter. But the precarious 
state of the devolved administration doesn’t bode well for the status quo.

Not for the first time, Northern Ireland could become a thorn in the UK prime 
minister’s side. — Olivia Fletcher

record diesel prices they acknowledge are crushing farmers and truckers. They 
ignored Trump’s call to shrink credit card swipe fees and a bipartisan plan to 
cap insulin costs. They’re divided on how to tame the nation’s soaring interest 
costs. And they made no headway on Trump-backed stimulus checks.

And Finally

Burnham has vowed to reverse what he calls a series of wrong turns that 
Britain has taken from the 1980s, a tumultuous decade in which “Iron Lady” 
Margaret Thatcher led the Conservative Party through labor unrest, 
privatization drives and Cold War tensions. He announced the repeal of her 
ideological ban on public ownership of water companies as part of a bid to 
reassert state control over basic services. His move todismantle Thatcherism 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/burnham-tries-to-turn-the-page-on-thatcherism-in-britain?cmpid=BBD100226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9644>
 and replace it with his own vision signals a possible ideological turning 
point for the UK’s economy.
