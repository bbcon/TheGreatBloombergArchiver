After a Blue Tuesday, No Black Wednesday

Markets and voters like springing surprises. Last week, Brazil’s voters 
upended expectations by returning Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva to power; markets 
freaked out. Now in the US, Democrats have swept the Congress and state offices 
in a conclusive fashion that nobody had dared predict — and markets responded 
with relief.

This ends a period when President Donald Trump could govern as though Congress 
didn’t exist, and opens an array of possibilities for the 2028 election. The 
initial expectation was that a Democratic landslide would be market-negative. 
That’s how US stock futures treated the ever more implausible Democratic gains 
in the Senate — until turning around at midnight on the East Coast:

This seems bizarre but there’s precedent for it, ironically on the night when 
Trump shocked the world by winning the presidency in 2016. During the campaign, 
the stock market had repeatedly sold off on any good news for Trump. That was 
detailed ina paper for the Brookings Institution 
<https://fordschool.umich.edu/news/2016/wolfers-and-zitzewitz-brookings-what-do-financial-markets-think-2016-election>
, which extrapolated these selloffs to predict that an election win would bring 
the S&P 500 down 10-15%.

As election night unfolded, stock futures fell across the world. Then the 
result was confirmed, Trump gave a magnanimous acceptance speech, and markets 
turned around. The S&P 500 rallied 2.22% on the Monday before the election on 
polls showing a clear victory for Hillary Clinton; it surged a further 1.1% the 
Wednesday afterward once Trump had won.

How to explain this? When confronted with a fact, we can all begin to see some 
of the silver lining in outcomes we previously found alarming. Ten years ago, 
it took the Trump victory to confront traders with the fact that many of his 
policies could be good for growth. Something similar is afoot this time. Here 
are some key points:

 * It’s a personal defeat for Trump; not necessarily for Trumpism
This is a massive personal repudiation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-04/-an-unmitigated-repudiation-of-donald-trump-and-a-very-clear-message-says-former-dnc-chair?cmpid=BBD110526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=10998>
 ofTrump the man 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-04/emanuel-election-was-repudiation-of-trump-video?cmpid=BBD110526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=10998>
. Direct rejections of his handpicked candidates, like Ken Paxton in Texas, are 
particularly ringing rebukes. His political judgment appears finally to have 
deserted him, and his personal control over the Republican Party must at least 
be compromised.

But it’s dangerous to spin a narrative of defeat for right-wing populist 
politics 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-07-17/populism-isn-t-peaking-it-s-diversifying?cmpid=BBD110526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=10998>
. Anger against the man is so acute because he blatantly broke promises to 
fight inflation and avoid wars. This result doesn’t mean that there’s no chance 
of success for an honest right-wing populist who does what Trump said he was 
going to do. And given that some very moderate Democrats will now take power, 
it’s not a big endorsement for left-wing populism, either. As Trump’s erratic 
behavior had driven volatility, few investors will be perturbed to see his 
wings clipped. The result is more market-friendly than it at first appears.

 * Gridlock can be good for you
The slim chance that a surprise Republican majority would pass another tax cut 
has been extinguished. Amid the current fiscal anxiety, that’s probably good 
news. The presidential veto ensures that Congress can’t push through any 
massive fiscal expansion. Congressional investigations might slow the works of 
government, which would also be no bad thing for investors.

As Manish Kabra of Societe Generale SA shows, the history is that a Republican 
president constrained by Democrats in Congress tends to mean good returns for 
both stocks and bonds:

 * Inflation really, really matters
Price rises have now inflicted defeats on two presidents in a row. Surely 
there won’t be a third. Whoever next moves into the White House will accept 
that inflation constrains their actions. Policies of any political stripe that 
risk reigniting inflation are much less likely. The immediate move in inflation 
swaps suggests hawks like this result:

 * The Fed’s independence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2025-07-03/independence-is-the-worst-form-of-central-banking?cmpid=BBD110526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=10998>
 is back off the agenda
The Senate must approve new Fed appointments. Any attempt to pack the central 
bank with governors who will do the presidential bidding is now at an end. With 
the resumption ofattempts to prosecute Fed Governor Lisa Cook 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/white-house-forms-committee-to-probe-federal-reserve-s-cook?cmpid=BBD110526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=10998>
 (who voted last monthto hike rates 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-10-29/fed-rates-lord-let-me-hike-just-not-now?cmpid=BBD110526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=10998>
), this did not go without saying. There are many problems with the Fed, but 
attempting to overhaul it this way was always a bad idea, and one more market 
risk has been removed.

 * The feared near-term uncertainty isn’t going to happen
The scale of the Democratic victory brooks no argument. Protracted and 
consequential lawsuits in states like Alaska won’t happen; there will be no new 
Jan. 6. One big risk has been allayed.

Some negatives remain:

 * 2028 is suddenly much more uncertain
The Democrats did a great job of finding good candidates for the specific 
circumstances of states like Kansas, Texas or South Carolina. It will be far 
harder to agree on one candidate for the entire country in 2028, and the 
decisive nature of the victory will probably encourage more to enter. The risk 
of afire-breathing egalitarian 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-08-14/how-much-socialism-is-america-willing-to-swallow?cmpid=BBD110526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=10998>
 whom markets would really hate (like Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez) must be taken 
ever more seriously.

For the Republicans, the assumption had been that whoever Trump endorsed would 
win the nomination. As a lame duck deemed culpable for this loss, it’s no 
longer clear he has that power. That’s good for the functioning of US 
democracy, and for Republicans’ long-term future; for the next two years, it 
likely means a mess, with several viable candidates 
<https://polymarket.com/event/republican-presidential-nominee-2028> covering a 
range from isolationism to classic business-friendly Republicans.

 * Lame ducks can be dangerous 
<https://www.foreignaffairs.com/united-states/dangers-lame-duck-trump?s=ESPZZ006N2&utm_medium=promo_email&utm_source=special_send&utm_campaign=PROM_Special%20Send_110426_The%20Dangers%20of%20a%20Lame-Duck%20Trump&utm_content=20261104&utm_term=ESPZZ006N2>
Trump cannot run again, but he retains the veto, and a range of executive 
powers that he’s shown no compunction in using. Foreign Affairs points out that 
a lame duck can still start a war, while European diplomats have briefed that 
he could be“more mad dog than lame duck.” 
<https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/04/lame-duck-or-mad-dog-why-trumps-defeat-could-spell-trouble-for-europe>
 Much rests on the psychology of one notoriously mercurial man who now has 
little left to lose.

 * AI’s politicization just ratcheted up
Data centers’ huge unpopularity 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-03/data-centers-on-midterm-ballots-as-opposition-surges-video?cmpid=BBD110526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=10998>
 had much to do with the Democrat Gina Hinojosa’s capture of the Texas 
governorship. She and many city mayors havethe power to limit data-center growth
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-02/data-center-restrictions-make-the-ballot-in-dozens-of-us-cities?cmpid=BBD110526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=10998>
 and must now follow through. This risk to the AI trade just got bigger — it’s 
not clear that Democrats can force any broader “AI pause” or impose greater 
regulation, because of the presidential veto, but that issue is now very much 
live for 2028.

Amid the instant reaction, the big AI hyperscalers joined the rally, but the 
market punished semiconductor groups and “neo-cloud” data center developers:

Endowments...

For decades, the Yale Model, pioneered by David Swensen 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/S98HBJT0AFB4?cmpid=BBD110526_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=10998>
 at Yale University’s endowment, set the gold standard for long-term investing. 
Applying efficient-market theory, he took advantage of Yale’s infinite time 
horizon to pick out a range of long-term illiquid investments in inefficient 
markets, working out correctly that this would give him the best chance to 
deliver returns above the public markets.

For Ivies, the returns have helped deal with the financial pressures that the 
Trump administration has imposed 
<https://www.thenation.com/article/society/trump-university-endowment-tax-higher-education/>
 — but the reliance on illiquid alternative assets restricts their room for 
maneuver when they need access to funds, as happened blatantlyin 2008 
<https://www.theguardian.com/business/2009/sep/11/harvard-yale-financial-crisis>
 when Swensen and others needed to borrow to meet their cash commitments.

The approach can still deliver big time, as shown by Washington University in 
St. Louis, which posted a return of more than 37% for fiscal year 2026, beating 
the average Ivy League endowment by more than 15 percentage points. But can 
such gains be sustainable, and what risks have managers taken?

Using annual data to construct factor-mimicking portfolios with a methodology 
detailedhere 
<https://www.markovprocesses.com/product/mpi-transparency-lab/?tab=endowments>, 
Michael Markov of Markov Processes International finds that WashU has some of 
the highest volatility among endowments, thanks in particular to concentrated 
co-investments in private companies, including AI-related investments.

That concentration, Markov argues, reflects a broader issue for all 
endowments. How much risk to take, where to invest, and whether to hedge 
exposures can all drive returns more than selecting the underlying funds. Some 
endowments, he says, are making consequential investment decisions that aren’t 
apparent to outside observers:
Taking more risk, hedging, these are indications that basically endowments are 
being run like a trading desk… Do we know that boards know about risk-taking? 
Do they know about the hedging decisions and things? How much do they know?
None of this shows that WashU has taken on more risk than its peers. The 
pertinent question is whether university boards fully understand their 
exposures and how they fit with their financial needs. Markov says the approach 
was rewarded last year — and that offers an opportune time for institutions to 
reconsider how much risk they want to carry.

This exercise requires assessing how the portfolio might fare in a severe 
downturn, when the university still has to meet spending commitments and fund 
private-market capital call:
The key of the endowment model is not leverage and not concentration. 
Swensen’s idea was diversification. That’s the one thing. You take as many risk 
factors as much, hopefully not correlated. Hopefully, it comes from different 
things. You have energy, real estate, you have private, public, emerging 
markets, and these different things that should behave differently in different 
environments.
Investments in standout companies such as SpaceX can pay off handsomely, but 
they’re high-risk, long-term bets. Not making them is not necessarily a 
mistake; but taking a bet and winning is not on its own a sound investment 
strategy.

—Richard Abbey
