Dimming Lights

Global energy prices may have come off their peak, but they’re still not 
falling fast enough for Asian nations struggling to keep their lights on.

The energy shock — and the economic destruction that comes with it — has 
persisted despite the short-lived ceasefire between the US and Iran in June. 
The latest round of peace talks willoffer little optimism 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-25/asia-braces-for-worst-case-energy-scenarios-as-iran-war-drags-on?cmpid=BBD080426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260804&utm_content=7298>
 for much of Asia heavily reliant on oil and natural gas flowing through the 
Hormuz strait.

The problem unfolding across Asia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-08-03/asia-s-energy-starved-nations-go-dark-as-hormuz-tensions-drag-on?cmpid=BBD080426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260804&utm_content=7298>
 is so vast that it’s visible from space. NASA satellite imagery shows that 
night lights have dimmed across nearly 60% of the region’s land area since the 
conflict began. Poor, import-dependent nations like Bangladesh, Pakistan and 
Cambodia have seen the starkest declines.


Nighttime radiance may not be a direct measure of economic impact, but it 
affirms similar evidence of higher fuel costs curtailing energy-intensive 
sectors such as tourism, transport and manufacturing — the very growth engines 
that have helped Asia outpace much of the world.

We spoke to business owners and bread winners in some of these hardest-hit 
communities, which are bearing the brunt of the economic fallout of a war 
thousands of miles away. Many of them havebarely recovered from the shocks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-10-06/covid-has-wiped-out-the-economic-dreams-of-an-asian-generation?cmpid=BBD080426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260804&utm_content=7298>
 of the pandemic and higher US tariffs.

In rural Cambodia, a father of three has pulled his children out of English 
classes and swapped an electric stove for charcoal to make ends meet.

In an island in the Philippines, a resort owner is struggling to raise prices 
at a time when fuel costs have doubled but customer bookings have also dropped 
by 25%. In the industrial heart of Bangladesh, factories have stalled 
production while offices are using their lights sparingly.

The risk now is that Asia’s energy crisis could have lasting growth 
implications. The Asian Development Bankcut its GDP growth forecast 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-08/asia-growth-improves-but-adb-warns-of-persistent-headwinds?cmpid=BBD080426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260804&utm_content=7298>
 for developing Asia and the Pacific to 4.9% from 5.1% this year. It also 
raised the inflation outlook to 4.3% from 3.6%. Even with a ceasefire in place, 
the ADB has warned that may stay well above pre-war levels well into 2027.

For the ADB, the bigger risk lies ahead: households taking on debt, businesses 
cutting jobs, and economies emerging from the crisis with scars that linger 
long after oil prices ease.

Need-to-Know Research

Euro-area households curtailing spending after the start of the Iran war 
seemed more worried by the overall uncertainty rather than faster inflation, 
according toresearch 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/euro-zone-spending-cuts-driven-by-iran-uncertainty-not-inflation-ecb-finds?cmpid=BBD080426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260804&utm_content=7298>
 by the European Central Bank.

Confidence sank and consumption momentum softened materially as the conflict 
erupted, economists including Neus Dausa i Noguera, Maria Dimou and Omiros 
Kouvavas wrote in anarticle 
<https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/focus/2026/html/ecb.ebbox202605_02~0100593e6a.en.html>
 for the ECB’s economic bulletin this week.

The slowdown was driven by weaker discretionary spending, they found. Nominal 
outlays on energy rose, reflecting costlier transport, while expenditure on 
housing and food was resilient. Higher-income households made the sharpest 
adjustments.

“The weakening in nominal consumption appears to have been driven largely by 
households that are not budget constrained but are choosing to delay spending 
in response to heightened uncertainty,” the researchers said. “Although higher 
prices associated with the war in the Middle East may also have played a role, 
the analysis below suggests the presence of a sentiment-driven channel that 
persists even after controlling for realized income.”
