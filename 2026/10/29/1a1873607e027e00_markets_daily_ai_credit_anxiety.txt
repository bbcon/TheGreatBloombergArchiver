AI’s debt deluge

A new anxiety is gripping Wall Street as it bankrolls the vast network of data 
centers and chips behind the AI boom: Early signs suggestthe credit market is 
approaching the limits of how much more debt it can absorb. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-28/wall-street-s-ai-debt-machine-is-starting-to-choke-on-supply?cmpid=102926_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10697>

One after another, yields are soaring into the double digits on bonds and 
loans funding the buildout. Bankers are finding it more difficult to offload 
risk to insurers and other investors. Elon Musk himself warned the other day 
that hundreds of so-called neoclouds risk going under as firms get cut off from 
fresh financing.

With those jitters growing louder each week — and spurring, in turn, angst 
about the potential economic fallout — Citadel Securities is warning that even 
the biggest names in tech could get ensnared as they put their balance sheets 
behind scores of projects critical to their AI ambitions. (Pimco, which helped 
supercharge the AI debt market with billions of dollars in lending in 2025,has 
cut positions just in time to skirt losses 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-28/pimco-offloads-billions-in-ai-debt-just-in-time-to-skirt-losses?cmpid=102926_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10697>
.)

The risk is becoming evident in credit default swaps, where banks and other 
lenders hedge their exposure. The cost of insuring against losses on the debt 
of companies from Broadcom to SpaceX and Oracle has spiked in recent days, 
reaching levels more typical of junk-rated borrowers. And the damage is already 
starting to spread to broader credit markets.

“At the beginning of 2026, the highest-rated data center bonds offered yields 
below 5%,” Morgan Stanley’s Carolyn Campbell said on a podcast this month. 
“Today similar bonds are yielding closer to 7%, and that increase matters 
enormously for companies trying to finance multibillion-dollar facilities. When 
borrowing costs approach 8%, issuing these bonds becomes significantly more 
challenging.” —Neil Callanan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQlzURIgG5Y/neil-callanan?cmpid=102926_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10697>

Latin stock drought

Two decades into the collapse in Latin American stock markets, Scott Piper 
likes to joke that investing in the region has become such a lonely job that 
he’s the last of his breed.

Piper, 58, got his first gig trading the region’s stocks in the mid-1990s, 
just a few years before a commodities boom would turbocharge the region’s 
economies and markets. Back then, Wall Street was ginning up new Latin 
America-dedicated funds seemingly every other day. Their numbers just kept 
growing, surpassing 660 by 2015.

Today, after scores of mergers, sales and closures, there are just 226 such 
funds across the globe 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-10-28/lula-s-brazil-election-win-deepens-latin-america-stock-fund-collapse?cmpid=102926_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10697>
, according to data compiled by Bloomberg Intelligence.

One by one, “people have sort of disappeared,” says Piper, who runs about $5 
billion for Itau Unibanco’s asset-management unit out of New York.

The atrophying of the market reveals the magnitude of the economic stagnation 
that’s gripped the region ever since that commodities boom fizzled some 15 
years ago.GDP growth has been grinding along at less than 2% a year, 
productivity has declined and innovation dried up, bringing the IPO pipeline to 
a near halt. Stocks across the region still haven’t recovered all the ground 
they lost during the global financial crisis.

The brutal stretch was only exacerbated by this week’s collapse in Brazilian 
stocks following the surprise re-election of leftist President Luiz Inácio Lula 
da Silva.

“I have seen all the Brazil cycles and Latin American cycles,” says Claudia 
Mesquita, who runs Brazil equity capital markets at UBS BB Investment Banking 
in Sao Paulo. “We are, unfortunately, in the worst one that we have ever seen. 
And it’s taken a toll.” —Vinicius Andrade 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ATq9HjCYCKg/vinicius-andrade?cmpid=102926_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261029&utm_content=10697>
