Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Asia Oil Trading Team Leader Serene Cheong explores how the energy industry has 
adapted to the threat of renewed attacks on shipping in the Middle East.

Iran’s latest attacks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/iran-ramps-up-ship-attacks-in-hormuz-as-oil-and-gas-flows-climb?cmpid=BBBXT100826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9866>
 on tankers should, in theory, be choking energy flows through the Strait of 
Hormuz.

But they’re not having the same effect that they once had. Crude shipments are 
nearing prewar levels 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/mideast-crude-oil-flows-hit-98-of-pre-war-level-jpmorgan-says?cmpid=BBBXT100826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9866>
 and soaring freight costs arepulling supertankers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-08/supertankers-racing-to-middle-east-worsen-global-ships-crunch?cmpid=BBBXT100826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9866>
 to the Middle East rather than driving them away.

Gone are the days when producers and buyers stepped back in anticipation of a 
peaceful resolution to the US-Iran conflict. Seven months of war have shown 
that the risks of sitting things out — and the rewards for running the gauntlet 
— are too big to ignore.

In fact, the frequency and scope of attacks in recent days, including a rare 
strike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-08/tanker-attacked-deep-in-persian-gulf-for-first-time-in-weeks?cmpid=BBBXT100826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9866>
 on a vessel deep inside the Persian Gulf, have prompted Middle Eastern 
producers toslash their crude prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/iraq-deepens-october-crude-price-discounts-to-as-much-as-37?cmpid=BBBXT100826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9866>
 in a fierce contest for market share.

The biggest discounts are reserved for those willing to buy on a free-on-board 
basis, meaning the customer is responsible for shipping. For a hefty fee, 
sellers inside the Gulf are also willing to move cargoes to safer ports beyond 
Hormuz, such as Fujairah or Sohar, where they can be transferred onto other 
vessels.
An oil tanker off the coast of Bandar Abbas, Iran on Sept. 9, 2026.
Photographer: Fatemeh Bahrami/Anadolu/Anadolu
Producer-owned fleets are being helped in this task by gutsy shipowners 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-05/the-supertanker-tycoon-making-millions-on-hormuz-shuttle-runs?cmpid=BBBXT100826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9866>
, such as Sinokor Group and Dynacom Tankers Management Ltd., that can earn up 
to $40 million for a single round trip in and out of the Gulf, according to 
shipbrokers familiar with the matter. Vessels are there precisely because 
they’re being paid top dollar.

Even major liquefied natural gas producer Qatar, which had taken a more 
conservative approach than oil exporters after a vessel carrying its fuelwas 
struck 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-01/tanker-carrying-qatari-lng-struck-while-transiting-hormuz?cmpid=BBBXT100826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9866>
 in August, is maneuvering to push more through Hormuz.

At least 12 LNG carriers owned or chartered by QatarEnergy — roughly 2% of the 
entire global tanker fleet for the fuel in terms of carrying capacity —have 
gathered 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/qatar-brings-empty-lng-ships-into-gulf-in-potential-export-boost?cmpid=BBBXT100826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9866>
 in the Gulf.

Just in the first week of October, UK Maritime Trade Operations reported ten 
attacks in Hormuz and within the Persian Gulf, compared with 16 in the whole of 
September.

There was a time when such an escalation in hostilities would have halted 
flows, at least temporarily. But the industry’s newfound resilience, 
accompanied by a higher risk appetite throughout the supply chain, appears to 
have changed that.

— Serene Cheong, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Weather remains one of the biggest factors shaping how much gas Europe will 
have in storage at the end of winter. BloombergNEF’s base case, which assumes 
weather in line with the 10-year average, puts inventories at 18% by end-March. 
But outcomes can vary widely, and a warmer El Niño winter doesn’t always 
provide the same relief. If conditions match gas year 2023 that would leave 
storage around 29% full, while a repeat of gas year 2015 would see it at about 
20%.
