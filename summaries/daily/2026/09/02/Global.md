**US-Iran Conflict and Energy Markets**
The conflict's market mechanism is now well-established: each escalation cycle drives oil higher, reignites inflation expectations, raises central bank tightening odds, and pressures both equities and bonds simultaneously. The pattern is self-reinforcing — higher energy costs feed through logistics indices (the US LMI near 240), into consumer prices, and back into rate expectations. US efforts to offset Hormuz disruption through Venezuelan output expansion are directionally logical but face execution risk given the scale of ambition and the unconventional profile of some operators. Diesel prices hitting four-month highs following attacks on refineries illustrate the direct passthrough channel. Pakistan faces a worsening electricity crunch after foregoing LNG purchases to replace Hormuz-disrupted supply — a downstream consequence receiving insufficient attention.

**Monetary Policy Divergence Narrowing**
The more notable global macro story may be convergence rather than divergence: Fed, ECB, BOJ, RBNZ, and the Bank of England are all moving toward or signaling tightening simultaneously. With traders pricing a greater-than-50% hike probability across three major central banks for September, the global liquidity environment is tightening in a coordinated, if uncoordinated, fashion. The G20's failure to produce a communique — and the broader pattern of G20 members scrutinizing developing-nation debt while running their own deficits above 6% of GDP at full employment — reflects a credibility gap in multilateral fiscal governance that bond markets appear increasingly willing to price.
