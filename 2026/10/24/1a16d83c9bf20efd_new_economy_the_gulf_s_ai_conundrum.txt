Even after the US-Israel war with Iran began in late February, Saudi Arabia 
designated 2026 asThe Year of AI 
<https://sdaia.gov.sa/en/MediaCenter/Pages/ai-year.aspx>. Crown Prince Mohammed 
bin Salman invited “all dreamers, innovators, investors and thinkers” to help 
the kingdom “unlock the value of data and AI.”

Saudi Arabia isn’t alone in betting on AI. Most of its Gulf neighbors have 
similar ambitions. And the stakes extend beyond the region, as global demand 
for computing power grows. The Gulf hopes to meet that demand by exploiting its 
natural advantages: available land, relatively cheap labor and plentiful energy.

AI could also help Gulf leaders achieve their long-standing goal of reducing 
oil dependence. But like Saudi Arabia, they all face the same problems, and the 
same threats. While postponing a conference is an inconvenience, large data 
centers and fixed power grids can’t easily be moved out of harm’s way.

The AI ecosystem has three broad layers, each dominated by different firms. 
First is hardware, including chips made by Nvidia, TSMC and others. Second is 
infrastructure, such as the data centers built by Amazon, Google and Microsoft. 
Third is models, where companies led by OpenAI and Anthropic set the frontier.

Much of the Gulf’s AI push has focused on data centers. ‎The region has 
partnered with global firms 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-06-11/nvidia-teams-up-with-g42-for-uae-data-centers-in-mideast-ai-push?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10428>
 to develop the ‎infrastructure. It has created domestic companies, ‎including 
Saudi Humain 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-08-25/saudi-s-humain-to-open-data-centers-with-us-chips-in-early-2026?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10428>
 and UAE-based Khazna, to‎further the cause 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-04-28/dubai-data-center-leader-plans-saudi-push-after-silver-lake-deal?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10428>
. And it’s working with Washington to ‎secure advanced chips to operate the 
centers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-19/us-reaches-ai-chip-sale-agreement-with-g42-in-win-for-uae-firm?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10428>
.‎

The ambition is big, and the expansion is already underway. Saudi Arabia and 
the UAE almost tripled their data center capacity between 2020 and 2025, 
according to BloombergNEF estimates. And that’s just the beginning: 
BloombergNEF expects capacity to grow another three- to six-fold by 2035.

AI’s economic effect on the Gulf has been negligible so far. The first 
benefits of data centers come from construction activity. Yet both top-down 
statistics and bottom-up calculations point to the same conclusion: data-center 
construction has barely moved the needle for Gulf economies.

The Gulf’s AI bet was never about the past five years: it’s about the next 
decade. And the bigger prize isn’t construction, but income from customers 
using the data centers. For AI to finally show up in economic statistics, the 
Gulf must meet itsambitious growth targets and avoid slippage 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-08-25/saudi-s-humain-to-open-data-centers-with-us-chips-in-early-2026?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10428>
.

But slippage isn’t the only risk, as Gulf states could also end up competing 
with each other. In recent years, they’ve targeted the same sectors to move 
away from oil: petrochemicals, logistics, tourism and finance. That has 
sometimes led to duplication and excess capacity. AI could face the same 
problem as countries race to build its infrastructure.

The Iran war adds another layer of risk. Builders and customers can no longer 
worry only about cybersecurity. They must also protect expensive data centers 
from physical attacks: strikes tied to the widening war have already hit 
facilities in the region,potentially destroying some data forever 
<https://www.wsj.com/world/middle-east/aws-says-it-cant-restore-some-data-from-mideast-facilities-struck-by-iran-ddcb7e5d>
.

The Gulf can invest in protecting its AI infrastructure, but stronger security 
could reportedlyraise costs by 5% to 7% 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-04/middle-east-s-data-center-push-endures-under-the-cloud-of-war?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10428>
, eating into profits. And even then, there’s no guarantee those investments 
can safeguard facilities against increasingly sophisticated military technology.

Another option is for Gulf nations to build some data centers abroad. But that 
would undermine much of their competitive advantage. The region would miss out 
on the construction boom and lose the benefits of its abundant resources.

That leaves the Gulf with a difficult choice: build at home despite the risks, 
or abroad despite the costs. When Saudi Arabia reconvenes its flagship 
investment conference, its next big idea may be less glamorous than futuristic 
cities: data centers that can withstand missile and drone attacks.
