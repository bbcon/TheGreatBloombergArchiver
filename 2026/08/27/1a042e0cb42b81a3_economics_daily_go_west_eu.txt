Go West, EU?

Can the European Union extend further into the Atlantic? That’s a question 
Icelanders might be about to help answer as they vote on Saturday about whether 
to restart talks to join.

For the 27-member bloc, the windswept volcanic island in the middle of its 
neighboring ocean might seem small fry. Iceland’s economy is tiny, and its 
400,000 or so inhabitants amount to less than a midsized city in a massive 
regional club whose population exceeds 450 million.

But the stakes for Brussels aren’t so low. One of Europe’s richest nations on 
a per capita basis, Iceland stands out from other possible applicants as a 
prestige project for the EU. And enlargement, focused on the poorer east, has
halted 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-05/eu-pushes-to-end-expansion-deadlock-to-curb-russia-and-china?cmpid=BBD082726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8223>
 since the last expansion to Croatia 13 years ago.

Meanwhile Iceland’s strategic position and traditionally stronger American 
cultural ties underscore the sensitivity of its status, not least after a 
diplomatic crisis this year over US President Donald Trump’s obsession with 
nearby Greenland, a semi-autonomous part of the Kingdom of Denmark.

The key question for voters is fishing, with Iceland’s catches far exceeding 
those of any member of the bloc. That issue and agriculture are likely to be 
the trickiest parts of any accession talks, according to Prime Minister 
Kristrún Frostadóttir.

There’s also the matter of the krona, because joining could eventually require 
the country to ditch its currency and adopt the euro. That step that would need 
“deep reforms 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-06/iceland-debates-benefits-of-euro-entry-as-vote-on-eu-talks-nears?cmpid=BBD082726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8223>
” of its labor market, according to its central bank.

Voters might wonder if it’s worth the bother, since Iceland already has access 
to the EU single market as part of its membership of the European Economic 
Area. The bloc takes two thirds of its exports, while the share of US is at 9%, 
according to WTO data.

Opinion surveys point to a very close race. The latest one, published 
Wednesday and conducted by pollster Maskina, shows the lead of the “yes” camp 
at just 2.6 percentage points. The business lobby’s own polling shows 66% of 
member companies are against joining the EU.

Dreamers of a much bigger bloc will be watching the outcome closely; After 
all, if Brussels can extend its reach to the mid-Atlantic, why stop there?

Sweden’s premier, Ulf Kristersson, joked earlier this year about the prospect 
of Canada joining, while Finnish President Alexander Stubb also quipped about 
allowing Ottawa to become a potential accession candidate.

That’s not something Canada is looking to do, according to Prime Minister Mark 
Carney. But next month, he will attend European Commission President Ursula von 
der Leyen’s State of the EU speech as a guest of honor, before addressing the 
European Parliament too.

With Canada embroiled in a bitter trade war with Trump, it’s hard not to 
notice a warming relationship with Brussels there too.

Need-to-Know Research

A budding narrative has it that the companies raising massive amounts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-26/artificial-intelligence-investment-now-competes-with-governments-for-capital?cmpid=BBD082726_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8223>
 of capital for giant investments in artificial intelligence are following the 
same path as the telecommunications firms that over-borrowed and over-built 
during the internet bubble. Eurizon SLJ Capital says not so fast.

True, the fact that hyperscalers have gone from funding capital spending out 
of their cash flows to raising money from credit markets marks “an acute 
shift,” Stephen Jen and Joana Freire at Eurizon SLJ wrote in a note this week. 
But what matters is revenues the funds produce. They borrow the incremental 
capital-output ratio (ICOR) concept — how much additional investment is needed 
to generate a unit of extra output — from developmental economics.

“Our ICOR calculations suggest that the rough range of ICOR for AI (8-13) is 
not unreasonable, compared to many other industries,” the duo wrote. They based 
their analysis on hyperscalers’ own revenue projections, as a starting point, 
in the blue bar, then removed “legacy” businesses in the two red-bar scenarios. 
The bottom line: the signs suggest the ICOR for AI “is not quite outlandish, we 
think, and may very well decline over time as the industry grows out of its 
adolescence.”
