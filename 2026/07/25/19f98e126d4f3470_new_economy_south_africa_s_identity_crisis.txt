Visiting the southeastern African nation of Malawi recently, my interactions 
with people in the commercial capital of Blantyre became uncomfortable as soon 
as I identified myself as coming from Johannesburg — where I’m based.

Among the questions I faced: Why wasn’t South Africa clamping down on 
anti-immigrant violence? What kind of people block pregnant women from seeking 
urgent care in a health clinic? The verbal bombardments were a reflection of 
the infamy earned from months of xenophobic protests against migrants, along 
with a government deportation campaign that’s seen tens of thousands of Black 
Africans driven out of South Africa.

While President Cyril Ramaphosa and fellow leaders have moved to stop the 
protests from escalating uncontrollably, they’ve been less strident about 
condemning the violence — which tends to recur every few years — than past 
administrations. Ahead of elections in November, government officials have been 
expressing understanding of the frustrations behind the protests, and talked up 
their efforts tospeed the deportation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-07/south-africa-to-crack-down-on-illegal-migrants-as-protest-surge?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260725&utm_content=6899>
 of undocumented migrants and tighten border security.

The image of “Afrophobia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-03/how-afrophobia-is-unraveling-south-africa-s-post-apartheid-vision?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260725&utm_content=6899>
” these expulsions and protests have created is harming the country’s relations 
across the continent, which South Africa had hoped to lead in the afterglow of 
Nelson Mandela’s ascent to office. Now Ghana has asked Ramaphosa topostpone a 
visit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-07/ghana-asks-ramaphosa-to-defer-visit-over-xenophobic-attacks?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260725&utm_content=6899>
 and South African companies are worried aboutpotential curbs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-15/south-african-firms-face-pressure-due-to-anti-immigrant-protests?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260725&utm_content=6899>
 on their businesses.

And as countries like Malawi suffer 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-16/cost-of-living-crisis-blights-malawi-president-s-reelection-bid?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260725&utm_content=6899>
 from lost worker remittances, the flip side is that South Africa’s economy 
suffers, too:labor shortages 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-18/fleeing-migrants-leave-hard-to-fill-gap-in-south-africa-s-moribund-economy?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260725&utm_content=6899>
 are now hitting everything from delivery apps and home-cleaning services to a 
textile hub.

The root of the protests lies in broader discontent over poor employment 
opportunities and patchy — or, in many municipalities, nonexistent — government 
services. Many analysts attribute those ailments to pervasive corruption, 
rather than the 3 million or so foreigners the official statistics agency says 
are in the country illegally.

As South Africa spurns its regional neighbors’ nationals, countries including 
Ghana, Nigeria, Zimbabwe and Malawi have sent planes and buses to fetch their 
citizens. In the South African coastal city of Durban, more than 10,000 
Malawians, many afraid of being attacked, recently gathered in an impromptu 
camp, which rapidly degenerated into a health hazard.

On July 20, the Economic Community of West African States said it would back a 
push by Ghana’s leader, John Mahama, for the crisis to be taken up at the 
African Union. Nigerian politicians have called for reprisals against South 
African companies active in the country.

Such angst in one sense reflects South Africa’s status as sub-Saharan Africa’s 
biggest economy. It’s long been a magnet for migrants from countries to the 
north, some of which are wracked by conflict while others are desperately poor. 
So what happens there matters to others.
A village near Blantyre, Malawi. Hardship awaits migrant workers forced to 
return home.Photographer: Antony Sguazzin/Bloomberg
One example: Zimbabwe in the first six months of 2026 received $420 million in 
remittances from South Africa, almost a third of the total. With 100,000 of its 
citizens now having returned, those financial flows will dwindle.

Since Mandela took office in 1994, the nation has aspired to speak on behalf 
of the continent. So the sight of crowds dressed in Zulu battle regalia 
shouting “abahambe” — “they must go” — is not a good look.

Nor does it chime with the image South Africa has tried to cultivate 
internationally — mediating in conflicts andchampioning the cause of 
Palestinians in Gaza 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-16/un-commissioned-report-finds-israel-is-committing-genocide-in-gaza?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260725&utm_content=6899>
 in a case against Israel at the International Court of Justice. Rather than 
serving as a voice for Africa, it now risks becoming a regional pariah.

South African diplomats push back against this narrative, saying some nations 
are taking advantage of the situation to score political points. In prepared 
remarks this week, Deputy President Paul Mashatile said, “South Africans are 
not inherently xenophobic. They are a welcoming and pan-Africanist people.”
People wait to board a bus headed to Zimbabwe at a repatriation center in 
Musina, near the Beitbridge border crossing, on July 9.Photographer: Emmanuel 
Croset/AFP/Getty Images
That’s not how Andrew Mahone, a Malawian who worked as a caretaker at a Cape 
Town church before he fled, sums it up. He spoke to Bloomberg earlier this 
month during a stop in Harare, Zimbabwe, en route to his home nation. While 
sharing his thoughts, concerned locals brought food and water to distressed 
mothers with small children who were among his fellow passengers.

“They took everything from me. Everything. I only have these clothes I am 
wearing,” Mahone said of the protesters. “I thought I was an African. But those 
people who did this to us are something else.”
