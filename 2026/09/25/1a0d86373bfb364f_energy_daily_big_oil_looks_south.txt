Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, Rio 
de Janeiro-based reporters Mariana Durão and Peter Millard explore why Big Oil 
is putting big money into South America.

Big Oil is going hunting for crude again, and South America is emerging as one 
of its favorite places to look.

ExxonMobil Holdings Corp., Chevron Corp. and TotalEnergies SE are putting 
renewed emphasis on exploration as rising energy demand and geopolitical 
turmoil increase the need to find new supplies.

The shift marks a change from just a few years ago, when the energy transition 
raised questions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-09-13/bp-says-the-era-of-oil-demand-growth-is-over?cmpid=BBBXT092526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9286>
 over how aggressively the world’s largest oil companies should search for more 
crude.

Now, growing energy consumption and concerns about security of supply are 
swinging the pendulum back.

ExxonMobil declared an “absolute resounding yes” to the need for more 
exploration at an industry conference this week in Brazil, where it’s active in 
a huge new offshore region called theEquatorial Margin 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-06-17/petrobras-and-exxon-team-up-to-win-disputed-oil-blocks-in-brazil?cmpid=BBBXT092526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9286>
.

Chevron is also growing in the country, expanding its resource footprint 
roughly sixfold over the past two years. And TotalEnergies chief Patrick 
Pouyanné — speaking at the event in Rio de Janeiro — said finding new crude 
reserves is crucial.
A floating production vessel operated by TotalEnergies in the Lapa field off

After falling about 10% over the past decade, the oil industry’s capital 
spending on exploration and production is forecast to tick up in 2026. 
Increased investment in South America will helpoffset declines in the Middle 
East 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/brazil-s-oil-output-hits-record-as-war-drives-up-non-opec-supply?cmpid=BBBXT092526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9286>
 and North America, according to the International Energy Agency.

The IEA pegs upstream spending at about $546 billion this year, still below 
pre-pandemic levels but 19% higher than in 2020.

South America took off 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-06-24/latin-america-s-two-oil-giants-team-up-in-search-for-their-next-boost?cmpid=BBBXT092526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9286>
 after ExxonMobil’sblockbuster find 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2016-06-30/exxon-s-guyana-oil-discovery-may-be-twice-as-large-as-thought?cmpid=BBBXT092526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9286>
 in Guyana demonstrated how quickly a frontier asset can become a bonanza.

Now companies are hoping that success can be replicated, not only offshore 
Brazil but also in waters off Suriname and elsewhere.

Aside from its geology, the continent as a whole is also less geopolitically 
turbulent than theMiddle East 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-04/iran-war-dents-qatar-s-reputation-as-lng-s-safest-bet?cmpid=BBBXT092526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260925&utm_content=9286>
 right now.

Of course, things can change quickly in South America. The oil industry 
learned that the hard way in Venezuela, where nationalizations by socialist 
governments hamstrung operators in the past.

But with large areas of the continent remaining unexplored and recent proof of 
world-class discoveries, big oil companies are betting the region could be key 
to replenishing their reserves.

—Mariana Durão and Peter Millard, Bloomberg News
