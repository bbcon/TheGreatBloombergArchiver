Deep Dive: Burnham’s Devolution Revolution
The Northumberland Train Line reopened in 2024 to passengers in Ashington.
Photographer: Michael Vince Kim/Bloomberg
Ashington is an old coal mining town in the North East of England that fell by 
the wayside during thedeindustrialization of the Margaret Thatcher era 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TJ6DS4N3N08Y?cmpid=080326_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7278>
. Its last pit closed in 1986 and its largest employer, an aluminum smelter, 
shut down in 2012.

 * For Britain’s new prime minister, Andy Burnham, towns like Ashington will 
be the test of his vision for “growth in every postcode” of the country. To get 
there, he believes the UK needs to end “40 years of neoliberalism” and what he 
and other critics disparagingly call trickle-down economics.
 * Instead, he will rebuild the economy “from the bottom up” by giving power 
away in a devolution revolution — the driving principle behind a politics he 
has called Manchesterism, named after the city where he was mayor for nine 
years.
 * The evidence suggests that devolution is no panacea. Scotland, the UK’s 
most devolved area, has grown more slowly than England since the 2008 financial 
crisis. Despite raising the top rate of income tax to 48% from 45%, and 
spending more on health and education, outcomes in those areas have been worse 
than in England.
 * Yet there are models of English devolution that have proved successful. 
Burnham’s Manchester is one, although a more comprehensive model is — 
ironically — closer to the Westminster power base Burnham wants to demote. It’s 
Sadiq Khan’s London.
