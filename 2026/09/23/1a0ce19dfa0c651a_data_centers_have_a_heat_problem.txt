Urban heat

by Elena Mejía 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWiNbok2RLM/elena-mejia?cmpid=BBD092326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9206>
 andKyle Stock 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ARKz8x17rNs/kyle-stock?cmpid=BBD092326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9206>

Behind the walls of an AI data center, massive racks of servers hum around the 
clock. The more electricity they use,the more heat they create 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-ai-data-center-heat-pollution-cities/?cmpid=BBD092326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9206>
.

A single facility can use over 50 megawatts of electricity. Virtually all of 
that electrical power is converted into thermal energy — waste heat — that must 
be ejected into the atmosphere. That’s roughly the amount of heat generated by 
54,000 homes.

Data centers often cool their systems with cold air and discharge superheated 
exhaust through equipment located on rooftops, producing intense plumes of hot 
air that can travel over 300 feet beyond the data center in certain wind 
conditions. Recent studies have detected hotter temperatures up to a third of a 
mile (500 meters) downwind from a data center — heating up surrounding areas 
and potentially raising their air-conditioning bills.

And, as more data centers pop up in dense clusters near residential areas, 
their warming effects on surrounding neighborhoods are set to be magnified, 
especially in places that already experience extreme temperatures.

In Phoenix, where the average daytime temperature in September is 100F (38C), 
a blizzard of fans cools the CyrusOne data center just south of the city 
limits. In communities downwind from this facility, temperatures were up to 4F 
higher than upwind, according to arecent analysis 
<https://asmedigitalcollection.asme.org/sustainablebuildings/article/7/2/024501/1233035/Data-Center-Waste-Heat-as-an-Emerging-Urban>
.
Thermal image of CyrusOne data center in the Phoenix area.Photographer: 
Caitlin O’Hara/Bloomberg
Belching its exhaust into the sky, the building is essentially a giant laptop, 
said David Sailor, lead author of the study and director of Arizona State 
University’s School of Geographical Sciences and Urban Planning. The dense 
neighborhoods just across the street? The lap.

“Put your hand behind your computer and you feel the heat coming off that 
fan,” said Sailor. “Now, imagine if you had 1,000 computer [fan] blades in your 
office — 10,000, 100,000, several hundred thousand blades in your office — 
that’s the amount of heat that we’re talking about.”

Emerging research suggests these invisible plumes spilling over data center 
fence lines could become a major contributor to the heat island effect, where 
cities run hotter than rural areas around them.

The extra heat load threatens to exacerbate 
<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212095518303560> 
already searing temperatures due to climate change, researchers say. In 
Phoenix, for example, the past two years werethe warmest 
<https://www.weather.gov/psr/PhoenixRecordData> on record. Data center heat 
could also upend ecosystems, affecting habitats for plants and animals — and 
potentiallyaltering rainfall and humidity levels 
<https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/urban-rain/#hds-sidebar-nav-2>
 as warm air blowing out of these sites influences how clouds form.

As AI developers hurry to stand up projects, they are fighting a surging wave 
of opposition from communities with a range of gripes, from the vast amounts of 
land, water and electricity required to the existential threat of increased 
emissions and the prospect of AI agents turning on their human architects. While
more Americans 
<https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/how-opinions-and-use-of-ai-differ-by-age/>
 are turning to chatbots, most aren’t keen on having the engines of AI in their 
backyard. In arecent Gallup survey 
<https://news.gallup.com/poll/709772/americans-oppose-data-centers-area.aspx>, 
seven out of 10 respondents said they would oppose a data center nearby.

Increasingly, the opposition is winning. In April through June, at least 45 
projects 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-21/new-data-centers-worth-68-billion-disrupted-in-us-data-show?cmpid=BBD092326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9206>
 worth about $68 billion were blocked or delayed, according to Data Center 
Watch, a research group tracking AI opposition. Lawmakers inNew York 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-14/new-york-leads-nation-in-moratorium-on-new-large-data-centers?cmpid=BBD092326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9206>
 andTexas 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/texas-sets-december-deadline-for-grid-reviews-on-data-centers-crypto-miners?cmpid=BBD092326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9206>
 have also approved data-center pauses and two federal moratoriumbills 
<https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/4214> are circulating 
in the Capitol.

In the US, one third of data centers are built in a cluster, defined as three 
or more data centers just over half a mile from each other. The vast majority 
of the 88 data center clusters in the US are within 500 feet of an urban metro 
area.

By 2040, another 23 clusters are expected to be built. Though the data center 
campuses using the most power are planned in less populated locations, most of 
the new clusters will be within urban areas.

The prospect of AI heat islands is becoming another rallying point for those 
who wantdata centers canceled 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-08-19/tech-tries-charm-offensive-to-save-trillion-dollar-ai-buildout?cmpid=BBD092326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9206>
 or at least frozen until permitting processes can catch up to the blitz of 
technology. It also brings up a new challenge for developers planning to add 
billions of dollars’ worth of new projects: What to do with all that heat?

“Heat is the most salient issue,” said Eric Runnestrand, a Phoenix-area 
resident who has been organizing opposition to data centers in the area. “But, 
to be frank, the overwhelming sentiment is just a ‘No.’ Not like, ‘Ah, well, if 
they can meet this and that and this, that would be OK.’ It’s a ‘No.’”

Turkey’s COP31 plan

By Lili Pike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXt16hTqABg/lili-pike?cmpid=BBD092326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9206>

With artificial intelligence and the Iran war topping the agenda at this 
week’s United Nations General Assembly, the president of the upcoming UN 
climate summit appealed to global business leaders tokeep rising emissions in 
focus 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-22/turkey-s-environment-minister-lays-out-agenda-for-cop31-summit?cmpid=BBD092326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260923&utm_content=9206>
.

“We believe we will be safer if we increase military spending, but the 
greatest threat is at our doorstep, which is climate change,” Murat Kurum, 
Turkey’s environment minister and COP31 president, said Tuesday during a speech 
at the COP31 Global Business Forum – New York Dialogue.
Murat KurumPhotographer: Yui Mok/Pool/Getty Images/Getty Images Europe
Turkey is hosting the November talks in the city of Antalya in partnership 
with Australia, which is leading negotiations. In his speech, Kurum laid out 
the agenda for the summit, including six global targets spanning areas from 
municipal waste to green industrialization. Top of the list is for countries to 
achieve 35% electrification by 2035.

“At the heart of the action agenda for COP31 lies electrification,” said 
Kurum, pointing to power grids, battery storage, green transportation and 
buildings as priorities. His speech came at an event hosted by Bloomberg LP, 
the parent company of Bloomberg News, at the company’s headquarters.

A new analysis 
<https://www.iea.org/news/electrification-takes-centre-stage-as-hormuz-shock-spurs-countries-to-seek-to-enhance-energy-security>
 released by the International Energy Agency found that the goal is within 
range. Using energy prices before the war, IEA researchers found that 
electricity could meet 33% of the world’s final energy consumption by 2035, an 
increase of 10% from current levels, and in a cost-effective way. The 
heightened fossil-fuel prices the world has seen since the war are making 
electrification even more affordable, they said.
