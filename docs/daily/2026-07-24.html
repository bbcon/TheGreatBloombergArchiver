US

  • The Magnificent Seven lost $797 billion in market value Thursday — their largest single-day decline since April 2025 — as Alphabet and Tesla capex plans stoked AI spending skepticism, even as Intel surged on better-than-expected revenue guidance.
  • Trump announced broad new tariffs of 10%–12.5% on imports from roughly 60 economies, the administration's most sweeping move yet to reconstruct the tariff wall previously struck down by the Supreme Court.

Tariff Escalation

The Trump administration announced duties of 10%–12.5% on roughly 60 economies, its broadest tariff action yet. Trading partners subject to forced-labor restrictions — including the UK, Canada, and India — face 10% levies; EU and Taiwan goods are capped at 10%, while Switzerland and Japan face up to 12.5%. The scope of the move signals a deliberate reconstruction of a tariff framework that was partially dismantled by the Supreme Court. Near-term economic consequences remain difficult to quantify, though the measures are directionally inflationary and create additional uncertainty for business investment planning.

Equity and Rates

Thursday's equity rout was centered on the Magnificent Seven, where Alphabet and Tesla's elevated capex disclosures reinforced concerns that AI infrastructure spending may not translate into near-term earnings accretion. Intel provided a notable counterpoint, with its revenue forecast exceeding Wall Street estimates by a wide margin, underscoring sustained data-center demand. The broader rates complex is under pressure: the global investment-grade yield at 3.68% represents a significant tightening of financial conditions, and the one-in-three implied probability of a Fed hike next week marks a material shift from prior market pricing, though the consensus among economists remains that the Fed's next move will be an easing. ---

China Market Intervention

The STAR 50 Index entered bear market territory earlier this month, falling approximately 17% as investors unwound leveraged positions at the fastest pace since the 2015–16 crash, with CXMT's $9.8 billion IPO threatening to drain market liquidity. Beijing's response was notably broad-based: state-linked ETFs absorbed record inflows, China Reform Holdings and China Chengtong Holdings disclosed fresh equity purchases, major insurers including China Life committed to increased equity exposure, and regulators pledged to maintain market stability and rein in quantitative funds. The intervention appears primarily motivated by the need to preserve investor confidence ahead of CXMT's listing and to support capital formation for strategic technology sectors competing with the United States. Whether it produces durable gains or merely stabilizes sentiment ahead of structural reforms remains uncertain.

Japan and Korea

Japan's inflation data suggest domestic price pressures are sufficiently persistent to support further Bank of Japan normalization this year. Meanwhile, the yen's weakness to multi-decade lows is driving a notable behavioral shift: department store sales of luxury jewelry and precious metals rose 19% year-on-year in the first half of 2026, as consumers seek inflation hedges. In Korea, the Kospi fell 5.7%, the sharpest regional decline, driven by Samsung and SK Hynix. The losses reflect both AI capex skepticism and portfolio repositioning by Asian hedge funds making room for the CXMT listing. China's new 12.5% tariff implies an effective US levy of 22.2% on Chinese goods — a modest escalation that Bloomberg Economics estimates is unlikely to trigger significant retaliation ahead of the expected Xi-Trump summit in September. ---

Europe

  • Euro-area private-sector activity improved substantially more than expected in July, with consumer inflation expectations declining significantly — data points that complicate the ECB's hawkish internal lean.
  • ECB officials kept rates unchanged Thursday but privately signaled readiness to hike in September unless the inflation outlook improves markedly; some policymakers considered an immediate increase.
  • Europe's summer heat wave has produced an estimated 20,000 excess deaths to date, with Spain declaring a national state of emergency over wildfires near Madrid — the first such declaration on record.
  • UK consumer confidence recovered to pre-war levels in July, per GfK, supported by the World Cup and Prime Minister Burnham's living-cost commitments.

ECB Policy

The ECB's Thursday decision to hold rates was reached quickly — President Lagarde noted the meeting concluded by approximately 10:15 a.m. — but the internal deliberations were more substantive than the outcome suggests. Some members raised the possibility of an immediate hike on a day when Brent crude briefly touched $100 per barrel. Officials subsequently signaled to Bloomberg that a September increase is the baseline unless incoming data or a de-escalation of Middle East tensions materially improves the inflation picture. The public messaging remains deliberately ambiguous: Bundesbank President Nagel declined to pre-commit, noting the volume of data due before September. Lagarde added uncertainty of her own by pledging to remain in post only through the end of 2026, implicitly opening the question of succession for European governments.

Heat Wave and Economic Impact

Europe's heat emergency is transitioning from a humanitarian concern to a measurable economic variable. One estimate places the cost of a single week of extreme UK heat at £1.15 billion in lost working hours, rising to £2.36 billion when infrastructure failures are included. ING's Carsten Brzeski noted that the thermometer has effectively become a leading economic indicator. Spain's wildfire state of emergency near Madrid and August's historically worse heat profile suggest the economic drag is likely to intensify before it abates. The political dimension is also shifting, with France's National Rally — historically skeptical of climate science — now acknowledging the need to engage seriously with the evidence. ---

LatAm

  • Brazil's Flávio Bolsonaro campaign faces compounding headwinds: new allegations of ties to a bank scandal, a fresh police probe, and fractured family political dynamics ahead of the presidential election cycle.

Currency and Commodity Pressures

The combination of a weaker dollar trend against commodity currencies and elevated global oil prices is creating divergent pressures across the region. Coffee and banana exporters — concentrated in Colombia, Ecuador, and Central America — are facing reduced dollar revenues as the greenback softens relative to local currencies, compressing export margins at a time when input costs remain elevated. The dynamic illustrates the asymmetric exposure Latin American commodity producers face to both energy price shocks and currency volatility simultaneously. Brazil, as both a major commodity exporter and an oil producer, faces a more nuanced trade-off, though political uncertainty ahead of the election cycle is an independent headwind to investor confidence.

Brazil Political Risk

The Bolsonaro campaign's operational difficulties are accumulating. New allegations linking the campaign to a bank scandal, combined with a fresh police investigation and the candidate's strained relationship with his broader political family, increase execution risk for what had been a credible opposition candidacy. How this develops over coming weeks matters for Brazilian asset pricing, as political uncertainty tends to translate into real spread widening in a market already sensitive to fiscal dynamics. ---

Energy and Middle East Escalation

The partial closure of the Strait of Hormuz following renewed hostilities has produced a sustained oil price shock that is now embedding itself in global financial conditions. Brent crude near $100 per barrel is simultaneously a supply disruption signal and an inflation input, complicating the task of central banks across developed and emerging markets. Trump's threat of a "massive attack" on Iran — described as larger than any previous strike — introduces a scenario tail that markets are beginning to price but cannot yet fully assess. The Strait carries approximately 20% of global seaborne oil trade; a further escalation that extends or deepens its effective closure would represent a material supply shock for which there is limited short-term substitute routing. Gulf sovereign bond issuance at record levels partly reflects the capital required to build bypass infrastructure, a signal that regional governments do not expect rapid normalization.

Monetary Policy Divergence

The global rates picture is growing more complex. The ECB is privately leaning toward a September hike driven by energy-price inflation. The Bank of Japan is on track for further normalization. The Federal Reserve faces a market now pricing a one-in-three probability of a hike next week, while its economist consensus still points to the next move being a cut. Russia's central bank, by contrast, extended its easing cycle. The divergence reflects fundamentally different domestic inflation dynamics layered on top of a shared energy shock — a configuration that typically produces meaningful currency volatility and cross-border capital flow realignment.
