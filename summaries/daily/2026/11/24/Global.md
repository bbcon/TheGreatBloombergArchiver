**Oil Markets: From Disruption to Surplus**
The pace of reversal in oil market sentiment is notable. The near-closure of the Strait of Hormuz earlier this year was widely characterized as a worst-case scenario; nine months later, Persian Gulf producers are exporting crude near pre-war levels via both tankers and pipeline alternatives. US shale, Canadian, and Latin American output has grown to fill the supply gap, while global demand has contracted more than 2% as the energy price spike weighed on consumption. Inventories remain severely depleted, limiting the system's resilience to any renewed supply shock — but with Trump signaling reluctance to escalate and Democrats' midterm victory raising expectations of a faster path to de-escalation, traders are pricing a materially looser market. European natural gas remains the exception, with prices rising sharply on cold weather forecasts and ongoing Hormuz-linked supply fragility.

**Trade Policy and Geopolitical Fault Lines**
The pending Section 301 tariff report, once released, will add a new dimension to already elevated US-China trade tensions — with the potential to push effective tariff rates on Chinese goods back toward 20% when the truce lapses on January 10. The G-20 meeting in Miami on December 14–15 creates a political incentive to delay the announcement, though analysts are divided on timing. China's decision to extend its critical minerals export suspension through January represents a measured, transactional response — preserving leverage without triggering an immediate escalation. Meanwhile, the Nvidia governance episode has introduced a new element to the US-China technology competition: the perception, reinforced by Beijing's state media commentary, that US export controls may be selectively subordinated to market considerations.
