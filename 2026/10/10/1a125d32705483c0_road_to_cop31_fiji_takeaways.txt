A Pacific path to COP31

By Azman Usmani 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AYPlpafOt_4/azman-usmani?cmpid=BBD101026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9915>

For international climate talks charged with a relatively sober task — 
sharpening the agenda for next month’s showpieceCOP31 summit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/latest/cop-climate-summit?cmpid=BBD101026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9915>
 in Turkey — the dress code in the picturesque Pacific this week was noticeably 
vibrant.

Vivid floral bula shirts and traditional headdresses featured prominently as 
about 4,000 climate envoys, negotiators, global ministers and participants 
gathered for Pre-COP meetings in a region that’s often literally on the 
frontline of global warming.
Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese and other leaders at the Tuvalu 
Coastal Adaptation Project in Funafuti, Tuvalu, on Oct. 6.Photographer: Mick 
Tsikas/Pool/Getty Images
Discussions in Fiji, and a whistle-stop tour by dignitaries to 
climate-threatened Tuvalu, where rising sea levels already challenge the 
world’s second-lowest-lying nation, helped focus attention on the consequences 
of rising emissions. There are few indications yet, however, that major 
countries are prepared to show greater ambition, or accelerate efforts to 
deliver on existing climate commitments.

Behind the warm island hospitality and colorful attire was a familiar message: 
symbolic pledges from richer nations will no longer substitute for concrete 
climate action, improved flows of finance, and a managed end to fossil fuels.

Here are some takeaways from the Pre-COP talks:

Climate finance

Availability of financing to lower emissions and fund adaptation against 
extreme weather impacts remains a key concern. Pacific nations, and other small 
states, didn’t shy away from expressing frustration with the delays and red 
tape they encounter to access global funds.

Of the roughly $16 billion in climate finance pledged to Pacific countries 
alone since the 2015 Paris Agreement, more than$5 billion remains undisbursed 
<https://unfccc.int/news/pre-cop-closing-day-wrap>.

“We scrutinize the consequences of approving finance. We should scrutinize the 
consequences of delaying it just as seriously,” Mark Brown, prime minister of 
the Cook Islands, told the meetings.

Papua New Guinea’s environment minister John Kaupa called for “fairer, simpler 
and faster” financing mechanisms, while Fiji’s minister for information, 
environment and climate change Lynda Tabuya urged better efforts to “get 
climate finance into the hands of the people who really need it.”
Fiji’s Minister for Information, Environment and Climate Change Lynda Tabuya 
at Pre-COP31 talks in Nadi, Fiji. Oct. 8, 2026.Source: Australia-Pacific 
Partnership for COP31
Recent adaptation finance flows would meet only about 26% of the projected 
annual needs in the Pacific, Asian Development Bank President Masato Kanda – 
who joined the visit to Tuvalu – told the talks. “Leaders I met with made clear 
what is needed,” he said. “Financing must reach vulnerable countries faster.”

Ambitions for Antalya

COP31 will aim to deliver a political decision text covering climate 
mitigation, adaptation, finance and implementation, the summit’s 
President-Designate and Turkey’s Environment, Urbanization and Climate Change 
Minister Murat Kurum told the sessions.

However, his careful words indicated that task won’t necessarily be simple. 
The agreement should “reflect the urgency highlighted by science” and provide 
“political coherence among different elements” Kurum said. Yet the outcome 
“will not replace negotiation tracks, nor will it create new obligations or 
commitments,” he said.
COP31 President-Designate Murat Kurum, left, and COP31 President of 
Negotiations Chris Bowen at Pre-COP31 talks in Nadi, Fiji. Oct. 8, 2026.Source: 
Australia-Pacific Partnership for COP31
The formal COP31 talks open Nov. 9 in Antalya, Turkey. Under a compromise over 
hosting rights, Australian officials will helm the negotiations.

“While there may not be a blockbuster on the agenda this year, there are lots 
of complex and vital issues that must be agreed,” Simon Stiell, executive 
secretary of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, said at 
the Fiji event. “That is incredibly hard when every country must agree on every 
single word. But strong outcomes are essential.”

Talks this week in Fiji “have made progress, and also shown us how much hard 
work remains ahead,” he said.

Fossil fuels fault line

About 30 nations backed the Taku Pakasoa declaration, an agreement that 
adopted the Tuvaluan phrase for strength in unity, setting out support for 
efforts to minimize overshoot beyond 1.5C of warming, defend climate science, 
and to accelerate the global energy transition.

The pact, endorsed by countries including Australia, the UAE and Singapore, 
also includes a pledge on “transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy 
systems in a just, orderly and equitable manner, accelerating action in this 
critical decade.”

Despite a landmark agreement at COP28 in Dubai in 2023 to transition away from 
fossil fuels, there has been limited action to achieve the target and annual UN 
meetings since then have made no additional progress. Last year in Brazil, a 
group of about 80 countries pushed for roadmaps to implement the goal, but were 
blocked by fossil fuel producers including Saudi Arabia.

This year’s COP hosts have both faced criticism over their own ties to fossil 
fuels. Australia as a major exporter of coal and natural gas, and Turkey as 
that nation’s power generation remains heavily reliant on the energy sources.

Oceans in focus

Delegates pushed to ensure the crucial importance of the world’s oceans is 
properly reflected during COP31, and that more action is taken focused on 
marine conservation.

“Never again should Mother Ocean, the planet’s primary climate regulator, be 
consigned to the back rows of deliberations,” said Peter Thomson, the UN 
Secretary-General’s special envoy for the Ocean.

Turkish officials confirmed next month’s summit will work towards an Antalya 
Ocean Declaration aimed at advancing ocean-based climate solutions and 
delivering better understanding of the ocean-climate nexus.

“If you were designing the climate architecture from scratch today, would you 
leave out the planet’s largest sink of excess heat? And its largest active 
reservoir of carbon? Perhaps not,” said Filimoni Vosarogo, Fiji’s minister for 
lands and mineral resources. “Yet that is close to what we are doing.”

Blanketed in haze

By Bloomberg News 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/?cmpid=BBD101026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9915>

Several Southeast Asian countries have been blanketed for weeks in toxic haze 
spewing from forest fires in Indonesia. Cases of respiratory illnesses have 
soared, schools have been closed and flights canceled. Governments are under 
pressure to step up and coordinate a response to what’s shaping up to be the
worst such episode 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-indonesia-wildfires-el-nino/?cmpid=BBD101026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9915>
 in more than a decade.
A firefighter tackles a wildfire in south Sumatra, Indonesia, in September.
Photographer: Muhammad Fajri/NurPhoto/AP Photo
Why is the haze so bad this year?

While the fires and accompanying haze are a near-annual occurrence in 
Southeast Asia, the situation this year is particularly bad. In September, the 
number ofsatellite-detected hotspots <https://www.iqair.com/air-quality-map> in 
Indonesia — thermal anomalies that indicate a fire — were at their highest 
since 2015.

Man-made climate change has increased the frequency and intensity of droughts, 
and this year arecord El Niño 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-08/this-year-s-el-nino-is-set-to-be-the-most-intense-ever-recorded?cmpid=BBD101026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9915>
 weather pattern caused by a warming Pacific Ocean has exacerbated and 
prolonged the dry conditions in Southeast Asia. Fires used to illegally clear 
vegetation are burning out of control on land that’s already been degraded by 
decades of extractive, industrial-scale agriculture.

The blazes across Borneo, Sumatra and other islands are burning through 
forests and across dried-out peatlands, where they can be particularly hard to 
extinguish as they often smolder underground and release noxious and warming 
gases like methane, carbon monoxide and sulfur dioxide.

How is the haze affecting communities, industries and ecosystems?

The fires have ripped through areas that are home to orangutans and other 
endangered species. Indonesian President Prabowo Subianto has called the 
deliberate setting of fires to clear land “ecological terrorism.”

Thousands of residents in Indonesian Borneo, which along with Sumatra has seen 
the worst of the fires and haze, have reported respiratory infections in the 
past few months, according to local media. Local governments have had to set up 
“oxygen houses” to help those with sensitive lungs, particularly children and 
the elderly.

More than 2 million students in Malaysia were ordered to stay at home on Oct. 
9 as the air quality deteriorated. Parks in the capital Kuala Lumpur emptied of 
pedestrians and joggers. Organizers of the city’s annual marathon had tocancel 
a kids’ event 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-02/malaysia-presses-ahead-with-marathon-scraps-kids-run-amid-haze?cmpid=BBD101026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9915>
, though they decided to press ahead with the adult full marathon and 
half-marathon.

Flights were disrupted or rerouted in Malaysia and Indonesia as visibility 
dropped below levels deemed to be safe. Plantation and mining operations in 
parts of Indonesia have been disrupted as employees work fewer hours outside 
and low water levels in some rivers hold up the transportation of timber, palm 
oil and coal.

Are governments cooperating to tackle the haze problem?

Past haze crises have caused diplomatic rows and blame games between Southeast 
Asian neighbors. Indonesia has been accused of not doing enough to prevent the 
fires, while Singapore and Malaysia have been criticized forhosting companies 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/greenpeace-links-20-agricultural-companies-to-indonesian-fires?cmpid=BBD101026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9915>
 whose supply chains include plantations on fire in Indonesia.

This time around, officials have been more restrained with their criticism, 
stressing cooperation instead.

Malaysian Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim called 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-08/haze-blankets-kuala-lumpur-as-state-leader-calls-for-action?cmpid=BBD101026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261010&utm_content=9915>
 Indonesian leader Prabowo to discuss joint efforts to tackle the crisis. Kuala 
Lumpur has offered to send aircraft and personnel, though it’s unclear if these 
have arrived.

There were calls for the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, or Asean, to 
coordinate a response. But the bloc’snon-interference principles 
<https://asean.org/what-we-do/> and a lack of data-sharing have made it 
difficult for its members to agree on meaningful remedial measures. TheAsean 
Agreement 
<https://asean.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/17.-July-2016-ASEAN-Agreement-on-Transboundary-Haze-Pollution.pdf>
 on Transboundary Haze Pollution, although ratified by all members since 2015, 
is a voluntary, collaboration-based framework — unlike directives in the 
European Union.
