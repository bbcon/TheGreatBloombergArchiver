Fixing the copper crunch

By Coco Liu 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AU-LNn94_HY/coco-liu?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>

The world has a copper problem 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-12/robert-friedland-on-the-world-s-monumental-shortage-of-copper-mty3hi1s?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>
. The average ore quality is plummeting, more resources are being plowed into 
obtaining the same amount of metal and new mines take years to open because of 
the cost and regulatory hurdles. Yet companies need more of the commodity to 
power the accelerating electrical revolution.

“It is essentially the metal that is underpinning 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-08/global-copper-shortage-is-set-to-intensify-on-ai-and-defense-spending-s-p-says?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>
 this transition,” said Kwasi Ampofo, head of metals and mining research at 
BloombergNEF. Electric vehicles, for instance, require three to four times more 
copper to build than their internal-combustion counterparts. Without a steady 
supply, Ampofo warned that the world will either slow its push to electrify 
transportation or pay a steep premium to achieve it.
A pilot-scale version of Endolith’s bioleaching technology at their facility 
in Arvada, Colorado.Photographer: Chet Strange/Bloomberg
By 2040, the world could face a copper shortfall of 10 million metric tons, 
according to areport 
<https://www.spglobal.com/en/research-insights/special-reports/copper-in-the-age-of-ai>
 published in January by research firm S&P Global. That represents a 25% gap 
between the projected supply and demand, the report said.

The bottleneck is yielding innovative solutions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-04-02/skyscraper-high-waste-piles-hold-key-to-trump-s-copper-goal-as-tariffs-loom?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>
. To extend the lifespan of one mine past its prime, Arizona’s Carlota Mine, 
its owner, KGHM Polska Miedz S.A., has recently recruited a group of 
unconventional miners. They arrived by the millions, transported in 
barrel-sized containers from a laboratory in Colorado.

These miners are specialized microbes, acid-loving microorganisms that 
liberate copper from ore by digesting surrounding iron and sulfur compounds.
Endolith, a Denver-based startup 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2026-green-tech-startups-bnef-pioneer-award-winners/?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>
 that supplies the microbes to KGHM, will continue to grow them in bioreactors 
onsite. From there, the microbes are channeled into a sprinkler system to mix 
with sulfuric acid. That liquid then drips into vast heaps of crushed rocks — 
some as large as 10 sports stadiums — setting off a series of biochemical 
reactions in a process known as bioleaching to release copper.

If all goes as planned, Endolith’s microbial miners will help squeeze more 
copper out of the heaps and reduce the use of sulfuric acid at a time when the 
war in Iran has sent the feedstock’s price sky-high.

Endolith is tight-lipped about its estimated additional copper output, saying 
that the experiment is still in its early days. But Brock Johnston, who manages 
Carlota Mine for KGHM, is already excited about the prospect of producing more 
with fewer chemicals.
Testing boxes that mimic bioleaching processes at Endolith’s facility.
Photographer: Chet Strange/Bloomberg
“Any kind of improvement is beneficial,” said Johnston. After years of 
continuous digging, Carlota now produces roughly a third of its peak volume. 
Unless something is done, the mining complex — which cost hundreds of millions 
of dollars to build — is set to shut down by the end of the decade.

In a world where copper is critical for everything ranging from EVs to power 
grids and renewable energy equipment toartificial intelligence data centers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2026-ai-data-center-build-costs/?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>
, Johnston worries about the scheduled mine closure. “I’m concerned about how 
that is going to play out over time if we can’t keep up.”

Companies big and small are scrambling for ways to extract more copper. Jetti 
Resources 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-10-24/copper-tech-firm-jetti-raises-funds-at-2-5-billion-valuation?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>
, another Colorado-based startup, develops proprietary chemical catalysts to 
help break down stubborn sulfide ore. In Missouri, Emerson Electric Co. 
upgrades conventional heap leaching systems with smart sensors and automation. 
Meanwhile, Nuton, a subsidiary of the British-Australian mining titanRio Tinto 
Group 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/profile/company/0730100D:AU?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>
, is creating its own microbial solution. Earlier this year, Rio Tinto struck a
deal 
<https://www.riotinto.com/en/news/releases/2026/rio-tinto-and-amazon-web-services-collaborate-to-bring-low-carbon-nuton-copper-to-u-s--data-centres>
 to supply Amazon.com Inc. with copper produced this way.

“There is so much more that could possibly be done,” said Buz Barstow, an 
associate professor at the department of biological and environmental 
engineering at Cornell University. Next-generation bioleaching, according to 
him, has a long way to go.

Droughts are pressuring beef prices

By Deena Shanker 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASznQGEjaUE/deena-shanker?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>

At Papi Steak in Miami Beach, Florida, guests don’t just come for the 
over-the-top, high-priced menu items; they come for the spectacle. Dishes 
include a $95 gold-encrusted baked potato, a $320 seafood medley served on a 
multitiered stand packed with ice and the restaurant’s main attraction: the 
$1,000 wagyu tomahawk steak. Weighing in at a whopping 55 ounces, the raw slab 
of meat arrives at diners’ tables in a diamond-encrusted briefcase, with 
waitstaff standing at the ready to brand it with the restaurant’s logo and then 
whisk it off to the kitchen to be grilled to perfection.
Photo Illustration: Justin Metz for Bloomberg Businessweek

According to co-owner David “Papi” Einhorn, the restaurant, which has an 
outpost in Las Vegas as well as a newmail-order service 
<https://papiranch.com/password>, has no problems finding patrons. Miami draws 
lots of corporate-card-wielding conventiongoers, plus the city is not lacking 
for its own big spenders. “I just had Ivanka and Jared,” Einhorn says, 
referring to President Donald Trump’s daughter and 
son-in-law-slash-diplomat-on-call, noting that the couple are regulars and 
often bring the family. “The kids are driving them crazy, like insanity. They 
must go, they must have their birthday there.”

In Trump’s America, a steak has become more than just an expensive cut of 
meat. It’s a symbol of health and wealth, with notes of pure masculine 
virility. The rib-eye (of which the tomahawk is a particularly large version) 
has pride of place near the top of the updatedDietary Guidelines for Americans 
the administrationreleased in January 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-08/new-us-dietary-guidelines-are-heavy-on-meat-and-carbon-emissions?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>
. Shortly after the new upside-down food pyramid was unveiled, the Health and 
Human Services Departmentposted <https://www.instagram.com/p/DTneu3GDrEi/?hl=en>
 a photo of Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. ready to blow out the candles on 
his birthday steak. And though the fervor for a steak-based diet may have 
started with RFK Jr.’s Make America Healthy Again movement, other cabinet 
members have become converts to Kennedy’s steak-and-sauerkraut routine, 
including Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Transportation Secretary Sean 
Duffy and Vice President JD Vance, as the Wall Street Journal reported in June 
<https://www.wsj.com/lifestyle/fitness/trump-vance-lutnick-rfk-sauerkraut-diet-2f33bdf1>
.

Beef has taken on another association, though, one that’s awkward for the 
administration: It’s one of those everyday items that many Americans can no 
longer regularly afford. Since Trump was first sworn into office in January 
2017, prices for steak and ground beef have soared 81% and 95%, respectively, 
according to government data, compared with 41% for food overall. Runaway beef 
inflation has spurred a series of announcements from the Trump administration, 
including the resumption ofshipments of live Mexican cattle 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-17/us-to-reopen-largest-port-for-mexican-cattle-shipments?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>
 to the US and a scheme to import300,000 metric tons of low-tariff 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-21/trump-to-allow-tariff-relief-for-certain-ground-beef-imports?cmpid=BBD101926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261019&utm_content=10246>
 “product for ground beef.” (Details on the latter are notably scant, leading 
to feverish speculation about the origins of the so-calledmystery meat 
<https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/07/opinion/trump-meat-import.html>.) In an 
emailed statement, White House spokesperson Anna Kelly said Trump “is 
delivering on his promise to consumers by reducing beef prices while allowing 
our American beef herd to grow.”
