Tech slowdown

The message from this week’s Big Tech earnings is hardly reassuring for 
investors 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-30/big-tech-s-750-billion-ai-bet-collides-with-slowing-core-growth?cmpid=103026_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10737>
.

The core businesses of America’s technology giants — the advertising, consumer 
and retail machines propping up a collective $750 billion in AI spending this 
year — are starting to show signs of slowing. In some cases, they’re being 
cannibalized by the very technology the companies are betting the house on.

Google owner Alphabet reported that ad sales from search and other channels — 
accounting for roughly half of overall revenue — grew at the slowest pace in 
five quarters. Amazon said yesterday that growth in e-commerce and advertising 
slowed last quarter. Microsoft and Meta similarly posted results that 
ultimately underwhelmed investors.

The earnings reports and the ensuing stock selloffs expose the tensions 
escalating over Big Tech’s transition to AI-powered products, and the spending 
required to get there. The companies have been issuingunprecedented levels of 
debt 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/google-debt-risk-gauge-rises-as-earnings-revive-ai-debt-jitters?cmpid=103026_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10737>
, selling equity and taking on trillions of dollars in future obligations to 
pay for the AI build-out. All this while new products are eroding the 
profitability and drawing attention away from some of their legacy offerings.

Investor patience is wearing thin, though some are taking consolation in the 
fact that the weakness really is confined to tech: The Nasdaq 100 is down 3.3% 
this month even as the equal-weighted S&P 500 has gained 5.7%. Alphabet’s share 
plunge yesterday wiped out almost $500 billion in market value.

The market is “scratching its head — how does all this pay off?” said Dan 
Morgan at Synovus Trust. —Julia Love 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AV1xH-GphXo/julia-love?cmpid=103026_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10737>

Bond revival

Long-dated government bonds spent much of the year punishing anyone brave 
enough to own them. Now yields are high enough thatsome investors are coming 
back 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-30/yields-too-high-to-ignore-lure-investors-back-to-long-dated-debt?cmpid=103026_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10737>
, particularly with stocks looking rocky.

The 10-year US Treasury peaked at almost 5.4% this month, the highest since 
2002, while Japanese yields reached a three-decade high of 3.14%. They’ve since 
fallen, rewarding investors with a return of 2.2% in October on a gauge of 
global government bonds maturing in 10 years or more — the first month since 
February in which longer bonds outperformed shorter-term securities.

There are still plenty of skeptics out there though, who point out that the 
dangers that sent yields so high in the first place are still looming.
Energy-driven inflation, tighter monetary policy and deteriorating government 
finances haven’t disappeared. The price of taking those risks has simply become 
more attractive.

“I’d call it a pause rather than a turning point, as the pressures behind this 
year’s selloff haven’t gone away,” said Hilary Winn at AJ Bell. —George Nixon 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AYVU5FrEuOk/george-nixon?cmpid=103026_marketsdaily&utm_campaign=marketsdaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10737>
