Amazon divide

By Fabiano Maisonnave 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXmFWA_HFBY/fabiano-maisonnave?cmpid=BBD102426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10468>

“We have been fighting against the environment.”

That is how Rosana Gonzaga, the vice mayor of Novo Progresso, explains the 
Amazon frontier town becoming one of Brazil’s strongest bastions of support for 
conservative presidential candidate Flávio Bolsonaro.

A cattle rancher affiliated with Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party, Gonzaga named 
President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Brazil’s former Environment Minister 
Marina Silva among the town’s adversaries in a two-decade fight over protected 
land.

Gonzaga is now helping lead a campaign to give Flávio Bolsonaro 90% of Novo 
Progresso’s votes in Sunday’s presidential runoff, after he won 84.13% there in 
the election’s first round. It was the second-highest vote share for the 
candidate among Brazil’s 5,569 municipalities. The goal of localbolsonaristas 
is for Novo Progresso to give him the strongest support in the country in the 
second round.

The runoff on Sunday presents a stark divide over the Amazon rainforest and 
the environment. Lula, a leftist, has pledged to end net deforestation — 
Brazil’s largest source of carbon emissions — by 2030 and has tied his agenda 
to Brazil’s Paris Agreement commitments. Flávio Bolsonaro has questioned 
human-caused climate change and pledged to eliminate only illegal 
deforestation, leaving room for legally authorized forest-clearing.

The race remains extremely close 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-19/bolsonaro-and-lula-tied-in-brazil-runoff-poll-as-vote-nears?cmpid=BBD102426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10468>
, with polls showing a technical tie. In the first round, the conservative 
candidate led 47% to 45.2%.

Gonzaga’s family moved to Novo Progresso, in southern Para state, in 2003. 
They bought an area of primary forest from a private seller even though the 
land itself belonged to the federal government and came without formal title. 
Then, three years later, Lula’s government included it in a new conservation 
area. Gonzaga says the designation left the family and other farmers without 
access to credit and exposed them to fines and enforcement actions.

Her family story follows a model that has shaped Brazil for decades, if not 
centuries: Settlers of European descent pushing the agricultural frontier 
deeper into the country’s interior. In the Amazon, that expansion has taken 
place across vast stretches of undesignated public land.

During his term as president from 2019 to 2022, Jair Bolsonaro, Flávio’s 
father, didn’t push to legalize the settlers’ lands, but his weakening of 
environmental law enforcement led to a surge in deforestation.

After Lula took office in 2023 for a third term, he reinstated environmental 
law enforcement across the Amazon but also tried to build bridges with Novo 
Progresso and other cities with high deforestation rates.

The mayor of Novo Progresso, Gelson Dill, a center-right politician who is not 
aligned with Bolsonaro, agrees that the region needs to legalize land already 
used for farming. But as Bolsonaro supporters anticipate a change in 
environmental policy, the mayor also sees a risk.

“I hope this expectation around the new government doesn’t bring any rush to 
deforest here,” he said.

The new EV math

By Kyle Stock 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ARKz8x17rNs/kyle-stock?cmpid=BBD102426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10468>
Illustration: Martin Gee
A friend texted me the other day with a question: Should we buy an electric 
car?

She lives on an island near Seattle, where her husband, a pilot, commutes 135 
miles each way to the airport. At $5.50 a gallon — some of the most expensive 
fuel inthe US <https://gasprices.aaa.com/state-gas-price-averages/> — each 
round trip costs about $62 in gas. In an EV charged on Washington’s cheap 
electricity, it would cost about $11.

I sent a list of cars to consider.

For years, the knock against EVs was that they couldn’t go far enough and 
there weren’t enough places to charge them. For most drivers, those concerns 
have faded as ranges have grown and charging networks have expanded. The harder 
question now is whether buying one actually saves you money. Google searches 
and online car listings havelit up 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-14/iran-war-reignites-ev-interest-in-us-markets?cmpid=BBD102426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10468>
 with electric car queries, and nearly half of American drivers now say gas 
prices have made them more likely to buy an EV, according to a recent Cox 
Automotivesurvey 
<https://www.coxautoinc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Rising-Gas-Prices-and-the-Vehicle-Shopper.pdf>
.

Yet while my friend is close to the ideal EV buyer, the calculation is messier 
for most Americans. Federal subsidies have vanished and new EVs still command a 
roughly$4,800 
<https://www.coxautoinc.com/insights/q3-2026-cox-automotive-sales-forecast-update-content/>
 premium over traditional cars, while the war has pushed gas prices higher.

That’s scrambled the old EV math. Forget asking whether EVs are cheaper in 
general. In 2026, the better question is whether an EV is cheaper for you, with 
the answer depending on where you live, how much you drive and how you use your 
car.

Fueling a car, the most persistent, persnickety part of driving economics, is 
where EVs shine most brightly. The more one drives, the quicker an electric car 
outstrips a gas model on cost.

A new wedding gift: solar panels

By Yinka Ibukun 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ARaPMKchTFs/yinka-ibukun?cmpid=BBD102426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10468>

Six days a week, merchants and would-be buyers pack the dense grid of plazas 
and narrow, shop-lined streets that make up Lagos’s Alaba International Market. 
Moving through West Africa’s largest electronics bazaar can feel like an 
obstacle course: Cars, motorbikes and hand-pulled carts ferry goods from 
warehouses to shops and from shops onto trailers.

Generators, televisions and refrigerators have long filled the shop fronts. 
Now, solar panels, inverters and lithium batteries increasingly compete for 
space. Brands like Deye and Felicity Solar are everywhere, alongside 
counterfeits in copycat packaging with names like Fidelity or Facility.
A worker offloads solar panels at Alaba International Market.Photographer: 
Victor Adewale/Bloomberg
“Even tomato sellers have put signs up saying they collect solar batteries and 
all kinds of solar waste,” said Nnamdi Nkemka, the broad-shouldered owner of 
CNN Global Solar. When his shop opened in 2008, it was one of a handful of 
specialized dealers, tucked down an alley in the belly of the market. Now, it’s 
among well over 200 solar shops and sits right on the main street, with an 
adjoining warehouse to hold additional stock.

“Most people selling electrical items are migrating to solar,” he said.

The grid serving Africa’s most populous nation is so weak that in order to 
keep the lights on and appliances running, most Nigerians produce power at 
home. Domestic generators powered by petrol and diesel have for many years been 
the go-to option. But increasingly, solar equipment is gaining ground.

By the end of last year, small-scale solar capacity in Nigeria comfortably 
exceeded what the grid delivers. This year, the country has already added as 
much capacity as it did in 2024 and 2025 combined. High fossil-fuel prices and 
affordable, Chinese-made, solar technologies areaccelerating the shift 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-15/a-solar-boom-in-rural-nigeria-lights-up-local-economies?cmpid=BBD102426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261024&utm_content=10468>
 away from generators, driving consumers toward renewable energy whether they 
care about climate change or not.

Solar panels, batteries that store their energy and inverters that make the 
power usable are turning up on wedding registries, alongside more traditional 
gifts such as bed linen and microwaves. In a country where crude oil has long 
shaped the economy, families are growing used to monitoring photovoltaic output 
— how much solar energy the panels generate — and battery-charge levels, 
instead of checking oil and spark plugs. In rural areas, solar mini-grids that 
power whole communities, not just single homes, are starting to emerge.
