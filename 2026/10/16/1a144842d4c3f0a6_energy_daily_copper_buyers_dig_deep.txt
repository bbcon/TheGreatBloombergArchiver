Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Archie Hunter takes a look at the vast sums being paid for copper.

In today’s bull market for copper, it takes a lot to secure access to the 
metal: in Mercuria Energy Group’s case, as much as$250 million 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/mercuria-agrees-250-million-congo-copper-deal-with-china-miner?cmpid=BBBXT101626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10136>
.

That’s what the trading house has agreed to pay upfront to lock in three 
years’ supply from a relatively small Chinese-owned mining company in the 
Democratic Republic of Congo.

That kind of price tag isn’t uncommon now, but it’s many multiples of what 
firms were willing to pay four to five years ago, when trading the metal was 
less lucrative. And that’s largely down to Donald Trump.

The president’s threats to impose a tariff on US copper imports have driven 
prices there to a hefty premium over those elsewhere, drawinghuge amounts of 
refined copper cathodes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/copper-heads-toward-record-high-on-lme-as-supply-tensions-build?cmpid=BBBXT101626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10136>
 to its shores.

That’s a boon for traders who can ship copper both to America 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-18/deluge-of-copper-arriving-in-us-leaves-new-orleans-with-logjam?cmpid=BBBXT101626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10136>
 and to clients who’ve lost out. Buyers in China are currently paying the 
country’s highest premiums for cathodes in almost four years.

But traders need large volumes to make the most of this once-in-a-generation US
arbitrage trade 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/us-copper-inflows-surge-as-market-awaits-trump-tariff-call?cmpid=BBBXT101626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10136>
. And the ensuing competition means copper producers can ask for massive sums 
upfront in return for exclusive access to their output.

Such lucrative prepayment deals 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-06-05/traders-are-desperate-for-copper-deals-and-miners-are-cashing-in?cmpid=BBBXT101626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10136>
 are helping to fuel a mining and processing boom in Congo, which has become 
the world’s second-largest producer of copper, after Chile.

Will the long-threatened US tariff actually happen? And if not, how much 
longer can the boomtime last? Both questions will likely be the subject of much 
debate at theLME Week <https://www.lme.com/events/lme-week> industry gathering 
in London next week.

“From here the direction of the price is policy-driven,” Mercuria’s metals 
trading head,Kostas Bintas 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-06-24/ex-trafigura-metals-boss-to-run-new-team-of-40-at-rival-mercuria?cmpid=BBBXT101626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10136>
, noted at the FT Mining Summit yesterday. If a chunky US tariff doesn’t 
materialize, cathodes may start to flow elsewhere, “primarily China,” he said.

The arbitrage trade is still open for now, suggesting traders believe a levy 
will eventually be announced.

If it’s not, and prices fall, the cost of copper deals struck over the past 
two years may start to look pretty steep.

—Archie Hunter, Bloomberg News
