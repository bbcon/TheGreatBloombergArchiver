China’s Unbalanced Stimulus

Just before China kicked off its so-called Golden Week holidays, policymakers 
outlined astimulus package 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/china-s-mini-stimulus-seen-securing-gdp-target-not-much-more?cmpid=BBD100826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9795>
 to ensure that 2026 growth meets the official target. It likely provided 
little cheer to local consumers. If anything, economists warn, it may worsen 
the nation’s imbalances.

Among the measures was an expansion of a central bank lending-support program 
for banks to fund infrastructure and targeted sectors including technology. 
Beijing will also accelerate its mammoth “Six Networks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-26/how-china-plans-to-supercharge-its-ai-ambitions-with-six-networks-program?cmpid=BBD100826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9795>
” push, a multi-trillion dollar initiative spanning telecoms and logistics to 
underground pipe networks. There was also some limited aid formortgage borrowers
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-29/china-offers-mortgage-subsidies-to-boost-ailing-property-sector?cmpid=BBD100826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9795>
.

“State-guided and supply heavy,” JPMorgan Chase economists said. “Focused on 
investment and property, with little consumption spillover,” Citigroup said. 
Morgan Stanley: “The modest package means it could only backstop the growth 
floor and is unlikely to break the current deleveraging trap.”

What’s also notable is this mini-stimulus came almost exactly two years on 
from a bigger package that had included a pledge from China’s Communist Party 
Politburo to get the property market to “stop declining and stabilize 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2024-09-28/bloomberg-new-economy-china-cries-uncle-with-big-pledge-on-property-crisis?cmpid=BBD100826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9795>
.” The numbers tell the result of that promise: sales fell over 13% for January 
through August. Both new and previously owned home pricescontinue to drop 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/china-home-price-slump-persists-as-focus-turns-to-policy-support?cmpid=BBD100826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9795>
.

That weakness is key to Chinese households’ deleveraging, as Morgan Stanley 
economists including Robin Xing and Harry Zhao wrote in recent notes.

Limited income growth is the second key challenge — a product of tightening 
local government budgets and stunted job creation. The export and high-tech boom
isn’t producing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-09-26/china-s-economic-regime-shift-may-sow-global-risk-new-economy?cmpid=BBD100826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9795>
 the sort of jobs and income gains enjoyed during the property market’s 
high-flying years.

The kinds of things that could get China out of this “trap,” the Morgan 
Stanley team says, include systematic support for services spending and 
employment, broader mortgage subsidies, faster improvements to the social 
welfare system and increased support for fertility and childcare.None of that 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/china-eyes-more-targeted-fiscal-support-after-growth-slowdown?cmpid=BBD100826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9795>
 appears in train.

For now, the bottom line is, China will likely meet its annual growth target 
of 4.5%-5%. But it will do so by strengthening parts of the economy that are 
already doing well, and leaving consumption to languish.

Need-to-Know Research

The run-up in global bond yields 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/ten-reasons-investors-are-driving-government-bond-yields-higher?cmpid=BBD100826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9795>
 over the last several weeks owes in large part to expectations for central 
banks to raise their benchmark (ultra-short-term) interest rates to quell 
inflation. This will pose a hit to economic growth, Bloomberg Economics warns.

For the US, UK and euro area, the group’s SHOK model shows a negative GDP 
impact of 0.2% to 0.3% by the end of 2027, “holding other economic fundamentals 
fixed,” economists Simon Lloyd, Ana Galvao and Björn van Roye wrote in a note 
this week. The drag builds over time. By the end of 2029, the estimated hit 
reaches around 0.3% in the UK and euro area, and 0.5% in the US. Some good 
news: headline CPI inflation is 0.1-0.2 percentage point lower.

The team highlighted that the reason for the rise in yields matters. “If the 
repricing reflects stronger demand or faster potential growth, those forces 
could offset part of the hit” to GDP. But “if it reflects an energy-driven 
inflation shock, the overall growth drag could be larger, as higher rates 
compound the hit to consumer purchasing power.”
