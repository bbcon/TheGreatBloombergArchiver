US Climate and Policy

The most domestically relevant US development in this period is the transition of power in Los Angeles, where newly elected Mayor Nithya Raman — a democratic socialist with degrees from Harvard and MIT — has outlined an ambitious urban climate agenda. Her platform includes building and transportation electrification, fossil fuel divestment from city pension funds, and restrictions on large data center construction. While a single municipal government carries limited macroeconomic weight, Los Angeles is the second-largest US city, and its policy trajectory carries signal value for urban climate governance nationally. Separately, FEMA's decision to stop publishing disaster aid data has drawn scrutiny amid allegations that aid approvals have disproportionately favored Republican-leaning states, adding political friction to federal disaster response capacity ahead of elevated wildfire and storm seasons.

Asia

  • China's Fifth Plenum resolution emphasizes centralized political control, raising structural concerns about innovation capacity and local economic dynamism.
  • Xi Jinping, now 73, has further consolidated authority over CCP cadres with a new "lifelong accountability" framework for major decisions.
  • Three former chairs of China Aerospace Science and Industry Corp. were purged, illustrating the operationalization of accountability mechanisms.

China: Political Economy

The CCP's Fifth Plenum, concluded days before this publication, produced a resolution with meaningful long-run economic implications. Xi Jinping's directive — requiring cadres to follow a "top-down approach" while simultaneously prohibiting passive decision-making — creates an institutional contradiction that analysts at Gavekal Dragonomics describe as a structural "glitch." China's economic model over the past two decades was substantially powered by decentralized local government competition and initiative: regional leaders championed sectors from EVs to chemicals to data storage, generating the bottom-up dynamism that drove growth. Natixis's Alicia Garcia Herrero characterizes local governments as "the unsung engine of China's economic model." The new framework subordinates that dynamic to political loyalty.

Implications for Growth

Carnegie's Michael Pettis argues the plenum's accountability architecture may cause officials to prioritize inaction over experimentation, reducing the bureaucracy's capacity to adapt to changing economic conditions even as political responsiveness to Beijing improves. With Xi focused on manufacturing capacity and technological self-sufficiency rather than consumer demand, Pettis warns that more efficient implementation of the existing growth model risks deepening existing structural imbalances. The long-run innovation consequences remain uncertain, but the directional shift toward centralization appears likely to weigh on the quality of local economic governance. ---

Europe

  • COP31 opens in Turkey on November 9, with European delegations expected to be central to financing and adaptation negotiations.
  • Syrian refugees in Turkey face compounding climate pressures — drought, flooding, and agricultural disruption — adding complexity to Turkey's domestic social and economic picture.

COP31 and Turkey

Turkey's hosting of COP31, beginning November 9, places the country at the center of global climate diplomacy at a moment when its domestic circumstances illustrate the summit's core themes. The Turkish-Syrian border region is experiencing an intensifying cycle of drought and flooding that has materially disrupted agricultural output — honey production among Syrian refugee communities has fallen by approximately 50% over five years due to climate-related stress. Official development assistance flows have declined 23% from 2024 to 2025, constraining adaptation capacity precisely when it is most needed. The gap between climate financing pledges and on-the-ground implementation is expected to be a central tension at the summit.

Geopolitical Headwinds

The Iran conflict has pushed energy security up government agendas across Europe and beyond, crowding out climate action as a near-term policy priority. The US will not send an official delegation to COP31 — its second consecutive absence — ahead of its formal UNFCCC withdrawal in February 2027. For European governments that have positioned climate leadership as a strategic differentiator, the combination of US disengagement and Iran-related energy market disruption presents a difficult navigation challenge. Whether the summit can deliver concrete implementation commitments rather than aspirational language remains genuinely uncertain. ---

Global

  • COP31 opens in Turkey on November 9 against a backdrop of active conflict (Iran war), US withdrawal from UNFCCC, and a 23% drop in development assistance.
  • Xi Jinping's Fifth Plenum centralization push has global supply chain implications, as local government competition has historically been the engine of Chinese industrial scaling.
  • Climate-conflict interaction — illustrated by Syria/Turkey — is emerging as a structural theme for displaced populations globally, with nearly 90 million forcibly displaced people living in climate-vulnerable countries.

Energy Security vs. Climate Action

The Iran conflict has introduced a durable tension into the global policy landscape: governments prioritizing energy security are deprioritizing decarbonization commitments, fragmenting the coalition needed to advance COP31 objectives. With the US formally outside the UNFCCC process from February 2027 and many emerging market governments stretched by debt and development finance shortfalls, the burden of climate financing falls increasingly on a narrowing group of willing actors — primarily the EU and select Asian economies. Whether pledges made in Turkey translate into binding mechanisms is the central question heading into the summit.

Structural Divergence

China's political centralization and the US's climate withdrawal represent two distinct but reinforcing vectors of global institutional fragmentation. China's shift away from decentralized economic governance may slow the innovation diffusion that has driven global clean technology cost curves lower — particularly in EVs and solar — while US disengagement removes a major source of climate finance and diplomatic pressure. The interaction of these two trends poses a material risk to the pace of global energy transition, though the magnitude and timing of any impact remains difficult to quantify with precision.
