China’s COP Decision

By Lili Pike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXt16hTqABg/lili-pike?cmpid=BBD082126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8023>
 andAlfred Cang 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/APzICHkxM-E/alfred-cang?cmpid=BBD082126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8023>

China is considering a proposal to host the annual United Nations climate 
summit in 2028, a move that would signal the country’s intent toshow greater 
global leadership 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-21/china-said-to-weigh-potential-bid-to-host-2028-un-climate-summit?cmpid=BBD082126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8023>
 on the issue.

Officials in Beijing are debating the merits of a potential bid to stage the 
conference — which attracted more than 42,000 attendees last year in Belém, 
Brazil — after India in April withdrew an offer to host the negotiations, 
according topeople familiar with the issue 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-21/china-said-to-weigh-potential-bid-to-host-2028-un-climate-summit?cmpid=BBD082126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8023>
, who requested anonymity to discuss private deliberations.
The China Pavilion during the COP30 climate summit in Belém, Brazil.
Photographer: Alessandro Falco/Bloomberg
Discussions, which have involved the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and 
environmental officials, are at an early stage and there’s no certainty that 
China’s government will choose to move ahead with a plan to hold the COP33 
talks, the people said. Any decision to do so would require support from the 
highest levels of government, they said.

China’s foreign affairs ministry and the UN Framework Convention on Climate 
Change, or UNFCCC, didn’t immediately respond to requests for comment. The 
UNFCCC is the UN entity responsible for addressing climate change and convenes 
the annual COP — or Conference of the Parties — talks between 197 states and 
the European Union.

President Xi Jinping has made environmental protection a key theme of his 
leadership, and China aims to significantly enhance its “influence, guiding 
capacity, shaping power and moral appeal in global climate governance,” through 
2030, according to a plan published last month by the Ministry of Ecology and 
Environment, the National Development and Reform Commission, and other key 
agencies.

However, hosting COP would put a spotlight on China’s own credentials and on 
its past lack of support for initiatives promoted at the UN talks, including 
efforts to set firm targets for a global transition away from fossil fuels and 
attempts to expand the pool of donor countries obligated to contribute climate 
finance to poorer nations.

The world’s No. 2 economy is the largest emitter of greenhouse gases and is 
targeting only modest cuts of 7% to 10% by 2035 in its latest strategy 
submitted to the UN. A recent dip in emissions could reverse with the rise of 
power demand and carbon-intensive chemical manufacturing in the coming years, 
even as renewables continue to grow.

Courts challenge Trump’s green rollbacks

By Zahra Hirji 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVypnhGtqak/zahra-hirji?cmpid=BBD082126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8023>

The Trump administration’s many steps to reshape and shrink the scope of 
federal environmental protection have been met with a flood of legal challenges 
from companies, environmental groups and state officials.

So far this summer, it’s been losing more than winning 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-21/trump-s-epa-environmental-rollbacks-hit-legal-roadblocks?cmpid=BBD082126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8023>
 in court.
A smoke stack at the Marathon Petroleum Galveston Bay Refinery in Texas City, 
Texas.Photographer: Mark Felix/Bloomberg
Over the past three months, federal courts nationwide have delivered rulings 
in several cases centered on attempts by the Environmental Protection Agency to 
terminate billions of dollars in climate grants and roll back Biden-era air 
policies. In more than half of them, the judges decided against the agency.

Some of those lawsuits remain unresolved. The EPA is looking to appeal some of 
the cases it lost — or already has, according to agency spokesperson Brigit 
Hirsch.

“The Trump EPA is committed to following the law, and courts have affirmed our 
rules and actions in a variety of contexts,” said Hirsch. “These wins rarely 
get the same attention in the media as short-term losses.”

But lawyers involved in the litigation challenging the EPA say recent court 
decisions represent a crucial backstop to the Trump administration’s aggressive 
pullback on environmental work.

“The tide feels like it is shifting,” said Jillian Blanchard, an attorney at 
Lawyers for Good Government who represents multiple organizations who lost 
environmental grants last year.

The usual way a federal agency goes about rescinding an existing regulation is 
through a time-intensive process that involves proposing a new rule and taking 
public comment before issuing the final version.

That’s not how the Trump EPA went about trying to roll back Biden-era soot 
standards last fall. Instead of starting a new rulemaking process to weaken 
them, it tried to achieve that via an existing court case.

When the Biden administration rolled out the new national standards in 2024, 
more than 20 Republican-led states sued to overturn them. The agency initially 
defended the stricter pollution standards in court, but under Trump, it 
reversed course. The Trump EPA told the court that it no longer believed the 
agency had the authority to update the soot standards when it did and asked the 
court to toss them.

Instead, the US Court of Appeals for the DC Circuit in June ruled 3-0 to 
reaffirm the stronger standards.

Carbon credits questioned

By Petra Sorge 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AV7cfwcPiCo/petra-sorge?cmpid=BBD082126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8023>

A carbon-credits project funded by ExxonMobil Holdings Corp. was among dozens 
found by German authorities to be “suspicious,” to haveoverstated their 
environmental impact or to be fake 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-21/germany-revoked-suspicious-carbon-credits-bought-by-exxonmobil?cmpid=BBD082126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=8023>
.
Photographer: Brandon Bell/Getty Images
Authorities withdrew carbon credits generated by 30 China-based projects that 
claimed to reduce pollution caused by fossil-fuel extraction. The projects 
included one purchased by the Texas-headquartered oil major, according to a 
German Environment Agency report dated mid-January, which Bloomberg News 
obtained via a Freedom of Information request.

The German government has not previously shared the names of companies 
involved in the scandal over upstream emissions reductions (UER) certificates, 
which first erupted in 2024.
