Deep Dive: Europe’s Rare Earths Play
Malmberget is the iron ore mine where they will also extract the raw material 
from the iron ore that will then be sent to the process plant in Lulea.
Photographer: Carsten Snejbjerg/Bloomberg
Deep inside the Arctic Circle, more than 1,000 meters underground, the smell 
of ammonia lingers in the tunnels of Sweden’s Malmberget mine after nightly 
explosions that loosen 50,000 tons of rock. For more than a century, the mine 
has churned out iron ore, playing a huge part in the country’s economic 
history. Now, those blasting operations are also thefirst stage in Europe’s 
quest 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-11-09/rare-earths-eu-taps-china-playbook-to-cut-dependence-on-imports?cmpid=110926_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11158>
 to build a viable supply chain for rare earths.

 * Rare earths have become some of the 21st century’s defining commodities, 
used in everything from electric cars and wind turbines to data centers and 
defense equipment. Reducing dependence on imports, particularly from China, has 
become an urgent issue as the minerals are increasingly used as economic 
leverage.
 * The Nordic region is emerging as a cornerstone of the effort, combining the 
continent’s largest known deposits with abundant clean power and established 
mining expertise. Sweden’s state-owned LKAB, which operates Malmberget, is in a 
particularly advantageous position given it doesn’t have to build everything 
from scratch.
 * Mining alone won’t be enough, however. To succeed, Europe has to borrow 
from China’s playbook, where government support helped the country build 
massive processing and refining capacity to fully exploit its huge deposits.
