Hi, this is Allen Wan in Shanghai.

One thing I’ve noticed in recent months is an increase in foreign tourists 
around town. What is harder to detect is how the travelers are helping the 
economy.

The wave of visitors 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-08-12/from-chinamaxxing-to-visa-free-travel-why-tourists-are-returning-to-china?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
 is especially visible near my office in the financial district, which is home 
to popular landmarks like the iconic Oriental Pearl Tower. While a rare sight 
after the pandemic, travelers from abroad like Livia Hillier, an office worker 
for Visa in the UK, have become much more commonplace over the past year.

The new innocents abroad 
<https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3176/3176-h/3176-h.htm> are being lured by 
easy visas, great food, affordable accommodation, and a fascination for China 
stoked by social media videos of robotaxis and cyberpunk-looking cities like 
Chongqing.

“The tourist areas are beautiful,” said Hillier, who along with her husband 
and daughter was also heading to an aquarium and the Bund waterfront area. 
“Visa-free travel has definitely made life easier.”
Miniso’s flagship store in Shanghai, which is popular with visitors from 
abroad.Photographer: Qilai Shen/Bloomberg
Around 35 million tourists arrived from overseas last year, putting China in 
the ranks of some of the most well-established travel destinations in Asia — 
more than Thailand and just shy of Japan.

An oft-cited reason for the return of the foreigners is China’s visa-free 
travel policies, which have been extended to 50 countries, including the UK. In 
the first half of 2026, 18 million people arrived this way, pushing overall 
foreign tourist arrivals up by 20%.

For Beijing, luring back tourists provides something of a soft-power win in its
rivalry-in-everything with the US 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-11/manus-to-resume-independent-operations-in-unwind-of-meta-deal?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
. In a recent public opinion survey by Pew, favorable views of China reached 
record highs in a number of countries in 2025.

Despite the influx of tourists, it is unclear how much they are helping 
reverse the slide in consumer sentiment or the economic rebound as a whole. The 
international tourism industry accounted for less than 0.5% of China’s GDP in 
2025, media outlets have said, much lower than for some mature tourism 
economies like Spain.

Tourists may get more bang for the buck in China than most countries, but that 
is because of deflationary pressure thatoverall is bad for the economy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-03-13/china-s-deflation-problem-why-falling-prices-are-bad-for-the-economy?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
. Policymakers in Beijing have tried a range of ways to get people to spend, 
but that’s proven really difficult.

This may help explain why the nation also generates less than one-third of the 
inbound tourism spend of the US, according to Trip.com.

Underscoring the headwinds for the economy, official data this week showed 
that consumer prices in July grew atthe slowest pace in six months 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-09/china-s-inflation-cools-as-oil-shock-of-iran-war-starts-to-ease?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
. Factory-gate inflation eased for the first time since the Iran war erupted in 
late February.

While China recently emerged from a record bout of deflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-15/china-s-gdp-price-gauge-turns-positive-after-years-of-deflation?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
 with weak upward momentum for prices, the economy needs much more than eager 
foreign tourists to really turn things around.

The $148 Billion Worry

China is finally trying to tackle a decades-long issue that has plagued its 
real estate sector.

The problem has its roots in the fact that almost all urban land is owned by 
the state, and the government wrote the rules for leasing those plots around 
the early 1990s. It granted leases of 40 years for shopping malls, 50 years for 
industrial and office properties, and 70 years for residential buildings.

The issue matters because more than 1 trillion yuan ($148 billion) 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-11/china-s-real-estate-market-races-to-defuse-a-148-billion-time-bomb?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
 of non-residential properties now have leases of 20 years or less, meaning 
they have passed the halfway mark or gone beyond it, estimates Andrew Chan, the 
head of valuation and advisory services for Greater China at Cushman & 
Wakefield.
The Shimao Festival City building, left, and Raffles City Shanghai in Shanghai.
Photographer: Qilai Shen/Bloomberg
The expiry dates might seem far enough in the future for the problem to be 
academic but it has real economic consequences. Local insurers and developers 
typically require land terms to be longer than two decades before they will 
enter into deals, according to consultancy Jones Lang LaSalle Inc.

The two groups have an outsized role in the market after the collapse of 
foreign inflows.

And many banks won’t extend or refinance loans to properties that have less 
than a decade left on their leases, risking a systemic issue if the problem 
persists, said Cushman & Wakefield’s Chan.

The issue has been a big headache for developers and the global funds that 
once rushed to invest in the real estate sector, prolonging a slump that has
lasted more than five years 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/latest/china-s-property-crisis?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
.

Some developers have even struggled to sell certain assets because of 
dwindling lease terms, according to people familiar with the matter.

Now officials are waking up to the issue. In Shanghai, officials have 
circulated guidelines on lease renewal, outlining both the terms and the costs 
of lease extensions. Guangzhou made a similar move earlier this year.

The actions may help bring clarity to a real estate market that is trying to 
find a bottom 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-24/china-property-crisis-why-market-is-a-mess-what-stimulus-measures-are-planned?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
, giving investors confidence that their assets won’t become worthless due to 
expiring leases. Office values in some big cities fell more than 40% from peak 
levels, and developers across the market have defaulted on around $130 billion 
of debt.

Property owners would ideally like Beijing to issue a nationwide policy. The 
central government can “test the waters” with local rules in places like 
Shanghai and Guangzhou, said Lillian Duan, a Shanghai-based managing partner 
who leads the real estate practice at Chinese law firm Kaiman Legal.

Behind the Great Firewall

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

The death of former Premier Zhu Rongji this week 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-12/zhu-rongji-chinese-premier-who-overhauled-economy-dies-at-97?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
 set off a debate over the costs of his forceful reforms, which helped the 
nation become an economic powerhouse but left tens of millions of people behind.

While state media remembered Zhu as a tough technocrat, social media users had 
a more mixed take. They praised his leadership but alsolamented the painful 
effects of his decisions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/china-mourns-zhu-rongji-while-debating-his-painful-reform-costs?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
, especially the restructuring of state-owned enterprises in the 1990s that led 
to mass layoffs across industrial centers.

As many as 40 million workers lost their jobs, and with them the housing and 
welfare benefits.

“Zhu’s policy drew criticism because he let society’s most vulnerable groups 
bear the brunt of SOE reforms,” one user wrote on the Threads platform.

Such views underline how the layoffs of that era have left a deep scar on the 
country’s collective memory, even as it went on to enjoy one of the fastest 
growth spurts in modern historybefore slowing in recent years 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-04/china-softens-gdp-goal-to-range-of-4-5-to-5-as-growth-slows?cmpid=BBD081426_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260814&utm_content=7635>
.
Former Premier Zhu Rongji with ex-President Bill Clinton in Washington in 1999.
Photographer: Joyce Naltchayan/AFP/Getty Images
A Weibo post that drew hundreds of likes recalled the psychological toll on a 
generation that grew up in that period:

“I always heard that someone had died after being laid off,” the user wrote. 
“All the adults were afraid they would be laid off — they always looked 
worried.”

Middle-class Chinese who prospered from housing booms, private business and 
export manufacturing often praise Zhu as the face of a confident, upwardly 
mobile nation. Those in regions that never found a new growth engine remain 
embittered.

Zhu is still widely remembered in China as the symbol of a bygone era with 
reform being the central theme. Many posts also hailed him as a leader who 
appeared willing to take big risks to reshape the economy.

Even among those directly affected by his reform agenda, the views weren’t 
uniform. Some laid-off workers and their children now acknowledge that the 
country’s entry into the WTO under Zhu’s watch ushered in what one Weibo user 
described online as “a golden era for China,” bringing jobs, rising incomes and 
unprecedented opportunities for many others.
