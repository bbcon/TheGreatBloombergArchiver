AIndicators

Investors have awakened to the financial risks of the artificial intelligence 
buildout, and they have a deep well of precedent to draw on. Like all 
transformational technologies, it must be financed with borrowed money long 
before it can generate a return. More than 150 years ago, financierJay Cooke 
<https://bloom.bg/4hSWxBW>’s ambitious campaign to fund the  Northern Pacific 
Railroad <https://bloom.bg/3SoviVx> began to unravel not because railroads were 
a bad idea, but because the bonds financing it traded at steep discounts for 
months. His firm’s collapse triggered thePanic of 1873 
<https://bloom.bg/4g0d3gK>. As Alberto Gallo of Andromeda Capital Management 
points out, history is full of worthy projects that weren’t worthy investments:

These projects benefited the wider population later on but ended up bringing 
insufficient financial rewards to their initial capital providers, especially 
when capital was in the form of credit.

Today’s debt-financed AI buildout isn’t necessarily destined for the same 
reckoning. But credit markets have a habit of registering doubt before broader 
markets and should be heeded. Spreads revealed mounting strain long before 
newspapers declared a financial panic in 1873. They are performing much the 
same function today. As Barclays shows, main AI players’ credit spreads have 
risen sharply in recent weeks. It’s not clear whether this is temporary or the 
start of something consequential, but July’s huge turbulence, which has 
nonetheless left the S&P 500 at a fresh all-time high, leaves both 
interpretations open:

Virtually nothing matters more to markets at present than the AI buildout. 
It’s such a sudden and massive stimulus for the US that it has shifted 
macroeconomic data. Columbia Business School’sStijn Van Nieuwerburgh 
<https://bloom.bg/4wOdOAK> argues that without it, the US would be in 
recession. He estimates AI infrastructure investment at roughly 2.8% of GDP — 
larger than therailroad boom <https://bloom.bg/4xvseWn> — and it’s projected to 
keep rising:

Will credit markets permit spending on the scale that AI’s backers want? 
Points of Return is starting a monitor of metrics of credit market confidence 
in AI, and in spending itself. We’ll call it AIndicators (brand names are 
always good) and hope this will be useful in the months ahead. Feedback will be 
gratefully accepted as we try to get this right.

Spreads aren’t signaling a crisis just yet. Their recent widening nevertheless 
suggests investors are demanding greater compensation for financing the AI 
buildout. State Street Investment Management’s Michael Arone argues the 
spending boom is reaching a mature phase, even if capital expenditures keep 
rising:

Credit is quietly repricing the AI boom: Spreads are wider and yields higher, 
not at crisis levels, but enough to show investors want to be paid for the risk 
that big AI spenders may not be the eventual winners.

CapEx

The concentration of debt financing is hard to ignore. The big five 
hyperscalers – Amazon.com Inc., Alphabet Inc., Meta Platforms Inc., Microsoft 
Corp. and Oracle Corp. – are expected to spend more than $1 trillion on capex 
next year. That figure begins to approach the Pentagon’sproposed $1.5 trillion 
expenditure <https://bloom.bg/3TNkeBI>:

An expenditure of that magnitude will inevitably move bond and corporate 
credit markets. Barclays strategists Dominique Toublan and Andrew Keches find 
that six tech groups (the five above plus SpaceX) account for 8.4% of the 
duration-times-spread (DTS) – which combines a bond portfolio’s credit spread 
with its interest-rate duration to measure overall spread risk – of the US 
investment-grade corporate bond market, based on data through July 31. By 
comparison, the six largest banks — historically far more important to credit — 
account for just 7.3% of the Bloomberg US Corporate Bond Index’s DTS:

Cash Flow

Hyperscalers’ free cash flows have fallen, giving them much less room for 
maneuver. Hence investors’ demand for proof that the model is paying off, and 
willingness to punish any slippage. Alphabet reported negative free cash flow 
in its latest quarter, for the first time since going public more than two 
decades ago. Meta Platforms disappointed as its second-quarter free cash flow 
plunged 91% year-over-year to $784 million. Others weren’t immune, although the 
complaint that Apple hasn’t done enough on AI does have the corollary that it 
still enjoys very healthy free cash flows:

Stock Markets

Meanwhile, stock markets constantly reassess who will profit most. Since 
ChatGPT’s 2022 debut, investors have successively rewarded companies building 
AI models, then those supplying the infrastructure, and those making the chips 
to power it. That’s now turned into unease about whether they can ever earn an 
adequate return.

So far this year, concerns that soaring capital spending was depriving the 
companies of free cash flow have combined with worries about profits to curb 
the Magnificent Seven stocks. Blowout second-quarter earnings, particularly for 
Microsoft, which had lagged badly, reignited some enthusiasm:

Winners and Losers

The spending weighing on cash flows will pay for itself if demand for 
computing power increases as the hyperscalers hope. But past industrial 
revolutions suggest linear extrapolation won’t necessarily happen. Drawing 
parallels to telecoms in the late 1990s, Man Group’s Matt Rowe argues some 
degree of overinvestment in AI infrastructure is inevitable. What matters is 
how it’s financed and absorbed by credit markets:

You’re going to see some M&A, partnerships, some failure, and ultimately more 
consolidation and prices coming down… I think we’re partway through that. We 
haven’t seen any marquee failures, but seeing credit spreads widen could be the 
next phase of the cycle.

If the evidence investors seek doesn’t arrive or proves unconvincing, they’ll 
probably do what they’ve done throughout the AI cycle: pivot toward areas with 
stronger near-term fundamentals (as in the recent boom in chipmakers), rather 
than abandon AI altogether. This could favor “pick-and-shovel” firms involved 
in data center construction, memory chips, or providing power infrastructure:

Token Usage

The bottom line is whether people use the product once it’s ready, and what 
they pay for it. InField of Dreams <https://bloom.bg/4wQqze8> terms, the 
industry is building it; will they come? A Federal Reserve study published in 
April estimates that AI adoption among US businesses reached 18% by the end of 
2025, pointing to substantial room for further expansion. Demand tells a 
similar story. Data fromOpenRouter <https://bloom.bg/4bGL2K2>, which tracks 
consumption of tokens (the basic units of text processed by large language 
models), show usage has more than tripled since January:
Source: OpenRouter 
Does soaring token usage necessarily translate into soaring revenues for 
providers of foundational models? Not quite. The LLM Token Expenditure Index, 
which measures the effective expenditure on large language models by combining 
token prices and usage, reveals the disconnect. As token volumes surge, falling 
prices and migration toward cheaper models suggest monetization has not kept 
pace with adoption:

What Next?


In the near term, the greatest risks come from rising long-term Treasury 
yields, and the possibility of tighter monetary policy by the Fed. This could 
make life harder for those who borrowed most heavily.

JPMorgan estimates that $4.1 trillion of the $5.5 trillion in AI capex will be 
debt-financed, so rate hikes would essentially pile on the costs of this 
buildout, putting a spoke in the wheels of the massive investment cycle.

Historically, hyperscalers have been very profitable and had no difficulty 
funding most investment from their own balance sheets using cash flows, equity 
and traditional debt markets. The AI buildout has forced them to look beyond 
conventional financing.

Columbia Business School’s Van Nieuwerburgh argues that while these financing 
structures have protected the mega caps’ balance sheets, they may be shifting 
risks elsewhere. Their opacity, he says, makes it hard to assess where leverage 
sits — and whether a reverse for AI could trigger losses across the broader 
economy:

We were making the exact same argument in the financial crisis when we talked 
about mortgage-backed securities… The argument was instead of having all these 
mortgages sit on bank balance sheets, why don’t we securitize them and spread 
them to other parts of the financial system that have less leverage. What we 
didn’t appreciate at the time is that a lot of that risk transfer actually 
didn’t take place and the banks kept, often through the back door, exposures to 
these structures.

We will update the AIndicators in due course.

Jobs

This is not a normal US labor market. Jobs data published Friday showed 
payrolls fell slightly last month. Companies aren’t hiring. But initial jobless 
claims, our best real-time monitor of layoffs, were spectacularly low. There’s 
minimal firing to go with the lack of hiring:

The unemployment rate remains low, barely higher than immediately before the 
pandemic. This isn’t about demographics, as it’s also true of the joblessness 
rate among people of prime working age. Exclude the Covid spike, driven by 
causes outside the economic cycle, and the jobs market looks far less cyclical 
than it was historically:

The payrolls data have helped calm expectations of rate hikes. This isn’t the 
stuff of which intense inflationary pressure is made:

But the overall picture is that the labor market gives very little reason to 
alter monetary policy in either direction. This comment from Carl Weinberg of 
High Frequency Economics sums it up:

Recently laid-off workers are finding jobs as fast as the economy is creating 
new ones. That suggests the labor market is still balanced. When claims reach 
300,000 or 400,000, give us a call, and we will start to worry that layoffs 
have risen to levels consistent with a recession. Until then… relax.

 Survival Tips 



On the subject of streaks: I wrote a jaded note on the Boston Red Sox’ 15-game 
winning streak, which tied an 80-year-old record, suspecting it was driven by 
randomness. OK, I got that wrong. The streak had a tangible effect on their 
confidence, and they’ve just completed another nine-game streak.

But streaks still count for naught in pop charts. The UK’s current number one 
Rein Me In <https://bloom.bg/3U83yF7> has stayed there a record 20 weeks. 
There’s nothing wrong with it — but nothing special to explain such longevity.
Previous holders of this record <https://bloom.bg/4xvsgNZ> were also anodyne, 
but many benefited from movie tie-ins:Everything I Do <https://bloom.bg/45d5heK>
 by Bryan Adams (Robin Hood, Prince of Thieves), Whitney Houston’s I Will 
Always Love You <https://bloom.bg/3S4YiBo> (The Bodyguard) and Love Is All 
Around <https://bloom.bg/4xmnTEU>(Four Weddings and a Funeral). The same 
exercise for the US Billboard chart yields another line-up ofbland fare 
<https://bloom.bg/3U6nEQ1>. Any thoughts why? Have a great week everyone.
