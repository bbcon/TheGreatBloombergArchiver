China’s manufacturing dominance casts a global shadow that’s evident as far 
away as South Africa’s factory floors.

Local manufacturers, which had been banking on a post-pandemic revival to end 
a three-decade slump, are instead contending with a surge of cheap imports, 
Bloombergreports this week 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/south-african-factories-crater-as-china-imports-stunt-revival?cmpid=BBD101426_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=9930>
 from Johannesburg. Donald Trump’s tariff regime is forcing China to seek out 
new customers where trade barriers are lower.

In the first eight months of the year alone, Chinese exports to South Africa 
have climbed almost 30%. Shipments have more than doubled since the start of 
the decade.

Moody’s Ratings ranks South Africa as the most exposed nation to China’s 
competition on the African continent, citing the country’s declining 
competitiveness, its large manufacturing sector and high reliance on foreign 
value-added exports, but specifically, those from China.

“This is particularly evident in machinery and automotive components, where 
domestic producers have lost market share to Chinese exporters over the past 
decade,” it said.

More signs of China’s export strength globally emerged on Wednesday 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/china-s-trade-surplus-nears-940-billion-as-export-growth-surges?cmpid=BBD101426_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=9930>
. Shipments abroad soared 36% last month from a year earlier and imports jumped 
31%. Through September, China’s trade surplus swelled to $938 billion since the 
start of the year — on track to exceed the $1.2 trillion record set in 2025.

In South Africa, the impact is felt beyond the automotive sector. The 
country’s trade remedies authority is investigating applications from companies 
seeking relief from rising imports, spanning steel fasteners to plasterboard 
and plastic makers.

For local manufactures, the timing could not be worse, as they grapple with 
domestic struggles including infrastructure failures, a weak labor market, high
energy costs <bbg://news/stories/TMVV1PT96OSH> and a tepid economy.

Related: South Africa Central Bank Vows to Stop Entrenched Inflation 
<bbg://news/stories/TMHT11N3N0A0>

South Africa’s problem though, is not that it’s manufacturing sector is 
incapable. It’s factory capacity utilization levels, at 76% in 2026, are as 
high as global peers in the developed world, suggesting the machinery and 
skills to produce more remain in place.

“Capability and capacity is not an issue,” says Takatso Sello, senior manager 
for manufacturing at Nedbank Group, which lends to companies in the sector. 
“It’s not bad to import; but it’s a concern when you import what you can make 
in the country.”
