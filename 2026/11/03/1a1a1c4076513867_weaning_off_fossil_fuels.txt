Tailor-made solutions

By John Ainger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD110326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10940>

In Dubai three years ago, the world struck a landmark agreement to transition 
away from burning fossil fuels. Yet how nations should go about achieving that 
mammoth task to curb climate change was largely left an open question.

Brazil, the host of last year’s United Nations COP30 climate summit, is now 
trying to answer it. On Tuesday it put forward a road map to help countries 
wean their economies off oil, gas and coal over the next decade. The idea is to 
diagnose just how reliant economies are on fossil fuel exports and imports, and 
then to design tailor-made solutions to phase them out.
The Petrobras Sao Caetano refinery in Sao Paulo, Brazil.Photographer: Victor 
Moriyama/Bloomberg
“We need to move this agenda forward,” Ana Toni, an economist and chief 
executive officer of COP30, said in an interview. “Every country needs to start 
now.”

Since Dubai, countries have sparred over what shifting from fossil fuels 
actually means. The government of Saudi Arabia, for example, a major fossil 
fuel exporter, tried to walk back the Dubai deal by saying the pledge was just 
one component of an“a la carte” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-11-16/cop29-clash-brews-as-saudis-resist-restating-fossil-fuel-pledge?cmpid=BBD110326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10940>
 menu to tackle climate change. More than 80 countries called for a global deal 
to adopt a road map at COP30, but the initiative failed due toheavy opposition 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-11-23/cop30-in-brazil-a-decade-after-paris-climate-diplomacy-is-about-saving-itself?cmpid=BBD110326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10940>

In the end, host Brazil took it upon itself to come up with plans for the 
fossil fuel transition and ending deforestation. As such, they are voluntary 
and don’t need to be negotiated by countries at a UN climate summit.

The fossil fuel road map sets out three phases. In the first, lasting the next 
two years, countries will be invited to assess their dependence on fossil fuel 
imports and exports, as well as the capacity of their economies to transition 
off them. Each country will then be categorized under a dozen “archetypes” — 
ranging from frontrunners to those constrained by fiscal challenges — which in 
turn point to different policy “levers” that can accelerate the green shift. 
That will inform COP33 in 2028, which is when the UN’s next so-called Global 
Stocktake falls due.

In memoriam

By Eric Roston 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQdgGkZhizQ/eric-roston?cmpid=BBD110326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261103&utm_content=10940>

Long before countries dispatched envoys to United Nations climate 
negotiations, and even longer before companies and cities trumpeted 
clean-energy goals, Yale economist William Nordhaus set out to understand how 
climate change would redraw the global economy. He died on Oct. 30 at the age 
of 85, after creating the new discipline of climate economics with his work in 
the 1970s and becoming a Nobel laureate.

“He was a leader of the field, showing how climate change could be priced into 
economic models,” said Sarah Kapnick, global head of climate advisory at JP 
Morgan Chase & Co. “He inspired us all to continue working on these problems 
and translate the physical world into economic understanding for 
decision-making.”
William NordhausPhotographer: Eduardo Munoz Alvarez/Getty Images via Getty 
Images North America
Nordhaus started teaching at Yale University in 1967 and turned his attention 
to the problem of a heating planet. In a 1975 working paper, “Can We Control 
Carbon Dioxide? <https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/365/1/WP-75-063.pdf>“, he 
considered how policymakers might decide to confront ”the carbon dioxide 
problem.” He was the first to suggest 2C as a potential limit for human-caused 
global warming, based on an estimate of how much warming humanity has 
experienced in its deep history. The work was arguably two decades ahead of its 
time, with the same threshold enshrined in the 2015 Paris Agreement.

He was the first to suggest 2C as a potential limit for human-caused global 
warming, based on an estimate of how much warming humanity has experienced in 
its deep history. The work was arguably two decades ahead of its time, with the 
same threshold enshrined in the 2015 Paris Agreement.

It was in 1992 that he introduced what became the central tool of 
climate-economic analysis, the Dynamic Integrated Climate-Economy model. Known 
as DICE, the model quickly became the default tool for understanding the 
dynamics between economic growth and pollution, warming impacts and policy 
choices.

“It is the gold standard” for analysis even today, said Gernot Wagner, a 
climate economist at Columbia Business School.
