The electrification COP

By Akshat Rathi 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUdOobfNzkQ/akshat-rathi?cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>

The COP31 climate summit might count as a success if there is a commitment 
from all countries attending to significantly boost the electrification of 
their economies, according to the event’s head of negotiations.

One of the main goals of the organizers of the United Nations climate 
conference starting next week in Antalya, Turkey, is to have countries party to 
the Paris Agreement to sign up to a goal to increase the global share of final 
energy demand coming from electricity to 35% by 2035, from about 20% today. So 
far, however, the ambition remains only a voluntary one for countries to commit 
to.

“I would like to see a strong outcome on electrification in our presidency-led 
decision,” saidChris Bowen <https://minister.dcceew.gov.au/bowen>, Australia’s 
climate change and energy minister and COP31’s president of negotiations, on the
Zero 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/green-zero-emissions-podcast?sref=jjXJRDFv&cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>
 podcast. That decision would have to be signed off by all countries attending 
the summit, which starts on Nov. 9.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-05/how-australia-gives-its-citizens-free-electricity?cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>
Watch the interview here: How Australia Gives Its Citizens Free Electricity 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-05/how-australia-gives-its-citizens-free-electricity?cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>

The rise in oil and gas prices linked to ongoing wars in Europe and the Middle 
East has forced fossil-fuel importing countries — which make upthree-fourths 
<https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/energy-security-in-an-insecure-world/>
 of the world’s population — to find ways to reduce consumption and find 
alternatives. Electrification is part of the solution. With costs falling for 
solar panels and batteries, many countries have opted tospeed up the transition 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-energy-transition-iran-war/?srnd=undefined&sref=jjXJRDFv&cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>
 to electric cars and heat pumps.

“Electrification is an unquestionably good thing,” Bowen said. “Even if your 
grid is powered by coal and you don’t have solar panels at home, if you’re just 
charging [an electric car] off a coal-fired grid, you are reducing emissions by 
something like 60%.”

Primary energy is how fuel is found in nature, like crude oil; final energy is 
how fuel is consumed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-11-17/primary-energy-vs-final-energy-why-replacing-fossil-fuels-won-t-be-so-hard?cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>
, for example as gasoline or electricity; and useful energy is the fraction of 
final energy that is converted into work, such as motion or cooling.

While it’s true that about 80% of the world’s primary energy comes from fossil 
fuels, only about 65% of its final energy is derived from coal, oil or gas. 
Thus electrification is foremost an energy efficiency solution, because it 
reduces primary energy demand without cutting the useful outcomes that citizens 
want.

The electrification goal for COP31 got a boost when China, the world’s largest 
emitter of greenhouse gases and the biggest manufacturer of electricity 
technologies, indicated in October that itsupports the target 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-21/china-backs-push-for-global-electrification-at-un-climate-talks?cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>
. Bowen is hoping it can be pushed further.

Dhaka market shifts to solar

By Stephen Stapczynski 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASLxhpk8tMA/stephen-stapczynski?cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>
 andArun Devnath 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQeSdimdcl0/arun-devnath?cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>

A cramped market in the heart of Dhaka has become an unlikely ground zero for 
Bangladesh’s energy transition. Down narrow aisles crowded with shoppers and 
delivery workers, tiny storefronts are overflowing with rows of solar panels 
and batteries.

Monju Hossain, a sales representative for Dongjin Group Bangladesh, said the 
retailer is struggling to keep up with demand, with sales jumping 50% in recent 
months from a year earlier. The country’sworsening energy crisis 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-12/gas-supply-woes-are-delaying-bangladesh-s-industrial-dreams?cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>
, triggered by the war in the Middle East, is pushing households and businesses 
to seek alternatives to an increasingly unreliable grid.
Solar panels on display at the Kaptan Bazar in Dhaka on Oct. 27.Photographer: 
Stephen Stapczynski/Bloomberg
“The blackouts in Bangladesh have gotten worse, which has increased the need 
for our equipment,” said Hossain, in a shop on the second floor of the Kaptan 
Bazar. “Demand from industrial customers has increased significantly, as they 
have to keep factories running.”

The busy market offers a glimpse of how the Middle East energy shock is 
accelerating the shift away from fossil fuels in poorer countries. From the
Philippines 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-12/china-is-supercharging-a-rooftop-solar-boom-in-the-philippines?cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>
 toPakistan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-30/pakistan-s-solar-boom-pits-china-s-clean-energy-exports-against-coal-investments?cmpid=BBD110526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261105&utm_content=11055>
 and Nigeria, people are turning to solar and batteries to cut utility bills or 
just to keep the lights and machinery on. It’s a threat to the long-term demand 
outlook for liquefied natural gas, given that populous developing nations like 
Bangladesh have long been touted as high-growth markets for the super-chilled 
fuel.
