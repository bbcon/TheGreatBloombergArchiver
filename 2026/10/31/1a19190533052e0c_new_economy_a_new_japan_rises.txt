Before I moved to Asia to lead the region’s economics coverage in late 2009, 
there was no question where I’d be based. Tokyo had long been the location for 
the job, and that’s where I was headed (and pleased to go). But even as I 
arrived, its legacy as a natural headquarters for Asia-wide operations was 
clearly waning.

While still Asia’s biggest (and the world’s No. 2) economy when I got there, 
Japan wouldlose those titles 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2010-08-16/china-economy-passes-japan-s-in-second-quarter-capping-three-decade-rise?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10207>
 to China during my first year on the job. Hong Kong, as well as Singapore, 
were increasingly attractive places from which to capture Asia’s evolving 
dynamism. So, fast-forwarding to this month, when I heard about aninitiative 
<https://fincity.tokyo/en/> that was marketing Tokyo as an Asian financial hub, 
I naturally viewed the idea with some skepticism.

What I discovered was that the narrative is different this time. Today, the 
tagline is about unlocking value in Japan itself. After years of laborious work 
by policymakers, regulators, investors and other market participants, corporate 
governance reforms are now bearing some serious fruit. Things are possible 
today in Japan that weren’t even a decade ago, and moreover, aren’t currently 
conceivable in any other Asian economy of Japan’s scale.

It’s now possible for foreign firms to play leading roles in the restructuring 
of key parts of Japan’s business architecture, and tomake big bucks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-08/bain-exits-kioxia-after-landmark-chip-deal-yields-record-returns?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10207>
 in the process. As detailed below, one prime example of this new reality 
involves a legacy enterprise that once was targeted for downsizing by the 
postwar US occupation.

Japan is now so dynamic that stock trading is “going through the roof,” says 
Hisanori Koto, head of Japan equities at Bank of America Corp. Indeed, average 
trading volume has more than doubled since just 2022. And with regard to 
investing in Japan, consider this: there’s a case to be made that Japan’s 
demographics — typically viewed as an enormous economic handicap given the 
aging and shrinking population — is actually a plus.

What Tokyo advocates hope is that veteran global asset management companies 
will set up shop (or expand) operations in Japan to improve returns on capital. 
In doing so, they will boost productivity, earning the Japanese more money to 
subsidize swellingranks of retirees 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/japan-s-centenarian-ranks-reach-100-000-as-welfare-costs-rise?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10207>
. The lure for these companies is handsome profits as they avail themselves of 
the corporate restructuring options now possible in Japan — and the enormous 
pool of domestic savings.

A great example of what can be done is embodied in Kioxia Holdings Corp., 
which today is one of the world’s biggest memory chipmakers. Kioxia used to be 
part of Toshiba Corp. — the electronics giant whose history stretches back to 
the 1800s. Toshiba was once so influential that it was among 18 companies 
targeted in the late 1940s by the US occupation for disinvesting parts of its 
operations. But by the 2010s,it’s influence had waned 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2018-02-14/toshiba-appoints-new-ceo-as-company-undergoes-major-revamp?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10207>
 and it was in significant trouble.

In the old Japan, such troubles would be socialized. Good parts of a business, 
like Toshiba’s memory chip manufacturing, would support the struggling parts. 
Restructuring wasn’t an option. This philosophy is what led to the nation’s 
lost economic decades, starting in the 1990s. But after years of 
corporate-governance reforms, championed by the late Prime Minister Shinzo Abe, 
something different happened with Toshiba.

In 2018, a group led by Boston-based Bain Capital bought its memory chip unit 
for $18 billion. Now called Kioxia, the firm could go full-tilt toward growth 
and investment. By 2024, Kioxia was listed. By July of this year, Bain had 
fully exited its stake, havingnetted around $15 billion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-28/rescued-by-the-ai-boom-japan-s-kioxia-faces-a-capex-race?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10207>
, people familiar with the matter told Bloomberg. Kioxia is now among Japan’s 
top 15 firms by market capitalization.
Hiroo Ota, chief executive of Kioxia Holdings Corp.Photographer: Shoko 
Takayasu/Bloomberg
Having a foreign private equity firm come in and slice off a crown jewel of 
Japan’s technology sector 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/apollo-to-support-15-billion-ai-infrastructure-project-in-japan?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10207>
, revamp it and make a fortune in the process would have been inconceivable in 
the old days. (It’s also inconceivable in a place like China, where the 
Communist leadership has for years been pulling out all the stops to remove any 
foreign presence from its tech supply chain.)

“The mindset has changed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-21/apollo-s-rowan-sees-japan-ready-to-roar-there-s-a-new-swagger?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10207>
 in Japan,” says Paul Sheard, who’s been following the nation’s economy since 
the 1970s and previously served as S&P Global’s chief economist. “I see 
enormous scope for that kind of restructuring to take place,” he said of the 
Kioxia deal.
Pedestrians cross an intersection in Shibuya district in Tokyo, April 17.
Photographer: Noriko Hayashi/Bloomberg
The Kioxia example is hardly an exception. New York-based KKR made 20 times 
the value of its initial investment in chip gear-maker Kokusai Electric Corp., 
with total proceeds clocking in at$5.5 billion <bbg://news/stories/TFBRZZKJH6YL>
. Kokusai was hived off from Hitachi Ltd., another Japanese corporate giant, 
which once modeled itself on America’s General Electric Co. and was the 
nation’s second-largest manufacturer. Again, it is very tough to imagine such a 
deal in China.

As for the public markets, interest in Japan’s equities is soaring among a 
generation that’s grown up knowing that sticking to cash is a losing strategy 
amid inflation. The cohort that lived through the bubble collapse of the 1990s 
is now moving off the scene. There’s a new generation of business managers 
taking charge who’ve been accustomed tonominal GDP growth 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2023-12-02/bloomberg-new-economy-china-s-economic-heft-sinks-for-first-time-since-1994?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10207>
.

“The household sector’s money is now moving,” says Tokio Morita, executive 
director of FinCity Tokyo, a partnership to promote Tokyo as a top-class 
financial center. Morita, who previously worked at Japan’s financial regulator, 
says “if they put money in deposits,the inflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-22/japan-inflation-rises-above-boj-target-level-backing-rate-hikes?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10207>
 will bite their financial assets. People have started really recognizing that, 
so that’s a real change.”
Office workers outside a securities firm in Tokyo.Photographer: Shoko 
Takayasu/Bloomberg
David Semaya, who serves as an “ambassador” for FinCity Tokyo and whose 
experience in Japan dates back to the 1970s, says “there was a generation that 
wouldn’t invest at all because of the experience they had with the bubble, and 
that’s now gone.”

In the corporate sector, there are many firms that were run by the same family 
for generations. Some of them are in play formergers and acquisitions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-13/megadeals-like-toyota-s-set-japan-on-thrilling-run-of-record-m-a?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261031&utm_content=10207>
, says Semaya, now with Sumitomo Mitsui Trust Asset Management Co. That’s 
because the children either don’t want to go into business, or there simply are 
no children to take the business over.

“We look at demographics as negative,” Semaya says. “But actually I think it’s 
a huge positive.”

Certainly, many investors are seeing the positive. Foreign net inflows into 
Japanese equities have broken new ground this year, with overseas ownership 
hitting arecord 34.7% 
<https://www.jpx.co.jp/english/markets/statistics-equities/examination/vk0khi000001sxp5-att/e-bunpu2025.pdf>
, data showed in July.

Ryusuke Yokoyama, chief executive of Tokyo Stock Exchange Inc., said at the 
time of international investors, “it’s clear that their perspective on the 
Japanese market has definitely changed.”
