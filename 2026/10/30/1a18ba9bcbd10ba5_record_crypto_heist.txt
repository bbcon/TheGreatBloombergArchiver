Deep Dive: Risk of a Water Network Collapse
Water supply disruptions leave households in Westbury without running water.
Photographer: Leon Sadiki/Bloomberg
When the taps suddenly run dry in the low-slung house that Sharlene Smith 
shares with her ailing mother in Westbury, Johannesburg, the 59-year-old South 
African isforced to leave 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-10-30/johannesburg-risks-a-total-collapse-of-its-water-network?cmpid=103026_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261030&utm_content=10773>
 the safety of their property, and cross dangerous territory in search of water.

 * Often, Smith finds what she needs at a church or a mosque, around a 
kilometer away. There, she joins other residents collecting supplies from large 
storage tanks. Sometimes, the municipality’s water entity, Johannesburg Water, 
gives notice that the network will be shut down for maintenance or repairs, 
allowing her to prepare by filling bowls, buckets and the bathtub.
 * Water tankers tend to stay away, and when they do turn up, they’re escorted 
by the police to protect workers from gang-related shootings that local 
activists say have killed 22 people since July.
 * The community, like others elsewhere in the country, is caught in a cycle 
of urban decline after years of mismanagement and underinvestment in basic 
services. Potholed roads hinder travel, power cuts disrupt work and education, 
and public safety has deteriorated.
 * But for Smith, it is the lack of water, which can last for weeks at a time, 
that is the most pressing concern. Without it, drinking and cooking, washing 
and caring for her mother — while keeping waterborne diseases at bay — becomes 
so much harder.
