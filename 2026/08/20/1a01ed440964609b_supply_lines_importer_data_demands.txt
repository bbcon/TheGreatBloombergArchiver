US Seeks Importer, Company Data to Build Up Trade Enforcement

The Trump administration offered a glimpse at how it plans to improve trade 
enforcement, starting with a closer look at who is importing goods into the US.

A Federal Register filing published Wednesday gives importers 30 days to 
confirm that their “accurate and complete” information — including physical 
address, email address and phone number — is on file with Customs and Border 
Protection.

If by Sept. 18 an importer’s Form 5106 
<https://www.cbp.gov/sites/default/files/2026-06/cbp_form_5106.pdf> is 
inaccurate or incomplete, CBP will immediately void the associated “Importer Of 
Record” number, effectively barring them from bringing goods into the US, the
notice warned 
<https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/19/2026-16911/accuracy-of-importer-of-record-data-submitted-to-cbp>
.

CBP’s scrutiny of importers marks an initial step in the implementation of an 
executive order President Donald Trump signed in June. Former CBP auditor Tom 
Jesukiewicz said better data on importers will lay the groundwork for better 
enforcement.

“Before CBP can address transshipment, origin, valuation, bonding or 
collectability, it has to know who the importer is, where it operates and who 
is responsible,” said Jesukiewicz, who retired recently after four decades as 
an auditor in CBP’s Office of Trade and is consulting part time.

“They want to get the information up front, know who they’re dealing with and 
secure the risk,” he said.

Shell Companies

Wednesday’s notice is a call to action for the hundreds of thousands of 
importers registered with the agency. It also specifies that customs brokers 
filing on behalf of importers should provide their client’s information, not 
provide their own — a common practice. It also takes aim at shell company 
importers by barring the use of registered agents and P.O. boxes, for example.

The White House acknowledges that historically high duties on imports have 
increased the incentive to move production to countries with lower tariff rates 
— which is not illegal. The high-stakes trade war has also increased the 
incentive to cheat, and last week the White Housedetailed an AI tool 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-13/trump-trade-enforcers-deploy-ai-to-crack-down-on-tariff-dodging?cmpid=BBD082026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7738>
 it’s developing to “detect, deter and prevent” tariff evasion.

That tool needs more data.

Jesukiewicz pointed out that the CBP’s importer identity form also includes 
optional fields for data on company structure, beneficial ownership and company 
officers, and said he’d be surprised if those details stay optional.

Armed with more information, CBP can establish risk profiles and determine 
which importers are in good standing. Those factors will allow the agency to 
assign larger bond requirements to riskier importers, for example.

“CBP will need to determine good standing, risk tier and bond sufficiency,” he 
said. “Year established, beneficial ownership, related companies, responsible 
officers, incorporation information, banking relationships, anticipated import 
activity and domestic assets all become relevant.”
