US

  • Federal EV subsidies have been eliminated, raising the cost premium of electric vehicles to approximately $4,800 over comparable internal-combustion models.
  • Elevated gasoline prices — reportedly around $5.50 per gallon in parts of the Pacific Northwest — are driving renewed consumer interest in EVs despite the loss of federal incentives.

Consumer and Energy Dynamics

The removal of federal EV tax credits has materially altered the economics of electric vehicle adoption. With new EVs carrying a roughly $4,800 premium over traditional alternatives and no subsidy offset, the financial calculus has become highly individualized — dependent on local electricity costs, annual mileage, and driving patterns. Survey data from Cox Automotive indicate that nearly half of American drivers report elevated gasoline prices have increased their likelihood of purchasing an EV, suggesting demand impulses remain intact even as the policy tailwind has disappeared.

Fiscal and Policy Context

The expiration of federal EV incentives reflects a broader shift in US fiscal and industrial policy. The downstream effect — shifting EV adoption economics from a broadly favorable to a more case-specific calculus — may temper the pace of fleet electrification in the near term, with implications for automakers carrying elevated EV inventory and capex commitments. Whether gasoline price pressure proves sufficient to sustain adoption momentum absent policy support remains an open question. ---

LatAm

  • Brazil's presidential runoff takes place Sunday, with polls showing a statistical tie following a first-round result of Flávio Bolsonaro 47.0% versus incumbent Lula 45.2%.
  • Deforestation under the Lula government has fallen approximately 86% from 2023 through 2026, a defining policy distinction heading into the second round.
  • The Amazon land-use divide — environmental enforcement versus settler property rights — has emerged as the central fault line of the campaign.

Political Economy

Brazil's presidential runoff presents a structurally important policy fork. Incumbent Lula has anchored his second term to environmental enforcement and Paris Agreement commitments, delivering an 86% reduction in deforestation — a result that resonates internationally but has generated significant domestic friction in agricultural frontier communities. Challenger Flávio Bolsonaro has pledged to eliminate only illegal deforestation, allow legally authorized forest-clearing, and privatize land within currently protected zones in Pará state — a platform that commands overwhelming support in commodity-producing municipalities such as Novo Progresso, where he captured 84% in the first round.

Market Implications

The policy divergence carries direct consequences for Brazil's agricultural export sector, sovereign ESG positioning, and foreign direct investment flows tied to sustainability criteria. A Bolsonaro victory could accelerate land regularization and expand the agricultural frontier, supporting near-term rural output but potentially triggering ESG-linked capital outflows and trade friction with the European Union, which has conditioned market access on deforestation commitments. The outcome remains genuinely uncertain, and markets should treat the binary risk accordingly. ---

Global

  • Gulf AI infrastructure faces compounding risks: Houthi strikes have already damaged facilities including an Aramco site near Riyadh, and enhanced physical security is estimated to raise data center costs by 5%–7%.
  • Saudi Arabia's flagship investment conference, originally scheduled for October 26, was postponed due to escalating Houthi attacks — a concrete signal of how the US-Iran conflict is disrupting Gulf economic diplomacy.
  • Nigeria's small-scale solar capacity now exceeds grid-delivered power, with 2026 additions already matching the combined total of 2024 and 2025.

Gulf AI and Geopolitical Risk

The Gulf states' AI infrastructure ambitions — built on advantages of land, energy abundance, and relatively low labor costs — are encountering a risk dimension that was not fully priced at the outset: physical vulnerability to military strikes. Saudi Arabia and the UAE nearly tripled data center capacity between 2020 and 2025, with BloombergNEF projecting a further three- to six-fold expansion by 2035. However, the widening US-Iran conflict, prosecuted partly through Houthi proxies, has introduced asymmetric risk to fixed infrastructure that cannot be relocated. The tension between building at home to capture competitive advantages versus building abroad to reduce physical exposure presents Gulf sovereigns with a strategic dilemma without an obvious resolution.

Energy Transition at the Margin

Nigeria's solar inflection illustrates how energy transitions can accelerate outside conventional policy frameworks. Driven by chronic grid failure, high fossil fuel prices, and falling costs of Chinese-manufactured photovoltaic equipment, small-scale solar in Nigeria has surpassed grid capacity — a development with implications for energy demand patterns across Sub-Saharan Africa and for the longer-term trajectory of African hydrocarbon consumption.
