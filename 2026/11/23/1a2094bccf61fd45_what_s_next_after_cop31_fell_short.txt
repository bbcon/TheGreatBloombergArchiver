How talks failed at COP31

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD112326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11736>
 andJohn Ainger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD112326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11736>

COP31 is not the first time the Conference of the Parties, as these annual UN 
talks are known, has failed to adopt a final declaration. But the failure in 
Antalya, Turkey, ends a five-year streak of more obvious displays of consensus 
even as the planet is on track to breach a critical 1.5C threshold for warming 
set out in the Paris Agreement.

The outcome underscores the limits of a decades-old diplomatic process 
designed to steer the world’s fight against climate change just as 
multilateralism is being strained by wars, economic uncertainty and rising 
populism that prioritizes domestic interests over collective action.

This is how it happened, as told to Bloomberg reporters on the ground by 
negotiators inside the rooms:

Delegates from Saudi Arabia, small island countries, China and India converged 
at the office of the top negotiators, demanding to be seen as time ran down 
over the weekend at the United Nations climate summit in Turkey. “Several 
countries would like to speak to the presidency,” one of the delegates told a 
guard. “You need to be inclusive,” insisted another.

Eventually, the diplomats were allowed upstairs for closed-door meetings with 
ministers from Turkey and Australia, co-leaders of the COP31 summit. Their 
exchange of grievances — and some ultimatums — over a proposed agreement meant 
to cap off the two-week conference resulted, by Sunday, in a grim milestone:no 
final deal on a new declaration to unite the world against rising temperatures 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/un-climate-talks-slide-toward-collapse-as-nations-reject-deal?cmpid=BBD112326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11736>
.

Watch reporter John Ainger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD112326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11736>
’s take:
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-23/what-the-collapse-of-cop31-means-for-climate-diplomacy-video?cmpid=BBD112326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11736>
For years, the European Union and vulnerable nations have played the role of 
consensus-builders by backing final bargains partly in order to keep the UN 
process inching ahead. This time, however, the faction moved toward defiance 
and rejected a proposed deal that enshrined carbon capture technology at the 
behest of Saudi Arabia’s negotiators.

Russia, meanwhile, ruled out any mention of fossil fuels in a final decree — 
effectively rejecting a key requirement for Switzerland, Panama and other 
nations who insisted the summit should agree to accelerate the world’s 
transition off coal, oil and gas.

Even UN-backed reports on bedrock climate science became divisive. Saudi 
Arabia, Kenya and other nations objected to efforts to compel the delivery of 
three key science reports before the 2028 conference formally takes stock of 
the world’s climate progress.

And there were no financial deals meant to help developing nations pursue 
economic growth without fossil fuels and manage climate disaster. The European 
Union had preemptively ruled out any new finance before COP31 talks began. Even 
setting a deadline to enumerate the details of a previous pledge for adaptation 
support proved too much.

Turkey, which served as COP31 president, was focused on advancing its 
voluntary initiative to boost the world’s use of electricity and enshrining it 
in an “Antalya Pact” at the end of the summit. But that effort met resistance 
from India and other nations leery of adopting another new top-down target. 
Some negotiators complained the campaign appeared to come at the expense of 
other priorities, detracting from the hard diplomacy needed to forge consensus.

Carbon capture — the final straw

Then came carbon capture, which hadn’t been on the agenda when COP31 kicked 
off — yet it caused one of the biggest fractures over the course of the talks.

Carbon capture seeks to combine fossil-fuel burning infrastructure with 
technology to trap planet-warming pollution. At COP31 it was championed by oil 
producers and viewed with hostility by critics who saw it as a way to prolong 
the use of fossil fuels.

A proposal that would call to accelerate the deployment of carbon capture and 
enumerate specific targets for its use was tucked into a proposed agreement on 
Nov. 16, catching many delegates by surprise. “This falls from the sky as if it 
is the solution to the whole problem,” said Wopke Hoekstra, the EU’s climate 
chief.
Kiara Worth/UN Climate ChangePhotographer: Kiara worth
Saudi Arabia had led the push for carbon capture and insisted on its 
inclusion, even as a coalition of roughly 130 countries, including the 
Netherlands, United Kingdom and the Marshall Islands, tried to dial it down and 
add stronger commitments on the fossil fuel transition.

As positioned hardened, other countries still hoping for a strong deal were 
effectively left negotiating with themselves, one delegate observed.

The dynamic was on display when EU officials met with Saudi Arabia in search 
of a breakthrough and the country’s energy minister, Prince Abdulaziz bin 
Salman, said there was no evidence that fossil fuels caused climate change, 
according to people familiar with the matter.

What’s next for the COP process

The breakdown of past UN climate summits has helped pave the way for major 
breakthroughs in subsequent conferences.

This year’s summit actually advanced many items that were mandated for 
negotiations, including efforts around adaptation and ensuring communities 
aren’t left behind by the energy shift. The failed final declaration wasn’t 
even obligatory; in fact, most COP summits haven’t produced them.

But by staking a cover decision out as a prime goal heading into the 
conference, Turkey and Australia ensured the subsequent failure to adopt one 
would be the main metric of their performance in Antalya.
Ethiopia’s Minister of Finance and chief negotiator at COP31 Fitsum Assefa
Photographer: Kiara Worth/UN Climate Change
Sunday’s outcome effectively hands more work to Ethiopia, the host of next 
year’s COP32 summit, even as it intensifies skepticism about whether the 
process can deliver.

And it could help galvanize smaller initiatives proceeding outside of the UN 
climate summits — including the so-called Santa Marta Process focused on 
helping countries shift away from fossil fuels. Their proliferation could 
present a challenge to the consensus-driven COPs where diplomatic outcomes can 
have more power but be far harder to achieve. But as smaller side efforts gain 
momentum, they can potentially be propelled into the COP negotiations.

China’s $150 billion side show

By Lili Pike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXt16hTqABg/lili-pike?cmpid=BBD112326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11736>

At a UN climate conference that was low on delivery 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-23/failure-of-cop31-talks-shows-hard-limits-of-climate-cooperation?cmpid=BBD112326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11736>
 and high on dysfunction, China’s clean-technology companies showed that some 
aspects of the green transition are persevering despite geopolitical tensions.

The country’s official pavilion at COP31 hosted events featuring more than 10 
world-leading Chinese companies. Solar manufacturer Longi Green Energy 
Technology Co. profiled its growing data-center battery business, CATL promoted 
efforts to reduce the carbon footprint of its batteries and a state-owned power 
company highlighted the sweeping scale of its hydropower dams in Southeast Asia.

These companies have been a point of pride for the Chinese government, even as 
its diplomats maintained a relatively low profile at COP31 in the absence of 
the US. China’s exports of electric cars and batteries have clinched records 
this year, with total clean-tech exports exceeding 1 trillion yuan ($150 
billion). That’s enough to shave the equivalent ofBrazil’s annual emissions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/china-s-green-exports-enough-to-offset-brazil-s-emissions-crea?cmpid=BBD112326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261123&utm_content=11736>
 off the global carbon footprint.

The main accomplishment of this year’s COP presidency was getting more than 75 
<https://www.nrdc.org/press-releases/more-75-countries-180-businesses-sign-global-electrification-pledge-cop31>
 countries to pledge to accelerate electrification — Chinese clean-tech 
manufacturers will play a significant part in facilitating that effort.

China has “the most complete new energy industrial supply chain,” Chinese Vice 
President Han Zheng said at an event 
<https://www.news.cn/20261112/4832155420b04c17918f3280c69d2b00/c.html> at the 
pavilion, pledging to deepen cooperation with other developing countries.

At the conference, Tuya Inc. — a Hangzhou-based, NYSE-listed smart-systems 
provider — showed off its new AI-powered weather forecasting functionality, 
picking up new European partners along the way.

The new energy market is particularly active right now “because people are 
going around searching for solutions,” said Alex Yang, the company’s 
co-founder, pointing to fuel supply disruptions in Russia and the Middle East, 
as well as extreme heat in Europe.

“The world needs the green tech from China to accelerate the pace of curbing 
climate change,” said Tonny Xie, the director of Bluetech Clean Air Alliance, a 
Beijing-based nonprofit supporting Chinese clean technology startups.
