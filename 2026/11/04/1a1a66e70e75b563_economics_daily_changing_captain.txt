Turnover at the Top

This week marks the first formal step in choosing a successor to European 
Central Bank President Christine Lagarde.

Candidate nominations are due on Friday 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-03/eurogroup-calls-for-ecb-presidency-nominations-by-nov-6?cmpid=BBD110426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261104&utm_content=10980>
, withonly two names 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/de-cos-and-knot-are-top-ecb-candidates-after-early-lagarde-exit?cmpid=BBD110426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261104&utm_content=10980>
 currently doing the rounds: Dutchman Klaas Knot and Spain’s Pablo Hernandez de 
Cos. Barring a surprise, one of them will get the job.

The bigger unknown is whether the winner will actually finish their term. 
Lagarde is leaving 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-30/lagarde-exits-ecb-after-big-wins-some-errors-and-a-long-goodbye?cmpid=BBD110426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261104&utm_content=10980>
 almost nine months early. She is the eighth out of 26 Executive Board members 
since the ECB’s creation in 1998 to exit before the end of their stint.

What’s true is that its eight-year terms are hardly short. Federal Reserve 
governor mandates are even longer, lasting 14 years.

Policymakers are granted job security for good reason: to free them from 
political pressure, the electoral calendar and considerations about their next 
role. Even so, exactly those issues tend to feature at the ECB.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-29/lagarde-says-she-can-t-stay-neutral-in-current-situation-video?cmpid=BBD110426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261104&utm_content=10980>
While some, particularly German policymakers, left either for personal reasons 
or in protest over policy, others have faced political pressure or have gone to 
pursue new career opportunities. That latter applies toIsabel Schnabel 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-24/schnabel-will-leave-ecb-board-early-for-senior-role-at-imf?cmpid=BBD110426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261104&utm_content=10980>
, who will exit a year early for a job at the International Monetary Fund.

Lagarde says her departure in February is to let her speak out 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/lagarde-quits-ecb-to-shed-neutrality-and-enter-political-fray-mvtp2d3u?cmpid=BBD110426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261104&utm_content=10980>
 on political matters precluded by her role. Her exit coincides with the final 
stretch of campaigning before France’s presidential election.

Turnover at the top of central banks is hardly unusual. Mark Carney notably 
left the Bank of Canada early to become Bank of England governor — for a 
shorter term than the stipulated eight years.

Does it actually matter when policymakers abandon secure terms granted to 
ensure central bank independence?

In the euro zone, it can be an institutional inconvenience, given that 
appointments to theECB’s six-person board 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-27/four-ecb-scenarios-sketch-a-succession-plan-for-post-lagarde-era?cmpid=BBD110426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261104&utm_content=10980>
 require high-level political horsetrading that balances nationality, views on 
monetary policy and gender.

Turnover has practical implications too. Such nominations used to be 
staggered, but are now lumped together. The ECB will see four of six board 
seats change within a year, the biggest overhaul in more than a decade.

Lagarde herself insisted last week that her exit won’t cause a problem.

“Business continuity has always been the mantra for this institution — it has 
never failed,” she said. “And you can rest assured that until Feb. 7 I’m in 
charge, I’m focused and I will be working without any reservation, without any 
excuses.”

Need-to-Know Research

Housing markets are in varying states of rough shape right round the world, 
and Morgan Stanley’s latest look atAustralia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-04/australia-s-household-spending-exceeds-estimate-boosted-by-fuel?cmpid=BBD110426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261104&utm_content=10980>
 shows the so-called lucky country isn’t offering an exception these days.Fresh 
figures 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-01/australia-s-home-prices-fall-further-ahead-of-expected-rate-hike?cmpid=BBD110426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261104&utm_content=10980>
 on property Down Under suggest the nation is on track for its biggest price 
declines in over 40 years.

“Persistently weak sentiment, elevated cost of living pressures and tighter 
credit are weighing on demand,” a strategist and economist team led by Chris 
Read wrote in a note this week. (Conditions that apply to the US and 
elsewhere.) The central bank’s fifth ratehike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-03/australia-raises-key-rate-to-18-year-high-in-split-decision?cmpid=BBD110426_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261104&utm_content=10980>
 this year, on Tuesday, took variable mortgage rates closer to 7%.

Morgan Stanley is expecting a 10% to 15% peak-to-trough decline in Australian 
house prices by the second half of 2027. The midpoint, of 12.5%, would mark the 
largest decline since 1982-83, the team said in a deep-dive into the topic last 
month. The bank says that changes in tax policy have “permanently” weakened the 
economics of investing in property, while “migration will no longer add as much 
to housing demand as it did in the past.”
