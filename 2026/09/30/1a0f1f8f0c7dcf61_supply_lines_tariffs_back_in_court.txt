A Chicago-area toy company and the Justice Department will argue over the 
legality of President Donald Trump’s tariffs before a federal court on 
Wednesday.

Sound familiar?

Learning Resources 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/754379Z:US?cmpid=BBD093026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9354>
, the same family-owned maker of educational toys that took Trump’s “emergency” 
tariffs all the way to the Supreme Court and won, is behind one of three main 
lawsuits challenging tariffs linked to forced labor imposed this summer on 
imports from more than 80 countries.

A three-judge panel at the Court of International Trade will ask questions and 
hear arguments over duties imposed under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, 
which authorizes tariffs to address an “unreasonable or discriminatory” 
practice by another country deemed harmful to US commerce.

 * Bloomberg’s Zoe Tillman will be in the courtroom so be sure to look for her 
post-hearing analysis, and you canclick here 
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fzeaLzHRXiE> to listen live, starting at 10 
a.m. in New York.
Just hours after the Supreme Court overturned his so-called reciprocal tariffs 
in February, Trump vowed to restore them using other authorities and almost 
immediately put a temporary 10% tariff on goods imported from nearly every 
country under Section 122.

Read More: Trump’s New Tariffs Prompt Lawsuit From Small Businesses 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-24/trump-administration-is-sued-over-new-round-of-global-tariffs?cmpid=BBD093026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9354>

The Section 122 tariffs were also challenged in court, though the 
administration was allowed to keep collecting the duties while the decision is 
appealed. According to Customs and Border Protection, importers owed more than 
$31 billion from the stop-gap tariffs when they expired in July.

Meanwhile, a US Trade Representative report accused 60 economies 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-23/us-sets-new-forced-labor-duties-as-trump-resurrects-tariff-wall?cmpid=BBD093026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260930&utm_content=9354>
 including the 27-nation European Union of failing to impose and or effectively 
execute forced-labor import rules, and imposed tariffs of 10% or 12.5% just as 
the Section 122 duties expired.

“Before the court’s ruling in February, the US average tariff rate was around 
13.5%. Neither the temporary 10% Section 122 duties, nor the subsequent new 
tariffs — many under Section 301 and some under Sections 232 and 338 — have 
fully replaced the overruled tariffs,” said Bloomberg Economics’ Nicole 
Gorton-Caratelli.

The small businesses and state officials alleged Trump and other top officials 
made public comments indicating they saw the Section 301 tariffs as an 
alternative way to accomplish the levies the Supreme Court struck down. The 
challengers argued the record featured other evidence that the forced labor 
concerns were a pretext, such as the administration’s decision to cite China’s 
importation of beef from Brazil as one of its “case studies” but then exempt 
Brazilian beef from the tariffs.

Tariff Confusion

The Justice Department argued that it was reasonable for the trade office to 
present a single, comprehensive report explaining its justifications for the 
tariffs and pushed back on the notion that the move was rooted in “bad faith.”

Even as the administration returns nearly $170 billion plus interest in voided 
duties collected under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, or 
IEEPA, US importers continue to struggle with the quick-turn changes to rates 
and rules.

Rick Woldenberg, the CEO of Learning Resources, said the cost to companies— 
particularly if they need to finance the duties.

“We borrowed the money to pay IEEPA, and then we got the IEEPA back, gave it 
back to the bank,” he said in an interview ahead of the hearing. “As soon as we 
gave it back to the bank, we borrowed more money so we could pay the 122 
tariffs, which we haven’t gotten back, and then we went and got more money to 
pay the 301 tariffs. This is all on top of normal tariffs and duties that we 
already pay.”

CBP hasn’t published data on how much revenue has been collected from the 
forced labor tariffs at the center of Wednesday’s court hearing, but Woldenberg 
said that because they hit just before the busy holiday season, it would be 
substantial.

“As of a few weeks ago, it was $700,000. But we bring in a lot of product at 
this time of year, so it it goes up quickly,” he said. “Believe me, it’s going 
to add up.”
