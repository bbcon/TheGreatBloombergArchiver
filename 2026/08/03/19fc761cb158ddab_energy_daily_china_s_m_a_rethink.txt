Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Annie Lee explores why Zijin Gold’s investment in a Canadian miner 
could herald a change in China’s approach to M&A.

For the best part of two decades, China’s metals heavyweights have been among 
the most acquisitive players in the mining world, making billions of dollars of 
investments from the Democratic Republic of Congo to Australia.

That splurge may be nearing an end, and Zijin Gold International Co.’s 
abandonment 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-29/allied-gold-says-china-s-zijin-gold-terminates-takeover?cmpid=BBBXT080326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7280>
 of a $3.9 billion takeover of Canada’s Allied Gold Corp. is the clearest 
indication yet that Chinese officials are changing tack.

Questions had clouded Zijin’s latest plan for some time, as troubling 
headlines increased in countries like Mali, where mining regions havebecome 
flashpoints 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-22/militants-target-sahel-s-gold-networks-beyond-mine-gates?cmpid=BBBXT080326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7280>
 for clashes between armed groups. An acquisition deadline waspushed back 
<bbg://news/stories/TFTCQYKIUPTR> in May.

At the same time, authorities in Beijing were signaling that they wanted 
greater efforts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-03/china-picks-state-firm-to-help-coordinate-mining-deals-abroad?cmpid=BBBXT080326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7280>
 to counter risk, especially as geopolitical and other tensions increase.

Ultimately, while there was no official rejection, Chinese regulators failed 
to give their blessing on time. Zijin Gold, a subsidiary of giant Zijin Mining 
Co., switched course and announced a $295 million stake purchase instead.

Beijing’s decision — as well as its apparent solution — are signals worth 
noting.


On one hand, they confirm what officials had known for some time. China is 
finally, and officially, pulling back from its aggressive acquisition phase, a 
lengthy spending spree that helped the world’s second-largest economy gain a 
strong grip on the global minerals supply chain.

On the other hand, they show that lower risk appetite doesn’t mean zero 
investment. While Beijing doesn’t wish to see Chinese miners splurge on 
hazardous deals, it still wants to encourage strategic bets.

Stakes like the roughly 9% of Allied to be acquired by Zijin — leaving the 
door open to future negotiations — could become more frequent.

This would dovetail with other efforts led by the National Development and 
Reform Commission, which oversees Chinese economic planning and hasintroduced 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-13/china-expands-strategic-mineral-toolkit-with-new-investment-firm?cmpid=BBBXT080326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7280>
 a state-owned entity called Guangyan International Investment Co. to improve 
oversight and risk management.

The Allied Gold deal had its peculiarities, including difficulties in Mali, 
where jihadist groups, rebel factions, pro-government militias and others have 
clashed. Resource nationalism, where producer nations demand a larger share of 
revenue, has also grown.

Chinese miners have encountered such challenges before. But from now on, they 
may prepare for them differently in future.

—Annie Lee, Bloomberg News
