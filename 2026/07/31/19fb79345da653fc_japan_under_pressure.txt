The Bank of Japan left its benchmark rate unchanged 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-31/boj-holds-rate-steady-with-upward-revision-to-economic-outlook?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7218>
 at 1% on Friday, with its messaging anything but neutral. The central bank 
upgraded its view of the economy, warned inflation could overshoot its 2% 
target and signaled confidence that Japan remains on track for further policy 
normalization after last month’s rate hike to the highest level since 1995. 
Markets are now treating virtually every BOJ meeting as a live one for another 
increase.

The decision came just hours after authorities appeared to step into currency 
markets 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/yen-surges-after-japan-intervened-in-market-again-ahead-of-boj?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7218>
 to prop up the yen, underscoring the growing pressure from a weak currency 
that’s feeding imported inflation. One board member even dissented in favor of 
another immediate hike, while traders are increasingly betting the next move 
could come as soon as October.

Zoom out, and Japan suddenly looks a lot more complicated than it did a few 
months ago. The BOJ is inching toward higher rates, policymakers are battling 
to steady the yen, and Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi is discovering that a 
landslide election win doesn’t guarantee an easy ride. Ourweekend Big Take 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-30/japan-pm-takaichi-faces-biggest-test-yet-as-yen-sinks-approval-ratings-drop?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260731&utm_content=7218>
 examines why her once-dominant political position is beginning to look 
surprisingly fragile.
