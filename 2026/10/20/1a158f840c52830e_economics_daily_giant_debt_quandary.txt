A Giant Debt Quandary

With two weeks to go in the midterm US congressional campaigns 
<https://about.bgov.com/>, the budget deficit has hardly been a hot-button 
issue. At the same time, this election will usher in a cohort of lawmakers who 
on their watch will be facing a major question affecting the American fiscal 
path.

The incoming class of US senators, who serve six-year terms, will be in place 
in 2032 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/social-security-benefits-face-22-cut-with-senate-candidates-dodging-crisis?cmpid=BBD102026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261020&utm_content=9992>
 when Social Security — the giant government program that currently pays out 
retirement benefits to more than63 million Americans 
<https://www.ssa.gov/oact/TR/2026/lr5c4.html> — will in one sense run out of 
money. That’s when the program estimates it will have used up the entirety of 
its reserves.

If no legislative action is taken, Social Security will have to cut its 
payments by an estimated 22%. Which is why observers anticipate the lawmaking 
gears to kick in. “It is hard to believe,” as George Mason University’s 
Veronique de Rugy and Jason Fichtner put it this summer, that Congress would be 
content to see a vast swath of older Americans lose more thana fifth of their 
payments 
<https://www.mercatus.org/research/research-papers/social-securitys-fiscal-gap-and-risk-bond-market-strain>
.

One option would see Congress simply opt to pay for the shortfall via the 
regular federal budget. For well over a decade now, the Treasury has 
effectively beenissuing debt to the public 
<https://bipartisanpolicy.org/article/yes-the-social-security-deficit-adds-to-the-federal-deficit/>
 to cover the gap, since the payroll tax revenue that goes to pay for benefits 
stopped being enough to do the job. What would be different is that the extra 
debt issuance would be made explicit.

Whatever happens, the debate over how to sort things out will be public and 
prominent. Market participants will be paying close attention. A solution that 
involves turning Social Security into, in part, a new deficit-financed welfare 
program — at a time when thegovernment debt burden 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-02-20/new-us-debt-projections-put-spotlight-on-the-doom-loop-scenario?cmpid=BBD102026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261020&utm_content=9992>
 will, if projections hold, be at a record high — would risk a major bondholder 
revolt.

That’s why the head of the Congressional Budget Office, a nonpartisan body 
charged with making economic and fiscal projections, suggested recently this 
event could prove pivotal.

“It’s hard to act 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-08/cbo-chief-warns-growth-solution-for-us-debt-is-unlikely-to-work?cmpid=BBD102026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261020&utm_content=9992>
 until, whatever the problem is, is here,” CBO Director Phillip Swagel said. 
But now things are coming into view. “Members of the Senate who are elected in 
next month’s election, that exhaustion date will be within their term,” he 
said, referring to Social Security’s reserves. “Within the political horizon.”

What about until then? “It could be that markets bring forward the need to do 
something,” Swagel observed. In other words, a selloff so damaging it sends 
borrowing costs to recession-inducing levels, a-la the 2010s European crisis.

But if not, then Social Security could prove the prime motivating force for a 
fiscal fix, just a few years from now. The clock is ticking.

Need-to-Know Research

At least a third of Federal Reserve policymakers are open to shrinking the 
number of policy meetings each year to fewer than eight, Bloombergreported 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-09/fed-officials-signal-openness-to-warsh-proposal-for-fewer-policy-meetings?cmpid=BBD102026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261020&utm_content=9992>
 last month. Deutsche Bank economists now say that indeed, “there are good 
reasons to revisit” a calendar that was set all the way back in 1981.

One drawback of the current schedule is policy meetings aren’t calibrated with 
key data releases, so this “often leaves the Fed with relatively stale” 
figures, economists led by Matthew Luzzetti wrote in a note Friday. They 
suggested data availability being a priority in setting the schedule, along 
with keeping roughly equal time gaps between gatherings. Avoid conflicts with 
other major central banks and holidays or times of low liquidity in markets, 
they added.
Construction on the Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve building in Washington, 
DC on Jan. 12.Photographer: Pete Kiehart/Bloomberg
Chairman Kevin Warsh has floated six policy-setting meetings 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-02/warsh-raises-rethink-on-fed-s-policy-meeting-frequency-timing?cmpid=BBD102026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261020&utm_content=9992>
. The Deutsche Bank team suggested this for 2027: Feb. 17, April 21, June 16, 
Aug. 18, Oct. 20 and Dec. 15. This would provide for the same set of new 
information each time, with two monthly reports each for employment, consumer 
prices and the Fed’s preferred PCE price gauge. The eight-to-nine week 
intervals between sit-downs would be only “somewhat longer” than the 
six-to-seven setup now, they noted.
