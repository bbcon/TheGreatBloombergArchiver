**US-Iran Conflict: Shifting Calculus**
The clearest immediate market signal from the US midterms was in energy. Brent crude fell more than 8% on Wednesday — its largest single-session decline in months — as traders reassessed the probability of further US-Iran military escalation. The conflict, now in its ninth month, has pushed oil prices more than 50% higher year-to-date, depleted US interceptor missile stockpiles, and contributed materially to the inflation that the election results suggest voters held against the administration. Vice President Vance explicitly cited the war as a primary reason for Republican losses, stating it "cost his party the Senate for at least six years." Whether the political pressure translates into a genuine diplomatic pivot remains uncertain — Iran retains leverage through Strait of Hormuz disruptions and Houthi operations against Saudi Arabia, and Trump retains executive authority — but the drumbeat of escalation has measurably softened.

**COP31 and Energy Transition**
COP31 convenes next week against an unusual backdrop: the energy shock from the Iran conflict is simultaneously straining fossil-fuel-dependent economies and accelerating their solar and electrification transitions, particularly in emerging markets. Bangladesh, where solar panel sales have risen 50% year-on-year, exemplifies a pattern visible across South and Southeast Asia. Australia's Chris Bowen, as COP31's president of negotiations, is seeking a binding commitment on the 35% electrification target — China's October endorsement of the goal represents meaningful momentum, though translating voluntary intent into enforceable commitment remains the structural challenge. Ireland's proposal to delay EU methane rules for energy importers illustrates the ongoing tension between climate ambition and near-term energy security.
