Deep Dive: The Tricky Task of Navigating Hormuz

The tanker captain summoned his years of experience when he saw the blast in 
front of him. He veered his300-meter-long ship 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/getting-oil-and-gas-through-hormuz-strait-is-dangerous-job-for-ship-captains?cmpid=100226_morningemea&utm_campaign=morningemea&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261002&utm_content=9632>
 around the vessel ahead and could see a fire had started. He called the US 
Navy to tell them of the attack.

 * The maneuver in the Strait of Hormuz with drones and missiles overhead is 
the sort of quick thinking that’s proving vital to getting oil supplies to the 
global market as Washington and Tehran fail to reach agreement on reopening the 
waterway to regular traffic.
 * The account of the incident, told to Bloomberg by a captain in his early 
40s who declined to be identified while discussing commercially sensitive 
journeys, shows how and why those flows from the Middle East are rising largely 
under the watch of the US military.
 * His vessel is among the ships engaged in shuttle runs through the strait, 
ferrying barrels often at night and with strict instructions about what 
equipment can be used — no lights, no phones and with only one radar.
 * They were often just a mile from the coast of Oman, though the depth of the 
sea there is about 100 meters, more than enough for even the biggest 
supertankers to sail through. The captain said he and his crew got their 
bearings by using the coast and lighthouses as a reference.
