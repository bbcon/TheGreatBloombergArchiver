Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Asian Energy Team Leader Stephen Stapczynski looks at how gas buyers are 
trading reliance on one region for another.

The war in the Middle East is accelerating a trend that’s been going on for 
several years now: The world is becoming increasingly dependent on US natural 
gas.

The shale revolution that transformed the country into the world’s biggest oil 
producer has also underpinned its emergence as the top liquefied natural gas 
exporter. This year, after about a fifth of global supply of the super-chilled 
fuelbecame trapped 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-02/world-s-top-lng-buyer-doesn-t-see-qatar-supply-coming-back-soon?cmpid=BBBXT100526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9699>
 behind the Strait of Hormuz, US dominance has become more cemented.

Around a third of the world’s LNG exports originated from the US last month. 
That’s up from 25% last year and 20% in 2022. With shipments slated to double 
by the end of the decade, this trend is set to continue.

There are clear benefits to the shift. It’s helped to blunt the impact of the 
sudden loss of Qatari supply earlier this year. Global LNG exports were down 
just 3% in September from a year earlier.

Prices in Europe 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-29/war-and-lng-shift-breaks-economics-of-european-gas-storage?cmpid=BBBXT100526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9699>
 and Asia have still surged to multiyear highs, but the market has so far 
avoided the kind of blowout seen in 2022 following Russia’s full-scale invasion 
of Ukraine.
A tanker at the Freeport LNG facility in Texas,.Photographer: Mark 
Felix/Bloomberg
The US is also one of the few places capable of adding substantial new supply, 
making it increasingly attractive to Asian buyers seekinglong-term contracts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-09-16/lng-buyers-turn-to-us-as-hormuz-crisis-forces-supply-rethink?cmpid=BBBXT100526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9699>
 to reduce their reliance on the Middle East.

But having so much of the world’s LNG supply come from one country creates new 
vulnerabilities. A severe hurricane season on the US Gulf Coast could knock out 
major export plants and trigger supply shocks from London to Tokyo.

There’s also a political risk. US dominance creates opportunities for 
Washington — depending on who occupies the White House — to exploit its 
leverage.

Take Alaska LNG. President Donald Trump has threatened South Korea 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-02/trump-threatens-south-korea-over-alaska-lng-project-dispute?cmpid=BBBXT100526_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261005&utm_content=9699>
 with retaliation if it doesn’t follow through on investing in the long-stalled 
project, after Seoul said its participation would depend on it proving 
economically viable.

LNG buyers may move from being too dependent on the Middle East to becoming 
overly reliant on the US.

—Stephen Stapczynski, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Batteries are helping solar developers capture higher prices from increasingly 
volatile power markets, leaving them less reliant on hedging, according to 
BloombergNEF. Madrid-listed Solaria hedges 65% of production through long-term 
power purchase agreements, leaving 35% exposed to merchant prices. The company 
said it has no intention of reducing that share, arguing that batteries and its 
in-house trading increase the value captured.
