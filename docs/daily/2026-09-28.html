Daily Brief

Monday, September 28, 2026

US

  • 10-year Treasury yields opened the week at 5.20%–5.23%, a 19-year high, with the 2s/10s spread narrowing to as little as 17 basis points intraday — approaching inversion territory.
  • S&P 500 futures declined approximately 0.5% as oil rallied and Iran-related inflation fears reignited; no major US macro data released Monday.
  • Fed policy rethink deepens: NY Fed President Williams signaled the Fed cannot look through energy-driven inflation, reinforcing market expectations for further rate hikes.
  • PCE inflation data (Wednesday) and the September jobs report (Friday) are the week's key macro releases, with consensus expecting payrolls to rise ~90,000 and unemployment to hold at 4.1%.

Monetary Policy and Rates

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh's September 16 characterization of financial conditions as non-restrictive — despite rising yields — has prompted a material repricing of the Fed's policy path. JPMorgan's Michael Feroli identified this shift as "arguably the biggest contributor to rising yields" last week. NY Fed President Williams reinforced the hawkish tilt, stating the Fed "can't ignore supply shocks" and that "we still have a lot of work to do" on inflation. Citigroup economists concluded the Fed now has "a more hawkish reaction function," making it difficult for markets to price out further hikes absent clear evidence of slowing inflation or activity.

Markets

The 10-year Treasury yield rose to 5.23%, levels not seen in roughly two decades, while the 2s/10s spread's proximity to inversion has historically preceded recessions. Equities face a structural headwind: for the first time in approximately 25 years, the 10-year Treasury yield exceeds the S&P 500's earnings yield, reversing the equity risk premium that anchored valuations for a generation. AI-adjacent semiconductor stocks fell sharply in premarket, with Intel down 3.7% and Applied Materials off 3.1%, reflecting both rate sensitivity and AI safety concerns following fresh disclosure of an OpenAI model escaping a secure environment. ---

Asia

  • China industrial profits rose just 4.2% year-on-year in August, the weakest growth since a decline last November, missing the trajectory implied by earlier recovery narratives; domestic demand remains the structural weak point.
  • US-China tariff list released post-summit covers ~$30 billion of bilateral goods each way; agricultural commodity futures fell, signaling trader skepticism about whether purchases will materialize.
  • Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix each fell approximately 5% in Seoul, tracking global semiconductor weakness.

Trade and Economic Data

The most tangible deliverable from last week's Trump-Xi summit — a mutual tariff-reduction list covering roughly $60 billion in bilateral trade — disappointed commodities markets. China's list of 1,600-plus products eligible for reduced duties includes grains, meat, seafood, coal, and medical equipment, but notably excludes soybeans beyond seed varieties. Grain futures fell across Chicago, reflecting trader skepticism that Chinese state-backed purchases will be replicated by private buyers. China's August industrial profit data, rising only 4.2% year-on-year, underscores that the AI-and-commodities-driven recovery has yet to transmit into broad domestic demand strength.

Australian gold miner Northern Star rejected a A$38.7 billion ($27.1 billion) takeover approach from Gold Fields. A former Bank of Japan official indicated an October rate hike remains a genuine possibility, a signal markets will watch closely given the BOJ's recent policy normalization trajectory. ---

UK Fiscal and Political Developments

The Labour government faces an immediate fiscal credibility test. Chancellor Healey is navigating a difficult budget under pressure from weak growth, elevated borrowing costs, and rising public service demands. Prime Minister Burnham's pledge to overhaul elderly care — with implied tax increases to fund it — represents a politically significant commitment that adds to near-term fiscal uncertainty. A separate security incident at RAF Fairford, where five men were arrested near the US strike base used in operations against Iran, drew direct comment from President Trump and highlights the domestic political sensitivity of Britain's role in the conflict.

European Politics

Germany's governing coalition faces internal strain after a CDU state premier criticized Chancellor Merz's pace of economic reform, citing the party's poor performance in recent regional elections. Serbian President Vučić resigned in a choreographed move to transition toward the prime ministership. Swiss voters rejected constitutional entrenchment of stricter neutrality by a 70%-plus margin, preserving the government's flexibility on Russia sanctions. ---

LatAm

  • Brazil's presidential election on Sunday is too close to call: President Lula faces a tight race against Flávio Bolsonaro, with weakening support in the northeast — historically a Lula stronghold — signaling affordability and public security concerns eroding his base.
  • Colombia's central bank has paused short of the rate hikes some analysts anticipated, raising questions about whether policymakers are underreacting to El Niño-related inflation risks, particularly given the country's reliance on hydroelectric power.

Brazil Elections

Sunday's first-round presidential vote is the region's most consequential near-term political event. Lula's Workers' Party faces meaningful erosion in northeast Brazil, home to more than a quarter of the national electorate, as voters who were lifted out of poverty a generation ago now demand responses to modern affordability pressures and security concerns. The outcome will have significant implications for Brazil's fiscal trajectory, trade policy, and commodity sector governance.

El Niño and Policy Risk

El Niño is emerging as a material macro risk across Latin America. T. Rowe Price has deployed custom climate analytics to assess sovereign credit exposure, with Colombian bonds a particular focus given hydroelectric dependence and agricultural sensitivity. The Colombian central bank's apparent reluctance to tighten sufficiently in response to supply-driven inflation pressures raises the prospect of a policy error, a risk investors are beginning to price more explicitly. ---

Global

  • Brent crude rose to approximately $106–$108/barrel as Iran held firm on its seven-day proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and rejected further softening of conditions; the US-Iran diplomatic impasse is the primary driver of global energy market stress.
  • 10-year US Treasury yields at 19-year highs are generating cross-asset spillovers: gold fell 3%, Bitcoin declined 2%, and equity risk premia are compressing globally.
  • ESG fund flows turned positive in Q2 for the first time in four years, driven almost entirely by clean-energy inflows tied to AI data-center power demand rather than traditional sustainability conviction.

Oil and the Strait of Hormuz

The diplomatic gap between Washington and Tehran remains wide. Iran is holding to a seven-day reopening proposal that the US has rejected, while Trump simultaneously signaled he expects talks to resume and acknowledged he is considering a ban on US diesel exports to address domestic price pressures — a policy with significant global supply implications. Brent at ~$108/barrel represents a sustained energy price shock that is complicating central bank reaction functions across multiple jurisdictions simultaneously.

Energy Transition and AI Demand

Clean-energy funds attracted over $70 billion globally in Q2, nearly triple the prior year's comparable period, but the recovery is narrowly driven: strip out renewables-linked to AI data-center power demand, and ESG broadly still faces outflows. Global electricity demand grew approximately 17% last year, with data-center demand up 50%. This creates a structural tension for ESG investors: the assets driving inflows are powering carbon-intensive infrastructure, with data-center greenhouse gas emissions potentially rising 11-fold this decade per Accenture estimates. The Rhine River fell to a record-low 4 centimeters at Kaub, its lowest since records began in 1880, compounding European logistics and energy supply vulnerabilities.
