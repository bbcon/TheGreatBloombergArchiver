Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Asia energy team leader Stephen Stapczynski reports from Bangladesh on how 
soaring LNG costs are hitting the developing nation’s economy.

To understand how disruptions to liquefied natural gas shipments through the 
Strait of Hormuz are reverberating across theemerging world 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-13/a-7-billion-gas-bill-sees-developing-asian-nations-sour-on-lng?cmpid=BBBXT110226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10877>
, look no further than Bangladesh.

Once-bustling textile and ceramics plants in the South Asian nation are 
running well below normal capacity as gas shortages lead tosurging energy bills 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-12/gas-supply-woes-are-delaying-bangladesh-s-industrial-dreams?cmpid=BBBXT110226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10877>
. Some factories were forced to halt production altogether in August and 
September, when an outage at an LNG import terminal compounded the crunch.

These types of industrial facilities in Bangladesh have been operating at a 
loss for months. Textile manufacturing, in particular, is energy-intensive, 
with gas-fired boilers producing steam that’s used in dyeing and finishing the 
fabrics.

Bangladesh relied on Qatar for about 60% of its LNG imports last year. That 
dependence had been a boon following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in 2022, 
since long-term agreements helped shield it from the surge in spot prices.
Clothing displayed at a Ralph Lauren store in New York. Bangladeshi textile 
manufacturers are suppliers to the well-known American fashion brand.
Photographer: Bing Guan/Bloomberg
This time around, the arrangement has been a crippling vulnerability for the 
world’s second-largest garment exporter. Qatar has been unable to deliver gas, 
forcing Bangladesh into thepricey spot market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/bangladesh-pays-most-for-gas-since-2022-to-ease-power-blackouts?cmpid=BBBXT110226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10877>
 to avoid worsening blackouts.

A single shipment of the super-chilled fuel now costs the country more than 
$100 million, according to Bloomberg calculations, about four times as much as 
a contracted cargo would have. That’s resulted in about $2 billion in 
additional costs so far.

Bangladeshi manufacturers supplying global brands like H&M and Ralph Lauren 
are dealing with spiraling energy costs and the possibility of more power cuts. 
Factory owners are worried that orders could be shifted to competitors in China 
or India, they told me on a visit to Dhaka last week.

There’s little relief in sight. While Bangladesh has scaled back purchase 
tenders, opting forbilateral deals 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-30/bangladesh-in-talks-to-buy-more-american-gas-says-us-ambassador?cmpid=BBBXT110226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10877>
 instead, those shipments are still linked to spot prices. The prospect of a 
revival in LNG flows through Hormuz was dealt another blow last week after a 
Qatari tanker was attacked despitePakistan’s arrangements 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-01/pakistan-lng-cargo-attacked-near-hormuz-had-iranian-approval?cmpid=BBBXT110226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10877>
 with Iran for safe passage.

As gas prices continue to climb in Europe and Asia, the pain won’t be confined 
to Bangladesh. The longer the crisis drags on, the more industries across the 
emerging world risk confronting the same pressures now playing out on the 
factory floors of Dhaka.

—Stephen Stapczynski, Bloomberg News

Chart of the day

Awash in cash 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-31/big-oil-is-grappling-with-how-to-spend-its-windfall-profits?cmpid=BBBXT110226_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10877>
 after their third-highest quarterly earnings on record, the world’s largest 
oil companies are uncharacteristically wary about how to spend it. ExxonMobil 
Holdings Corp. and Chevron Corp. are stockpiling the cash, BP Plc is paying 
down debt and other liabilities, and Shell Plc and TotalEnergies SE promise 
they’ll be cautious about future investments. It all points to an industry that 
has suffered the ill effects of overspending during previous booms and a 
feeling that current high prices may not last much longer.
