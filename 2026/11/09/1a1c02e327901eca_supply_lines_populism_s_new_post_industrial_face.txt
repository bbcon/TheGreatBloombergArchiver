There’s a new breed of economic populists in Washington after last week’s 
midterm elections and the most striking thing about them is what they’re not 
focused on.

All of a sudden American populism isn’t about factory jobs. Or tariffs. Or 
anything to do with trade even. In fact, last week may well have heralded the 
arrival of an anti-protectionist populism. Which is head-spinning for anyone 
who has tracked the debate around economic policy over the past decade.

I dove into how the 2026 elections featured both a rebuke of the leading US 
economic populist (President Donald Trump) and a rebirth of American populism in
an essay 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-06/us-midterm-results-show-american-populism-evolving-beyond-trump?cmpid=BBD110926_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11145>
 that ran over the weekend.

Lots of advocates of tariffs including Vice President JD Vance have spent the 
days since the election arguing that the real reason voters backed a blue wave 
was theIran war 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/trump-says-strikes-unlikely-even-as-rubio-slams-iran-s-demands?cmpid=BBD110926_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11145>
 and the resulting spike in gas prices. Also among those was Oren Cass of 
American Compass who last week joined my boss Stephanie Flanders in the final 
episode of her podcastTrumponomics 
<https://podcasts.apple.com/gb/podcast/the-midterms-changed-washington-will-they-change/id1038108799?i=1000800021732>
 before it reverts to its original name Stephanomics.

There’s no doubt the Iran war and gas prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-08/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-nov-9?cmpid=BBD110926_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11145>
 contributed to Republican losses. But the shift in the electorate against 
Trump’s tariffs and Democrats’ exploitation of that was also strikingly clear 
in this year’s campaign.

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-06/trump-congress-set-to-clash-on-politically-toxic-tariff-blitz?cmpid=BBD110926_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11145>

The debate over the economic benefits and consequences of slapping taxes on 
imports is likely to continue for many years to come. That’s the nature of 
theological debates. But the political argument is over for now. Tariffs 
certainly weren’t a vote getter last week.

Which is why the new populists landing on the scene are focused on not just 
the broad area of affordability but very specific costly components of the 
middle class grind like housing and health care. And why the new bogey man 
isn’t globalization or trade but AI and the tech titans pushing it and their 
data centers on the world.

There is political common sense at work when the industrial economy takes a 
backseat in election campaigns. Just 8% of the US workforce is in 
manufacturing. The American working class in 2026 is a complicated quilt. More 
people work in healthcare (18.6 million) and retail (15.5 million) than in 
factories.

The identifiable payoffs of tariffs or industrial policy also tend to take 
time and can be relatively modest. Which is something Joe Biden learned as he 
touted his industrial policies and resulting investments as wins on the 
campaign trail while voters focused on inflation.

Ahead of the midterms vote, Trump traveled to Georgia to announce what was 
billed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-24/volkswagen-plans-new-audi-plant-in-georgia-handing-trump-a-win?cmpid=BBD110926_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11145>
 as a major win for his trade policy in the form of a new $4 billion investment 
by Audi in a manufacturing plant. When production starts in the early 2030s it 
will employ 4,500 people. Or less than 0.1% of the 5 million people nowemployed 
in Georgia <https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/GANA>. Democrats won not just 
the Senate seat in Georgia but all the statewide races as well last week.

The truth is the big threat of economic change hanging over the US economy if 
you live and work in it is not globalization these days. It’s all the things 
associated with AI and its billionaires.

AI Fears

Which means everything from the wave of automation and job losses feared by 
many to thecapital-hungry buildout 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-08/wall-street-races-for-megadeals-as-countdown-begins-on-trump-era?cmpid=BBD110926_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11145>
 of data centers testing neighbors and the invasion of privacy many communities 
are revolting against in AI-enabled surveillance cameras. It’s a remarkably 
wide target affecting a huge portion of the voting population in one way or 
another.

So whichever side of US politics you sit on, if you are planning a 
presidential campaign after last week’s Democratic wins, you better be drafting 
policies to address the fears linked to AI as well as bringing down the cost of 
health insurance, housing or child care.

Reindustrialization may be part of your economic agenda. So too may be 
competing with China 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/china-car-exports-to-slow-from-supercharged-growth-group-says?cmpid=BBD110926_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261109&utm_content=11145>
. Yet they are unlikely to be your priorities.

Things could change by 2028, of course. The calculus in the past has been 
different in presidential elections. Trump’s 2016 protectionist campaign was 
built on chasing the electoral college votes of a few industrial swing states.

But that was before tariffs became associated with high prices in voters’ 
minds. Or Texas turned blue.

Trump’s tariff-based industrial populism suffered a major defeat last week. 
Another brand of populism was born, and its priorities already look very 
different.
