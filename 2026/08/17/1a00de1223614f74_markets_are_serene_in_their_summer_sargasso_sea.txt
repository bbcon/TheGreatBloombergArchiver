Wide Sargasso Markets

The Sargasso Sea <https://bloom.bg/4xKad6P>, an elliptical area of the 
Atlantic around Bermuda, is a model for global markets in the depths of this 
hot summer. An area of relative calm caused by the space between four major 
Atlantic currents, it’shome for Sargassum seaweed <https://bloom.bg/4idrTmQ> 
and mating ground for plentiful fish. It continues to be a sea without a 
shoreline as waves are whipped up all around.

So it is with financial markets. It’s hard to believe from the intensity and 
anger of political debates, within the US and beyond, but they’ve left a space 
during the summer doldrums for remarkable stability. Exhibit A) is Bloomberg’s
financial conditions index, <https://bloom.bg/4qhA6bH> which gauges whether it 
is relatively easy or hard to raise finance from a range of market metrics. 
Initiated in 1990, it has never shown conditions easier than they were at the 
close on Friday:

Among the currents reinforcing safe conditions for financing is dwindling 
stock market volatility. Rather than irrational exuberance, as excoriated by
Alan Greenspan <https://bloom.bg/4xB6fgM>, it looks like irrational equanimity 
<https://bloom.bg/4g35BD8>. The VIX index <https://bloom.bg/4g0IVTX>, gauging 
volatility by investors’ positions in the options market to guard against 
turbulence in the S&P 500, is at its most relaxed for 2026. It’s only closed 
lower than this on a handful of occasions since Donald Trump was reelected:

And this isn’t (just) about excessive confidence in the buildout of artificial 
intelligence. That is of huge importance, of course, and in the last few weeks 
both credit and equity investors have shown signs of having second thoughts. 
But several broad indexes designed to limit the impact of AI made all-time 
highs last week. This is true of Bloomberg’s measure of the “other 493” 
large-cap stocks, excluding the Magnificent Seven platform groups; of the 
equal-weighted version of the S&P 500 in which all stocks, including Nvidia 
Corp., have only a 0.2% weighting; and of the S&P 500 excluding tech:

So it’s evident that something beyond the direct impact of the AI investments 
is buoying equities. That would be corporate fundamentals in the form of 
earnings per share and revenues, which enjoyeda phenomenal quarter 
<https://bloom.bg/3TRTu39> and seem set fair for the rest of the year. 
Semiconductors and financials have raised earnings far faster than revenues as 
they expand margins, which may yet create its own problems, but strong earnings 
growth on the back of solid sales repeats across many sectors:

Earnings like this go a long way to calm nerves. Beyond that, can we attribute 
the calm to the summer doldrums? With earnings season winding down, and most of 
the key economic data for the month already published, the diary for this week 
has no potential market-moving events that we can see coming. The closest 
approach could be the publication of the minutes to the last Federal Open 
Market Committee meeting, due Wednesday. Nvidia’s results and the Jackson Hole 
central banking conference, both potential market movers, aren’t until next 
week. People are on vacation. It’s hot. No wonder nothing much happens in the 
dog days of August.

This is a fair argument, but it isn’t always thus. There’s a long history of 
August shocks, thanks to big news — Saddam Hussein’s invasion of Kuwait (1990), 
the coup against Gorbachev in the Soviet Union (1991), Russia’s debt default 
(1998), Standard & Poor’s downgrade of US sovereign debt (2011) or China’s 
botched devaluation (2015). Internally-generated market mayhem includedthe 
desperation of August 2007 <https://bloom.bg/4qiiuwi> as the subprime crisis 
took shape, and the 2024 meltdown of the Japanese carry trade.

This year, the spasm of nerves around the AI trade briefly had the potential 
to turn into such an event but has desisted. Japanese currency intervention 
hasn’t sparked another major carry trade unwind. And by being rather 
disappointing, data have calmed the notion that the Federal Reserve will be 
tightening straight away. The economy is strong enough for companies to make 
money; and the negative surprises suggest it’s not so strong that the Fed will 
need to hike rates, which counts as good news:

As for the obvious geopolitical risks, there is a belief — correct or 
otherwise — that the market has a handle on them, and that they are less than 
catastrophic. That applies to the situation in theStrait of Hormuz 
<https://bloom.bg/4qiQgln>, and also to trade policy. News that the US is 
levying a new wave of tariffs hasn’t stopped Bloomberg’s index of global trade 
policy uncertainty, built on natural language processing analysis of press 
coverage, dropping to one of its lowest levels of the Trump era:

Whether markets are right to be this confident is another question. Meanwhile, 
the greatest risk, also diminished slightly by the slowdown in the recent data, 
comes frombonds <https://bloom.bg/3RvjkWd>. The notion that Treasury investors 
might finally revolt against ever higher fiscal deficits and force yields far 
higher has been around for years. Real 10-year yields have threatened to break 
out but remain just below 2.5%. If they rise to take out the post-crisis high 
of 2.516%, set three years ago, that could shatter the calm:

But as it stands, Great Earnings + Goldilocks Economic Data + Absence of News 
= Continued Calm. Just likeeels must leave the Sargasso 
<https://bloom.bg/3SAxGIU>, this cannot go on indefinitely, but with any luck 
these vintage doldrums have another couple of weeks left in them.

Emerging Glitters

Beyond the US, emerging markets’ resilience — amid elevated geopolitical risks 
— also looks increasingly entrenched. The cohort hasn’t been immune to the 
disruptions of the past two years, but it’s shown an increasingly impressive 
ability to maneuver through them.Emerging markets ex-China 
<https://bloom.bg/4qnrGQc> equity funds attracted nearly $7 billion of net 
inflows in the first half, the strongest six-month period on record and a sharp 
reversal from 2025’s outflows, according to Morningstar:

A similar measure, from the Institute of International Finance, tracking 
non-resident flows into EM equity reinforces this resilience. Equity outflows 
in July narrowed sharply following a massive selloff in response to the Iran 
conflict. Net portfolio flows reached about $19 billion last month, reversing 
$18 billion outflows in the previous two months:

Despite all the adversity, the headline numbers show that EM is repaying its 
investors:

Is it reasonable to assume they can maintain this resilience? As Points of 
Return notedhere <https://bloom.bg/4xL29mw>, commodities like copper have 
provided a tailwind in Latin America, but that comes nowhere close 
totechnology. South Korea and Taiwan, home to big chipmakers, are being treated 
as pure AI plays. Their year-to-date total returns of at least 60% have lifted 
overall returns, and also disguised lackluster performance elsewhere in the 
emerging universe:

Morningstar’s Daniel Haydon urges investors not to chase momentum and remain 
mindful of valuations:

Those choosing index tracking strategies should be aware of market 
concentration, which in some respects resemble a thematic emerging markets tech 
fund. For example, TSMC (c.20%), Samsung Electronics (c.8%), and SK Hynix (6%) 
make up a significant proportion of Emerging Market ex-China indexes.

Another positive driver is the weaker dollar, which flatters emerging assets 
when priced in dollars while also making it cheaper to pay dollar-denominated 
debts:

This follows a prolonged period of serious and persistent EM currency 
weakness. Capital Economics’ William Jackson argues:

The Iran war started when most EMs had historically strong external balance 
sheets. FX reserves have remained high, while exports have been strong and 
demand has slowed after some post-pandemic overheating, narrowing current 
account deficits. As a result, we think most EM currencies should weather a 
period of dollar strength pretty well.

In the credit universe, although the extra spread that investors require for 
EM dollar-denominated debt compared to US corporate credit persists, the 
American advantage is rapidly narrowing. On a duration-adjusted basis, the gap 
is now the narrowest on record, a convergence that is notable because EM and US 
corporate credit have historically moved closely together. Bloomberg 
Intelligence’s Damian Sassower says this shows that investors demand far less 
compensation now for taking emerging-market credit risk:


For EM borrowers, this is significant. The penalty for being an EM credit is 
growing less pronounced, even as global investors distinguish between the risks 
of individual markets. Meanwhile, local currency debt (for those who could 
stomach exchange rate risk) outperformed the benchmark Bloomberg Global 
Aggregate Bond Index:

Gains driven by currency appreciation across the developing world give 
investors another reason to look beyond conventional developed market bonds as 
they navigate renewed price pressures, higher energy costs, and divergent 
central bank paths. This confluence of forces is unlikely to dissipate while 
inflationary pressures stemming from the Iran war constrain the Fed’s room to 
ease. Put differently, the summertime conditions that are making life easier 
for US investors also add to the case to investing in the emerging world. At 
least as long as they last.

—Richard Abbey
