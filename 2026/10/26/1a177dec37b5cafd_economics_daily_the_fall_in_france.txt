French

France just started the week on a brighter note after days of nervous tension 
both in debt markets and on the streets.

Bonds and stocks gained 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/french-markets-rebound-after-nation-avoids-moody-s-downgrade?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 on Monday after the nationdodged a downgrade 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-23/france-avoids-moody-s-downgrade-in-reprieve-after-week-of-crisis?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 late on Friday at Moody’s Ratings.Meanwhile 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-24/france-on-edge-as-new-protests-raise-fears-of-violence-looting?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 a heavy police presence ensured weekend demonstrations involving hundreds of 
thousands of protestorsdidn’t come with 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-25/france-dodges-repeat-of-looting-and-riots-with-tougher-security?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 the violence that had shocked the nation a week earlier.

It turns out that Australia’s second-biggest pension fund is now even buying up
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/french-bond-oats-australia-pension-australian-retirement-trust-yields-ecb?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 French government bonds on the view that markets have overpriced its fiscal 
troubles and the prospect of European Central Bank interest-rate hikes.

Long term however, the predicament 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/macron-bargaining-chips-paris-protests-violence-student-union-unef?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 of bloated public finances and political gridlock remains. The heightened 
scrutiny of France’s stretched fiscal situation that sent its bond yields to 
24-year highs last week is likely to heavily overshadow the ECB’s decision on 
Thursday.

A meeting that was meant to showcase officials’ analysis of current inflation 
risks against the backdrop ofwaning growth momentum 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/euro-zone-slowdown-central-bank-ecb-rates-hold-for-now?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 is now dominated by the troubles of the euro region’s second-biggest economy.

The problem for the policymakers is that if France’s bond rout turns into 
something far nastier, people will look to the institutionas a firefighter 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-02/ecb-s-nightmare-scenario-gets-closer-as-french-bond-yields-surge?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
.

“If there’s a meltdown in financial markets, then central banks have to 
intervene,” said former ECB Chief Economist Peter Praet. “But the question for 
any president is, how you do it, and how quickly?”

That matter is all the more pertinent with the prospect that President 
Christine Lagarde will leave the institution sooner than the scheduled end to 
her term next October. Some officials assume she could exit in January.

It’s against that backdrop that the race to succeed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-23/eurogroup-extends-deadline-for-ecb-board-applications-by-a-month?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 heris playing out 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-27/four-ecb-scenarios-sketch-a-succession-plan-for-post-lagarde-era?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 because the winning candidate might soon find themselves in the hot seat amid 
financial turbulence.
Pablo Hernandez de Cos, left, and Klaas Knot.Photographer: Lina Selg/Bloomberg
With France’s debt woes evoking memories of euro-zone turmoil, the choice of 
candidate between Dutchman Klaas Knot or Spaniard Pablo Hernandez de Cos looks
increasingly likely to hinge 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-25/french-bond-rout-puts-crisis-at-heart-of-ecb-succession-endgame?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 on which one can best take on thebond vigilantes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-23/de-cos-is-narrow-favorite-to-follow-lagarde-at-ecb-survey-shows?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
.

Whatever happens, a politically fraught role awaits the next ECB chief. 
Earlier this month 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-15/draghi-sees-high-bar-for-ecb-to-help-france-with-crisis-tool?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
, former president Mario Draghi said that public discussion in France centering 
on aid from the central bank has made the situation less comfortable.

“That if anything makes the interventions even more difficult,” he said. “The 
ECB, I assume, doesn’t want to be part of the political infighting in any 
specific country.”

Need-to-Know Research

Bank of England officials are sending their most hawkish signals 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/boe-officials-most-hawkish-since-inflation-was-10-model-shows?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
 in over three years, laying the ground for a rate hike as early as next month.

That’s according to BOESPEAK <bbg://screens/BECO%20MODELS%20BOESPEAK>, a 
natural language processing model built by Bloomberg Economics that tracks 
communications from Monetary Policy Committee members. The index has jumped to 
its highest level since early 2023, when the BOE was raising rates to battle 
10% inflation, after a recent slew of appearances by policymakers.

“The rise in the index is striking and has coincided with the ramp up in 
energy costs in recent months,” saidDan Hanson 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASReugI9vlI/dan-hanson?cmpid=BBD102626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10577>
, chief UK economist at Bloomberg Economics, who expects hikes in November and 
February. “Of course it’s possible the MPC is just talking tough. But we think 
the balance of risks around the growth and inflation outlook has shifted by 
enough to convince the majority of the committee that higher rates are needed.”
