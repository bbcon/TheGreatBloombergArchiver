Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporters David Wethe and Kevin Crowley look at the upswing in oil production 
in Alaska.

There’s an oil renaissance afoot in Alaska, giving the US a new source of 
growth as shale’s go-go era has ebbed.

After years of slumping production, the state is forecast 
<https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=68444&utm_medium=email> to add 
23,000 barrels a day this year and about twice that amount in 2027, according 
to the US Energy Information Administration.

The growth is fueled by two projects ramping up on Alaska’s North Slope: 
ConocoPhillips’ Nuna development and thefirst phase of Pikka 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-17/santos-repsol-begin-commercial-oil-flows-in-alaska-arctic?cmpid=BBBXT111826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11563>
, jointly owned by Santos Ltd. and Repsol SA. And that’s all before the biggest 
Alaska crude project in the works — ConocoPhillips’ colossalWillow development 
<https://www.conocophillips.com/operations/alaska/willow-project/> — comes 
online in 2029.

Alaska was once the US’s biggest oil-producing state, peaking in 1988 at about 
2 million barrels a day. It’s been mostly declining ever since as its fields 
age, especially the once-prolific Prudhoe Bay.

President Donald Trump has sought to reverse that trend, making Alaska a 
cornerstone of his energy agenda by pushing for major oil and gas development. 
The groundwork for the current production gains, however, began largely before 
his second term.

The Willow project was approved under Former President Joe Biden and is 
supported by Mary Peltola, the Democratwho just prevailed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-18/democrat-peltola-wins-alaska-senate-race-cnn-projects?cmpid=BBBXT111826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11563>
 in the final outstanding US Senate race of the 2026 midterm elections.

Despite the renewed growth in crude output, fully restoring Alaska to its 
former glory remains unlikely. Drilling there has limited appeal. It’s 
expensive and slow, thanks to long winters and the harsh terrain and remoteness 
of the North Slope.

And there are environmental considerations. Even though Trump has touted the 
potential of the oil lying within the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge, major 
oil companies have so farsteered clear 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-05/trump-s-alaska-oil-and-gas-lease-auction-draws-just-two-bidders?cmpid=BBBXT111826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11563>
 of lease sales amid concerns that a future Democratic administration may 
reimpose a ban on energy development on that land.

But for now, at least, the rebound in Alaska has legs. Even though its supply 
is dwarfed by output from the Permian Basin of Texas and New Mexico, the shale 
fields in the Lower 48 states are maturing.

The year’s historic oil price spike has helped shale drillers to squeeze more 
barrels out of their fields, putting the US on pace to increase production by 
378,000 barrels a day by the end of 2026. But that’s a fraction of the kind of 
growth seen a decade ago, and the expansion may vanish if prices continue to 
decline.

In such a scenario, Alaska once again becomes a critical element of American 
energy supply.

—David Wethe and Kevin Crowley, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Battery-grade lithium prices in mainland China fell further in October amid 
continued speculation that the market was looser than anticipated. The declines 
followed a methodology revision by the Shanghai Metals Market that more than 
doubled reported inventories at the start of September. Prices hit a 2026 low, 
but could still recover if the market adjusts to fundamentals. BloombergNEF 
expects lithium demand to outpace supply in 2026.
