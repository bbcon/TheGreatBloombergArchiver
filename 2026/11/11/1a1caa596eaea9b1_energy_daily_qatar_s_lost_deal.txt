Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Asia Energy Team Leader Stephen Stapczynski looks at the lasting implications 
of the Middle East war for gas giant Qatar.

It’s taken more than eight months of war, but Qatar has finally lost a 
long-term LNG contract due to disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz.

Poland’s Orlen SA is exiting a 1 million-ton-a-year 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/poland-s-orlen-terminates-lng-supply-deal-with-qatar-amid-war?cmpid=BBBXT111126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11275>
 commitment running through 2034. The market will be watching closely to see 
whether others follow.

It’s a defining moment for QatarEnergy, which has long been able to demand 
rigid contract terms and premium prices, in part because of its reputation as 
one of the most reliable suppliers of liquefied natural gas.

That image was shattered this year when the US and Israel attacked Iran, 
setting off a regional conflict that’s turned the world’s most vital energy 
conduit into a battlefield.

Qatar has extended force majeure 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/qatar-extends-lng-force-majeure-as-hormuz-disruptions-drag-on?cmpid=BBBXT111126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11275>
 on shipments every month since March, aprolonged disruption 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-29/qatar-agrees-to-buy-lng-from-woodside-in-second-long-term-deal?cmpid=BBBXT111126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11275>
 that can eventually give buyers the right to terminate contracts. Some may 
well be studying their agreements — and possible alternatives — according to 
people involved in the trade.

QatarEnergy didn’t immediately respond to a request for comment.
An LNG storage tank at the Gazoport terminal in Swinoujscie, Poland.
Photographer: Bartek Sadowski/Bloomberg
Even before the war, Poland had been increasing its exposure to US gas. 
American contracts typically give buyers greater flexibility to resell cargoes 
and are linked to Henry Hub prices, making US supply more competitive in the 
current market than Qatar’s oil-indexed deals.

Qatar’s LNG contract with Orlen was among the most expensive in the market, 
and it’s not certain others will follow. But the company’s decision underscores 
the challenges facing Qatar as it works to almost doubleLNG export capacity 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-09/qatar-pushes-start-of-its-massive-lng-expansion-to-end-2026?cmpid=BBBXT111126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11275>
 by early next decade.

The emirate has long-term commitments covering about 70% of the targeted 
production in 2031, according to Bloomberg calculations that factored in 
Orlen’s exit. Finding takers for the rest could be tough, given how the war has 
highlighted the risk of relying on LNG from such a volatile part of the world.

Qatar was Poland’s first LNG supplier, sending its initial cargo 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2015-10-12/poland-opens-lng-terminal-pledges-to-end-russian-gas-dependence?cmpid=BBBXT111126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11275>
 in 2015 as Warsaw stepped up efforts to reduce dependence on Russian piped gas.

Qatargas’s then-Chief Executive Officer Khalid Bin Khalifa Al-Thani was there 
to welcome the shipment on the Baltic coast, saying it “demonstrates our 
commitment to provide Europe with a clean energy source, reliably and safely.”

Unfortunately for Qatar, the US’s war in Iran meant it couldn’t be reliable 
enough.

—Stephen Stapczynski, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

This month is shaping up to be an outlier for US gas storage. BloombergNEF 
<https://about.bnef.com/> expects a 65 billion-cubic-feet injection, only the 
second November injection in the past decade. Record-strength El Niño 
conditions are skewing winter weather risks warmer, contributing to the delayed 
onset of storage withdrawals. That warm-weather support, alongside strong dry 
gas production, is expected to leave end-of-winter US gas storage above the 
2021-2025 average.
