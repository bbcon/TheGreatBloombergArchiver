China’s export dominance shows few signs of weakening as the nation’s trade 
surplus swells above $1 trillion so far this year.

Through the first 10 months of the year, China’s surplus is nearly 9% larger 
than it was during the same period of 2025, when it posted a record $1.2 
trillion, Bloomberg reports Tuesday from the world’s No. 2 economy. (Click here 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/chinese-trade-surplus-surpasses-1-trillion-for-second-time-ever?cmpid=BBD111026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11148>
 to read the full story.)

Chips and computer parts accounted for 40% of the year-on-year increase in 
Chinese export value so far, reflecting soaring prices as a result of a 
building frenzy around data centers.

In volume terms, growth came from a broad range of high-tech products, ranging 
from the “New Three” sectors promoted by China’s leadership — solar panels, 
electric vehicles and batteries — to industrial robots, according to a Goldman 
Sachs analysis.

Other signs emerged today that the AI boom is continuing to fuel exports from 
Asia. TSMC, the Taiwanese chipmaker that’s an important supplier to companies 
like Nvidia and Apple, posted a60% increase

 in monthly sales.

Tailwind or Headwind

The main suppliers of AI-related goods are emerging markets, with Taiwan, 
Mexico, Vietnam and Thailand accounting for more than 80% of US imports of 
AI-related products, according to a report this week from Oxford Economics.

“The real value-added exposure of Asian EMs and Mexico is rising fast and the 
experience of the dotcom bubble suggests it could contract sharply if the AI 
boom ends abruptly,” Sergi Lanau, Oxford’s director of EM strategy,” wrote in a 
research note.

Simon MacAdam, deputy chief global economist with Capital Economics, wrote in 
a note on Monday that “while this AI tailwind should continue to support 
exports into 2027, we think it will flip to a major headwind later in the year 
with the biggest fallout in Asia.”

Meanwhile, US officials in Washington are voicing concerns again over 
Beijing’s curbs on a different group of key exports — critical minerals. Those 
restrictions and added bureaucracy are limiting the availability of products 
used in everything from aerospace and defense to semiconductors and magnets for 
electric vehicles.

Manufacturers from Japan to Europe and the US are feeling the squeeze, 
Bloomberg reports 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/rare-earth-strategic-metals-price-gap-flags-supply-strains-trump-xi-meet?cmpid=BBD111026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11148>
 today.

Prices are reflecting the scarcity of some strategic minerals outside China. 
Yttrium, used in electronics and turbines, is a staggering 125 times more 
expensive in Europe than in China, according to price reporting agency Argus 
Media.

A ton of the metal costs about $1.15 million compared with just $9,144 in 
China, where supplies remain ample.

China’s total exports of yttrium oxide are running at about two-fifths of the 
average level for 2023-2024. Almost none has been exported to Japan this year, 
and shipments to the US are about 70% lower than before 2025.

Charted Territory

Tight market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/aluminum-faces-severe-squeeze-as-lme-market-awaits-new-supply?cmpid=BBD111026_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11148>
 | The aluminum market is in the grips of a major supply squeeze fueled by 
declining stockpiles of the crucial industrial commodity, with buyers facing 
rising prices for spot metal and little immediate prospect of relief. The 
crunch is playing out on exchanges in London and Shanghai, with both markets 
exhibiting a pricing structure known as backwardation, where spot metal trades 
at premiums to later-dated futures due to constraints on immediate delivery. 
The pressure comes after a sharp decline in inventories backing the contracts, 
with London Metal Exchange stockpiles slumping 56% this year to the lowest 
level since 1990.
