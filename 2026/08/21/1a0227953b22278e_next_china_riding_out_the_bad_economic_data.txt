Why China’s Policymakers Will Ride Out Poor Economic Data

Hi, this is Philip Glamann in Shanghai.

The casual observer could be forgiven for thinking the latest economic tidings 
were dire enough to force China’s hand. They’d be wrong.

Yes, of course, data this week did land well short of expectations 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/china-s-economy-extends-slowdown-by-weakening-across-the-board?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
. Industrial production, retail sales and fixed-asset investment all fared far 
worse than expected last month. The jobless rate for young people inched up.

On top of this, new home prices not only continued to fall but declined even 
faster in July, underscoringa persistent slump in the real estate market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/china-new-home-prices-fall-at-faster-pace-damping-rebound-hopes?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
.

That downturn really stings because the wealth of most Chinese families is in 
their homes. When housing value slides, people feel poorer and less willing to 
spend.

The overall picture seems pretty ugly, raising questions over whether China 
can hit its annual growth goal of 4.5% to 5%.

Economists at BNP Paribas, Macquarie and Pantheon say the July numbers suggest 
the nation is running below the lower end of the target range. Goldman and Citi 
warned their forecasts for a pickup in GDP growth in the third quarter could be 
in doubt.
Li Qiang, China’s premier.Photographer: Samsul Said/Bloomberg
Shortly after the economic figures were released, state media put out a speech 
in which Premier Li Qiangcalled on officials to ramp up supportive measures 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-18/xi-s-no-2-urges-more-support-for-economy-after-big-growth-slump?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
.

The government then announced it was tapping a $1.6 trillion program 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/china-widens-use-of-1-6-trillion-fund-to-spur-spending-on-homes?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
 previously used to help people buy homes. The housing provident fund could now 
be used for things like home renovations, a move aimed at spurring stubbornly 
weak consumption.

So, fair to say the government is rolling out the big guns, maybe even getting 
ready todeploy aggressive stimulus it has long shunned 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-08-18/the-china-show-8-18-2026-video?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
?

Not so fast. For now, it is staying in wait-and-see mode.

Li himself called on officials to “give full play to the effectiveness of 
existing policies,” a hint that he’s not thinking about a slew of big changes 
just yet.

Then there’s the fact that Li and his economic lieutenants can feel pretty 
good about exports, which arezipping along due to the AI boom 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-07/china-exports-imports-post-double-digit-gains-but-growth-slows?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
.

In the same economic data released this week, China reported that output of 
electronic equipment soared more than 19%. Manufacturing of industrial robots 
and production of integrated circuits also jumped.

While numbers like that will remind officials of a deep divide in their 
economy — a K-shaped phenomenon that isleaving traditional sectors and some 
regions behind 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/china-s-ai-boomtowns-race-ahead-as-rest-of-economy-falls-behind?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
 — they are so good they give decision-makers room to stand pat.

So, too, is a key political factor. Right now, front of mind for officials 
across the country is a major meeting of the ruling Communist Party in October.

Getting through that smoothly is essential considering that next year the 
party will carry out a major personnel reshuffle. Avoiding risks — like those 
that could come with major policy changes — is the smart play for a bureaucrat 
seeking a promotion.

This might explain why some of the measures we’ve seen lately to boost the 
economy can be described as modest. For example, this week the government said 
it would take steps to spur consumption … in the country’s smaller markets.

Policies focused on the county level shouldn’t rock the boat too much, and may 
even help the economy some.

Bloomberg economists Chang Shu and David Qu said they “expect a near-term lull 
in fresh policy signals as policymakers assess incoming data.”

They added: “By late September, the government should have a clearer read on 
whether the current pace of fiscal deployment is enough to meet the annual 
growth target — or whether further stimulus is needed.”

Yet the approach has a downside. It likely means that the big moves needed to 
spur consumption and fix an imbalanced economy will wait for the foreseeable 
future.

Work to Do

Another month, another big tech listing in China.

After memory chip maker CXMT 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-17/cxmt-s-rise-to-china-s-most-valuable-firm-heralds-new-tech-era?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
 raised as much as 66.6 billion yuan in Shanghai last month, this week Unitree 
Robotics became the first producer of humanoid robots to debut in China.

Unitree raised 6.1 billion yuan in its IPO and surged 460% on its first day 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-18/unitree-robotics-set-to-debut-after-904-million-shanghai-ipo?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
 — underscoring excitement that adoption of robots is accelerating.

Wang Xingxing, the millennial billionaire who founded Unitree (officially 
known as Yushu Technology Co.), should also be pretty excited. He saw his shares
soar in value to $15.9 billion 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-18/robot-dogs-make-chinese-billionaire-even-richer-despite-us-curbs?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
, though he plans to distribute some $5 billion of that to employees in the 
coming years.

Unitree’s debut will serve as a barometer for how investors view the 
commercial prospects of embodied AI, which is when algorithms are used in 
robots or autonomous vehicles.

Its successful listing could also build fundraising momentum for other Chinese 
robotics firms, including Leju Robotics and Deep Robotics.
Unitree robots perform a martial arts demonstration.Photographer: Qilai 
Shen/Bloomberg
Unitree rose to fame in China when its creations appeared in the country’s 
annual Spring Festival Gala, a widely watched holiday TV show. It is also known 
for dexterous robots used in research institutions across the US.

Unitree drew attention because it has some serious financial backing. About 
one-fifth of the offering was set aside for investors including AI startup 
DeepSeek as well as an investment vehicle affiliated with Tencent, and the 
investment arms of big state-owned enterprises in oil, electricity grids and 
telecoms.

Despite all the hoopla over the company 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/unitree-ipo-why-investors-are-betting-big-on-china-s-humanoid-robots?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
, Unitree has work to do to justify the big trading debut. For starters, just 
last month the US cracked down on foreign-made robots due to national security 
concerns, and the company has said that could hurt its sales in the world’s 
biggest economy.

Then there are questions over how quickly humanoid robots can move from 
showing off to daily use 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/unitree-founder-sees-robots-reaching-mass-market-within-decade?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
.

The number of pilot deployments is indeed rising and shipment forecasts point 
to rapid growth of the industry, but many observers are cautious about the pace 
of adoption. They say that widespread commercial use will still depend on 
advances in AI models and real-world training data.

Use of robots in the home — and to do things like help care for China’s 
growing number of elderly people — seems to be a ways off givenhow difficult 
teaching the devices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-06-05/china-s-robotics-firms-heading-for-listings-look-to-prove-they-re-here-to-stay?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
, say, fold clothes is proving.

Vey-Sern Ling, managing director at the private Swiss bank Union Bancaire 
Privée, was skeptical that Unitree deserved such a huge opening day.

“There’s clearly no fundamental basis for the share price surge,” he said, 
adding “the outlook is uncertain given the early stage of development and 
adoption of humanoids.”

JPMorgan forecasts that global shipments of humanoid robots will rise to 
60,000 units this year and 1.75 million by 2030. China will account for more 
than half of global demand, it says.

Unitree is among the companies expected to benefit most if that growth 
materializes. But right now it has its work cut out showing it deserves all the 
investor enthusiasm.

Behind the Great Firewall

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

A low-budget animated film has become a big hit in China, both with moviegoers 
and stock investors desperate for something to cheer about.

Niu Lai is a flick about a calf who learns about friendship and other life 
lessons through a series of adventures, usually with mom at his side.

The movie, made by self-taught director Xin Yumeng and his mom, Sun Lifang, 
made little money initially and got savaged by reviewers.

But that actually drew crowds keen to see the supposedly bad movie for 
themselves 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/chinese-cartoon-so-bad-you-have-to-see-it-becomes-box-office-hit?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
.

Then something funny happened on the way to the Forum 
<https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0060438/>. The movie became a hit. Many people 
liked it for the endearing story. Others began makingreferences to the stock 
market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-08-19/china-s-latest-meme-is-a-complete-load-of-bull?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
.
A hand-drawn poster for the low-budget film Niu Lai at a cinema in Beijing.
Photographer: Andy Wong/AP Photo
They noticed that the title character, Niu Lai, sounds like shorthand for “the 
bull market is coming.”

Retail investors searching for a change of fortune needed no other reason to 
start snapping up tickets. After all, they’d love a diversion and improved luck 
considering the nation’s benchmark stock gauge, the CSI 300, has tumbled since 
its high in June.

“Watching Niu Lai is like going to a temple to burn incense,” lawyer and 
commentator Zhang Jie said in a video posted on Weibo, where he has more than 
2.6 million followers.

“It’s not really about whether the movie is good,” he added. “It’s about 
expressing a certain sentiment.”
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/games/pointed?cmpid=BBD082126_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260821&utm_content=7887>
Equity investors thought they found other signs in the movie. Some interpreted 
a scene in which Niu Lai trips on a rock as a metaphor for their losses in 
semiconductors, a sector that stumbled in July.

And others took a scene where the calf cries out for his mother as an appeal 
for help from the central bank, which traders in China sometimes refer to as 
“central mother.”

While the movie gave a boost to a handful of shares whose names included the 
Chinese character for cow, the broader market seems immune to the animal’s 
charms. Stocks are set to retreat for a second week.
