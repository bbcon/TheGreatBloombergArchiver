Competing priorities

By Fabiano Maisonnave 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXmFWA_HFBY/fabiano-maisonnave?cmpid=BBD101526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10127>

On a hot, dusty day in August, road crews descended on a crumbling two-lane 
highway in the middle of the Amazon rainforest. Their job was to pave a dirt 
stretch of the route, which runs 550 miles from Rondonia state up to the port 
of Manaus on the Amazon River.

For most residents of Manaus and nearby areas in Brazil’s Amazonas state, the 
project is a godsend. It promises a modern, convenient link — the first by land 
— to prosperous regions in the country’s south and southeast. BR-319, as the 
highway is numbered, is a long-overdue answer to the expense and unreliability 
of moving goods by water.

But environmentalists worry that deforestation will follow the roadworks, as 
it has time and again in Brazil’s Amazon, with settlers arriving to clear land 
and plant crops or raise cattle.

Read More: How Saving Brazil’s Rainforest Pushed the Crisis Into Bolivia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-08-07/why-deforestation-is-surging-in-bolivia-as-brazil-protects-the-amazon?cmpid=BBD101526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10127>

Suely Araújo, a former head of Ibama, Brazil’s federal environment agency, who 
now works for the research and advocacy coalition Climate Observatory, said 
work on the road is outpacing measures to avoid negative impacts like land 
grabbing and forest clearing. Climate Observatory has sued to halt it. 
Completing BR-319 would be a “historic mistake,” she said.
Trucks travel through a preserved area of the Amazon along the BR-319, in 
Berurii, Amazonas state.Photographer: Victor Moriyama/Bloomberg
Brazilians will go to the polls on Oct. 25, and if Flávio Bolsonaro 
<bbg://people/profile/20991337> unseats President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva 
<bbg://people/profile/1747604>, there will likely be more BR-319s. During a 
campaign visit to Manaus last month, Bolsonaro said he wants to pave additional 
roads in Amazonas, which is largely preserved forest. He blamed delays in doing 
so on red tape driven by “left-wing ideology in the licensing agencies.” “This 
will be resolved,” he promised. The conservative senator — a son of former 
President Jair Bolsonaro — supports farmers settling more of the Amazon, with 
faster titling of land.

Over the past 40 years, native forests and green areas across the Brazilian 
Amazon have shrunk by 208,000 square miles (539,000 square kilometers), an area 
roughly the size of France, according to MapBiomas, a network of universities, 
nonprofits and technology companies. The loss is the main source of climate 
emissions in Brazil. Virtually all of it has taken place along major roads.

Lula, a leftist, has since pushed deforestation down to record lows 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-07/brazil-s-amazon-deforestation-rate-approaches-a-historic-low?cmpid=BBD101526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10127>
. But Lula himself supports the BR-319 project. It shows him trying to 
reconcile two priorities as he runs for reelection: conserving the rainforest, 
and improving living standards and opportunities in a poor region within one of 
the world’s most unequal countries.

Waste not, want not

By Sanne Wass 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWV_9b7EAeo/sanne-wass?cmpid=BBD101526_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261015&utm_content=10127>

Opening a mystery bag from the surplus-food app Too Good To Go has become a 
winning formula for influencers on TikTok. In one viral video, a woman shows 
how $6.99 landed her 32 bagels. Another shopper reveals seven pounds of cake. 
Soon, they might be opening packages of houseplants, dog treats or Halloween 
decorations.

Too Good To Go has built a global following of about 150 million thrifty and 
eco-conscious users since getting started in 2015 with a simple idea: Keeping 
perfectly edible food out of landfill, where it generatespotent greenhouse gases
 
<https://www.unep.org/en/events/unep-event/towards-zero-waste-and-circular-cities-key-findings-food-waste-index-report-2024>
. Now, the Danish company is betting the concept will work beyond food.

It’s starting to list surprise bags of makeup, pet food, plants, seasonal 
decor, supplements and more. The company has signed up 8,500 vendors offering 
non-food products, such as flowers from Whole Foods Market Inc. and Dove 
skincare, and expects the category to become a growing part of its business in 
the coming few years.

Chief Executive Officer Mette Lykke says she wants to give products a second 
chance at reaching consumers. Before people do their shopping, “we would like 
to create more of a habit of checking what’s available out there,” Lykke said 
in an interview at Too Good To Go’s headquarters in Copenhagen.
Food loss and waste together account for about 8% to 10% of global 
greenhouse-gas emissions, according to the United Nations Environment Programme.
Photographer: Jack Taylor/Getty ImagesSource: Bloomberg via Photographer: Jack

The business model is based on giving suppliers a way to recover some value 
from goods that would otherwise be discarded, by offering items at a discount. 
App users typically pay a quarter to half of the original retail value for a 
bag, snapping up about 400,000 meals a day on average, according to the company.
