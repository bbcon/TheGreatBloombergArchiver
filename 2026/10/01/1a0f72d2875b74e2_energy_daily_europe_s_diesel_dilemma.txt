Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
Europe oil team leader Alaric Nightingale looks at the dilemma the continent 
faces as the US pushes it to release more diesel reserves.

The US is piling pressure 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-30/trump-downplays-diesel-export-ban-over-impact-on-other-fuels?cmpid=BBBXT100126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261001&utm_content=9578>
 on the European Union to release diesel stockpiles to help contain soaring 
prices for the industrial fuel, and the bloc needs to tread carefully in its 
response.

Government officials in Europe have told Bloomberg that they believe their 
release rates have been adequate for meeting the market’s needs and in line 
with what was agreed under efforts coordinated by the International Energy 
Agency.

They’ve also pointed to lackluster take-up of the supplies that have already 
been offered to the market.

Even so, it’s not clear why, given rocketing prices for diesel, Europeans 
haven’t been dipping into their strategic stocks more.

All the while that American inventories have been slumping, millions of 
barrels have been getting shipped on tankers to Europe from refineries on the 
Gulf Coast, contributing torecord prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/diesel-prices-at-record-highs-in-the-us-as-iran-war-hits-seventh-month?cmpid=BBBXT100126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261001&utm_content=9578>
 of more than $6.50 a gallon back in the US.
A fuel pump displaying $7.99 per gallon of diesel fuel at a G&M gas station in 
Torrance, California this week.Photographer: Caroline Brehman/Bloomberg
Indeed, figures from the Joint Organizations Data Initiative show that, until 
July, American stockpiles had been draining faster in both percentage and 
barrel terms than any other nation that’s taking part in the IEA’s coordinated 
release.

Reuters reported 
<https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/us-tells-france-germany-release-diesel-stocks-or-face-us-export-ban-sources-say-2026-10-01/>
 today that the US has told Europe directly to release inventories or risk 
facing diesel-export curbs.

That threat can’t be ignored. Long self-deprived of Russian imports (which are 
banned because of the Ukraine war), Europe’s single-biggest source of external 
diesel supply is now the US.

The need for those shipments has become even more acute because of the Iran 
war. The conflict has choked off supply from both the Persian Gulf and Red Sea.

If European Union member states miscalculate, the consequences for the bloc’s 
diesel prices, which have become afocal point for central bankers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-09/oil-s-crack-spread-captivates-central-bankers-fretting-inflation?cmpid=BBBXT100126_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261001&utm_content=9578>
 trying to rein in inflation, could be grave.

—Alaric Nightingale, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

The oil market is growing more vulnerable as the Strait of Hormuz stays 
disrupted, with Brent averaging about $102 in September. Under BloombergNEF’s 
base case scenario of a November Hormuz reopening, Brent is forecast to average 
$92 in 2026 and $75 in 2027. A closure lasting through the winter until April 
2027 could push Brent to between $147 and $150.
