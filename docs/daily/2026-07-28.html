Daily Brief

Tuesday, July 28, 2026

US

  • No major US macro data releases on July 28; the Federal Reserve meeting this week is the dominant focus, with Citadel Securities calling for a surprise rate hike.
  • S&P 500 futures fell 0.1–0.3% and Nasdaq 100 futures declined 0.8–0.9% as AI spending fears spread from Asian equity markets into US tech.
  • Washington imposed new blanket tariffs of at least 10% on 60 trading partners — markets barely reacted, a sign of "tariff fatigue" following Liberation Day.
  • Major hyperscaler earnings — Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, and Apple — are due Wednesday through Thursday, with capex scrutiny the central investor concern.

Monetary Policy

The Federal Reserve meeting this week carries unusual uncertainty. Citadel Securities publicly argued that a surprise rate hike would strengthen Chairman Kevin Warsh's inflation-fighting credibility, with its head of macro strategy noting that "the market may once again be underestimating the extent of the hawkish shift at the Fed." No consensus has formed around such a move, but the possibility alone is shaping rates markets, with the US 10-year Treasury yield at 4.62%.

Technology and Markets

US equity futures weakened ahead of the open, with chip names under broad pressure: Nvidia fell 1.3%, AMD 4%, and Micron 5%. The more consequential story, however, is this week's hyperscaler earnings. Alphabet's prior-week results — which included cloud revenue surging more than 80% but free cash flow turning negative for the first time since its 2004 IPO — set a cautionary template. Investors are increasingly unwilling to reward revenue growth funded by ever-expanding capex, with the five largest hyperscalers planning roughly $740 billion in combined AI investment for 2026. Microsoft now trades at approximately 19 times forward earnings, well below its 10-year average of 27. ---

Asia

  • South Korea's Kospi plunged 11% on July 28, triggering multiple trading halts — the index's largest single-day decline in recent memory — as Samsung and SK Hynix each fell more than 13%.
  • A report that a Chinese state-backed company has begun mass-producing immersion deep-ultraviolet lithography machines fueled fears of rising competitive capacity and a potential flood of global chip supply.
  • Bank Indonesia Governor Perry Warjiyo abruptly resigned for "personal reasons," shocking investors who had met with him in Singapore just days earlier with no indication of his departure.

Semiconductor Selloff

The proximate trigger for Asian equity losses was a confluence of AI-spending anxiety and China's reported chipmaking advances. SK Hynix has now fallen approximately 47% from its all-time high set in June, erasing market value comparable to SpaceX — this despite expectations for record quarterly earnings on Wednesday. The dynamic illustrates a sentiment shift: investors are no longer buying the dip in AI-adjacent hardware stocks, instead interpreting each data point as a reason to reduce exposure. Japan's Nikkei 225 and Taiwan's Taiex each fell roughly 4%. A Taiwanese court detained an Nvidia employee as part of a chip-smuggling probe, one of at least eight such investigations across four jurisdictions — adding regulatory overhang to sector sentiment.

Indonesia Political Risk

Warjiyo's abrupt departure from Bank Indonesia introduces policy uncertainty at a sensitive juncture. His resignation — characterized as for "personal reasons" — raised immediate questions about political interference in monetary policy under President Prabowo, and the rupiah came under pressure. The central bank had been navigating a delicate balance between supporting growth and defending the currency; continuity of that approach is now in question. ---

Europe

  • European equity markets reflected global tech weakness, with Philips falling 11% in Amsterdam on margin pressure in its medical-imaging division and Barclays dropping its most in over a year on earnings that lagged US peers.
  • Unilever and Mercedes-Benz offered contrasting bright spots: Unilever rose 6% after lifting its full-year outlook, while Mercedes gained 3.9% after beating profit expectations despite a China-driven volume decline.
  • Massive wildfires in France and Spain continued for a second week, with more than 300,000 people evacuated; both governments are preparing emergency financial aid packages.

Corporate Earnings

Mercedes-Benz's resilient profit performance — which beat consensus — was driven by cost discipline rather than volume, as deliveries fell due to weakening Chinese consumer demand amid the prolonged property downturn. The result illustrates a broader tension facing European automakers: margin defense through restructuring while confronting structural demand erosion in their most important growth market. Barclays, by contrast, reported earnings that disappointed relative to US investment banking peers, reflecting a share of dealmaking activity that has not kept pace with the Wall Street recovery.

Wildfire Emergency

France and Spain are preparing emergency-zone declarations and financial support packages for wildfire-affected regions. Madrid's regional government announced an initial €30 million aid package. French Finance Minister Lescure committed insurer coverage for relocation costs for the more than 200,000 evacuees. Officials warned that a new heat wave forecast for this week could render containment efforts unpredictable. MSCI's CEO Henry Fernandez used the moment to underscore climate risk's growing materiality to asset valuations, noting that "rising temperatures are now a source of risk and return in any investment." ---

LatAm

  • Chile's central bank is expected to hold its benchmark interest rate unchanged at its July 28 meeting, with no surprise anticipated.
  • Keiko Fujimori was sworn in as Peru's president on July 28, becoming the ninth head of state in a decade in a country where every elected leader since the 1990s has faced impeachment, imprisonment, or criminal investigation.
  • IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva arrived in Buenos Aires to assess Argentina's economic recovery under President Milei, expressing optimism as the country approaches the critical repayment phase of its IMF program.

Peru Political Transition

Fujimori's inauguration marks a significant moment for Peru's political economy, though the structural fragility of the country's institutions remains unchanged. The sheer turnover rate of heads of state — nine in a decade — continues to deter long-term investment and complicates policy continuity, particularly in the mining and energy sectors where Peru holds substantial resource wealth. Investors will monitor her early cabinet and fiscal appointments closely.

Argentina and the IMF

Georgieva's visit signals cautious institutional confidence in Milei's stabilization program. Argentina has moved from chronic IMF tension to a more cooperative posture, but the program's real test — debt repayment — is now imminent. The IMF chief's public optimism provides political cover domestically but does not reduce the fundamental challenge of rebuilding reserves while maintaining fiscal adjustment. The trajectory of commodity prices, particularly given energy market disruption from the Iran conflict, will influence export revenues and the program's arithmetic. ---

Global

  • Brent crude fell as much as 4.1% on July 28 to approach $86 a barrel, extending Monday's 8.7% decline — the largest single-day drop in more than three months — after Trump indicated the US and Iran are engaged in talks and a ceasefire extension is holding.
  • Washington imposed new blanket tariffs of at least 10% on 60 trading partners, citing forced-labor concerns; markets absorbed the announcement with minimal reaction, a stark contrast to Liberation Day 2025's shock selloff.
  • The Swiss National Bank is expected to hold its key rate at zero through end-2027 before beginning to raise, according to sources — among the most dovish trajectories of any major central bank.

Oil Markets and Strait of Hormuz

The scale of the oil price decline reflects the degree to which geopolitical risk premium had been built into energy markets. Iran's de facto seizure of the Strait of Hormuz had severely disrupted tanker traffic, with Iran clinging to enriched uranium stocks and Gulf Arab states absorbing strikes. The pause in hostilities — and talks brokered via Oman on restarting Hormuz shipping — has released some of that premium. However, ECB researchers caution that conditions remain "highly volatile," and that the pre-crisis market had an unusually favorable starting point: a supply surplus of approximately 2.5 million barrels per day, large inventories, and aggressive strategic reserve releases. A prolonged disruption scenario has not been eliminated.

Tariff Fatigue and Structural Trade Costs

The non-reaction to the latest US tariff round is analytically significant. The Supreme Court's February ruling constrained presidential trade discretion, companies have adapted pricing strategies — including "trickle-up" incremental price increases under long-term contracts — and bond markets are less reflexively hostile given fiscal revenue needs. Yet the long-term structural damage to investment planning, captured in what analysts are calling the "Great Hesitation," may be the more consequential effect: capital expenditure decisions with 10-year horizons are increasingly difficult to authorize under persistent policy uncertainty, creating a slow-moving drag on global growth that does not manifest in short-term volatility.
