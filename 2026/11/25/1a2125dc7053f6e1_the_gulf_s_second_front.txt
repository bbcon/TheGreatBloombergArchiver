The Saudi Arabia-Houthi conflict is worsening, with the Iran-backed group 
targeting the kingdom’s 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-25/houthis-hit-saudi-finance-center-extending-tit-for-tat-attacks?cmpid=BBD112526_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11845>
 main financial district and energy ports, as well as making gains in Yemen.

After capturing much of the western coastline, the Houthis are now pushing 
toward Aden, a port city of hundreds of thousands of people.

Controlling Aden would consolidate the Islamist militants’ grip over two 
waterways vital for global shipping and energy markets: the Gulf of Aden and 
the Bab el-Mandeb Strait at the southern end of the Red Sea.

The Houthis’ latest gains are part of an offensive they launched 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/mbs-and-saudi-arabia-face-crisis-with-key-oil-pipeline-shut-for-weeks?cmpid=BBD112526_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11845>
 in September when they moved south from the northwestern territories they’ve 
held for over a decade. They rapidly beat back Saudi-backed groups, including 
those of Yemen’s internationally recognized government, and took the port of 
Mokha.

Taking, let alone holding, Aden will be far from easy for the Houthis. They 
captured it in 2015, only to be quickly driven out by forces supported by the 
United Arab Emirates.

Their success this time will depend on whether the disparate anti-Houthi 
fighters — now mainly backed by Saudi Arabia and assisted by the kingdom’s 
fighter jets that have struck Yemen hundreds of times since last month — can 
set aside longstanding rivalries.

Those disputes may be the biggest advantage the Houthis have as they look to 
eventually control the whole country, which hasbeen at civil war 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-20/why-are-the-houthis-so-hard-for-saudi-arabia-to-defeat?cmpid=BBD112526_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11845>
 for around 12 years, and its roughly 40 million people.
Houthi supporters in Sanaa during a protest against Saudi intervention in 
Yemen.Photographer: Mohammed Huwais/AFP/Getty Images
The sides are preparing for a prolonged confrontation, likely lasting into 
next year.

The fighting, effectively a second front in the US-Iran war, is hurting the 
Saudi economy and Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman’s efforts to attract 
billions of dollars in foreign investment.

Still, for now, his ministers are sending a message of resilience 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-24/saudis-see-economy-taking-smaller-hit-from-war-as-oil-flows-rise?cmpid=BBD112526_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11845>
 and saying they can contain the crisis, even as they urge patience. — Veena 
Ali-Khan

Chart of the Day

The UK is poised to register its lowest level of net migration 
<bbg://screens/NSN%20TP0JT5T9NJLS> outside the Covid pandemic in almost three 
decades. Just 54,000 more people are thought to have come to the UK than have 
left in the 12 months through June, analysis by James Bowes, a migration 
specialist at Warwick University, shows. Oxford University’s Migration 
Observatory projects a level around 120,000, which would still be a 29-year 
low, excluding 2020. Official data are due to be published tomorrow.
