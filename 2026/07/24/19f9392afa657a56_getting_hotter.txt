Forget fun in the sun: Europe’s summer is growing more dangerous.

The heat death toll is already close to 20,000 people, according to a 
Bloomberg compilation ofexcess fatalities 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-07/germany-recorded-5-000-excess-deaths-in-late-june-heat-wave?cmpid=BBD072426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6908>
 in countries including Spain, France, Germany andthe UK 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-12/heat-waves-may-have-led-to-over-2-700-deaths-in-england-wales?cmpid=BBD072426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6908>
 in June and July to date.

The Spanish government declared a national state of emergency 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-24/spain-declares-national-emergency-over-wildfires-around-madrid?cmpid=BBD072426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6908>
 over wildfires for the first time today after blazes went out of control on 
the outskirts of Madrid.

The string of heat waves 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-28/france-has-1-000-excess-deaths-as-heat-wave-sweeps-across-europe?cmpid=BBD072426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6908>
 has shocked people across the continent, with extreme temperatures made worse 
by climate change. Roads cracked, railways melted and homes became dangerously 
overheated, changing the way residents, economists and political leaders 
perceive the threat.
A wildfire burns near Madrid.Photographer: Alberto Ortega/Sipa/AP Photo
The death rates 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-01/spain-saw-record-heat-deaths-for-june-after-heat-wave-hit-europe?cmpid=BBD072426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6908>
 as well as the discomfort experienced by millions should be a “wake-up call 
for all European policymakers,” said Carsten Brzeski, global head of macro at 
ING Group NV. The economist, who spends most of his time focusing on inflation 
and gross domestic product, warned the thermometer has now “become a leading 
indicator” for tracking economic growth.

Numbers on the economic impact 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/heat-morphs-into-a-major-economic-shock-for-an-unprepared-europe?cmpid=BBD072426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6908>
 are trickling in. One estimateput the cost 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-23/heat-wave-in-june-cost-the-uk-economy-1-5-billion?cmpid=BBD072426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6908>
 of a single week of extreme heat in the UK at £1.15 billion ($1.5 billion) 
through lost working hours. That climbs to £2.36 billion by another calculation 
if heat-induced failures in infrastructure and equipment are included.

Leaders are starting to react. London Mayor Sadiq Khan said the city will need 
private investors to help fund a multi-billion effort to adapt homes.

Even France’s far-right National Rally party, whose head, Marine Le Pen, once 
dismissed the major UN-backed IPCC climate report as “very, very alarmist,” has 
acknowledged the need to take the science seriously.

August is traditionally the worst month for heat.

All the indications are that Europe’s heat waves will only get longer, more 
intense and — unless urgent measures are taken — even deadlier.— Laura Millan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD072426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6908>
People stand in the shade in Paris on June 26.Photographer: Benjamin 
Girette/Bloomberg

Chart of the Day

Japan has one of the highest concentrations of zoos per capita in the world 
and fans travel hundreds of miles for a glimpse of celebrity animals. Yet 
behind the crowds, a network developed over almost 150 years is facing an 
uncertain future as rising costs collide with tightening municipal budgets in 
one of the world’s fastest-aging societies. Many zoos with hundreds, sometimes 
thousands, of animals are now struggling to sustain them, raising difficult 
questions about how ashrinking society 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-24/japan-s-zoos-aren-t-benefiting-from-their-world-famous-animals?cmpid=BBD072426_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6908>
