Europe’s wildfires worsen

By Thomas Gualtieri 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ATE2PsR0P_A/thomas-gualtieri?cmpid=BBD072426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6944>
,Nayla Razzouk 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AP4EwDhUUiw/nayla-razzouk?cmpid=BBD072426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6944>
, andSabrina Nelson Garcinuño 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXeC6LJ3eas/sabrina-nelson-garcinuno?cmpid=BBD072426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6944>

Spain has declared a state of emergency and sent the military to try to 
contain wildfires near Madrid, while France evacuated thousands of tourists 
threatened by a blaze west of Bordeaux.

More than 10,000 people have been evacuated from areas as close as 50 
kilometers (31 miles) from the Spanish capital, with more than 270 firefighters 
trying to extinguish the blazes. In Ávila province, about 1,500 people have 
been evacuated. Several highways have been closed.
Firefighters at the site of a wildfire near Madrid on July 23.Photographer: 
Alberto Ortega/Sipa/AP Photo
Spain and France have been at the epicenter of Europe’s wildfire season, which 
started early this year and accelerated in recent weeks as successive heat 
waves baked vegetation, making it easier for flames to spread rapidly. Fires 
have burned more than 300,000 hectares across the continent, destroying crops 
and disrupting transport services.

French authorities ordered the evacuation of 40,000 residents and tourists 
after wildfires threatened to reach Cap Ferret peninsula, a popular tourist 
area west of Bordeaux, Interior Minister Laurent Nunez said. The blazes have 
burned about 10,000 hectares and engulfed a number of houses.
Gendarmes evacuate a resident near Lege-Cap-Ferret.Photographer: Julien De 
Rosa/AFP/Getty Images
By declaring a national emergency for Madrid and Avila, the Spanish government 
has taken control of the response, with the Interior Ministry coordinating 
efforts and the country’s military emergency unit managing field operations. 
Other wildfires are burning across the country.

“Very adverse meteorological conditions are expected for tomorrow,” Madrid 
region president Isabel Díaz Ayuso wrote in a post on X on Thursday evening. 
“It is necessary to reinforce national and even international support to these 
fires.”

Temperatures in Andalucia will climb as high as 44C (111F) on Friday, with red 
and amber heat alerts throughout Spain’s central and eastern provinces, 
according to meteorological agency Aemet.

Climate change is intensifying periods of extreme heat in Europe, the 
fastest-warming continent. Record-breaking heat and drought have fueled the
worst start ever 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-12/worst-start-to-wildfire-season-raises-alarm-as-el-nino-threatens?cmpid=BBD072426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6944>
 to the global wildfire season, with climate scientists warning that a 
developing El Niño could bring even harsher conditions later this summer.

Predicting where the smoke will go

By Summer Maxwell 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AYSnifPVQIs/summer-maxwell?cmpid=BBD072426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6944>

As the climate warms, wildfires are increasing in frequency and intensity. 
Still, scientists say, their ability to forecast where and how the resulting 
smoke will show up is limited, leaving communities little advance notice to 
prepare for unhealthy air quality.
Smoke from wildfires shrouds the Manhattan skyline on July 16.Photographer: 
Victor J. Blue/Bloomberg
“This is what makes the risks challenging for people to understand, because 
you can’t plan for it,” said Joseph Allen, an environmental health researcher 
at the Harvard University T.H. Chan School of Public Health. “And it seems to 
just hit us all at once.”

Major cities across the US Midwest and Northeast that were blanketed with 
heavy smoke fromCanadian wildfires 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-16/smoke-engulfs-new-york-chicago-as-canada-fires-rage-photos?cmpid=BBD072426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260724&utm_content=6944>
 last week had little advance warning before dangerously polluted air kept 
millions of Americans inside and snarled sporting events and concerts.

Scientists predict smoke behavior by combining satellite observations of 
wildfires with weather forecasts. Satellite imagery, however, only provides a 
snapshot of data, not a continuous picture of conditions, and cannot penetrate 
cloud cover or intense smoke. That makes collecting measurements challenging.

Even under perfect conditions, observing fires from the atmosphere doesn’t 
allow for granular observations of variables like a fire’s temperature, a key 
factor in predicting where smoke will travel.

“Ideally we need to know fire temperatures at a meter resolution,” said Ravan 
Ahmadov, a research scientist at the National Oceanic and Atmospheric 
Administration. “But that’s still very hard, impossible to get just based on 
the satellite observations.”
