Tariffs Return

Governments in the countries targeted by President Donald Trump’s latest 
tariffs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-25/us-targets-china-eu-with-new-tariffs-after-overcapacity-probe?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
 have expressed their annoyance publicly. Behind closed doors, there are likely
plenty of businesses 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-25/us-tariff-proposal-raises-stakes-for-india-to-clinch-trade-deal?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
 who are equally upset.

The measures apply to many economies that featured heavily in the so-called 
“China+1” strategy — where companies sought alternative bases for production – 
and enjoyed an investment boom as a result. Now, countries like Vietnam could 
potentially end up withrates 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/brazil-lashes-out-at-new-us-tariffs-saying-it-will-not-yield?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
 approaching or even in some cases exceeding those onChinese goods 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/trump-s-china-tariffs-raise-threat-of-new-test-for-trade-truce?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
.

A lot of firms “will say: ‘Well, screw it, we’re just going to go back to 
China,’” said Deborah Elms, head of trade policy at the Hinrich Foundation in 
Singapore. “Why would we be dealing with this massive amount of paperwork, 
compliance costs and tariff changes with the threat ahead if we don’t need to.”

The US rates announced on Wednesday 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-25/us-proposes-excess-capacity-tariffs-full-list?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
 aren’t set in stone, with room for countries to finalizetrade deals 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-25/us-tariff-proposal-raises-stakes-for-india-to-clinch-trade-deal?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
 with the Trump administration or find some other way toappease it 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/us-partners-respond-to-tariffs-with-anger-protest-and-diplomacy?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
. Still, it’s a statement of intent toward nations that in Washington’s eyes 
have done enough.

Several of the 14 economies 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/singapore-is-very-disappointed-by-trump-s-proposed-tariff-hike?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
 subject to the new tariffs are in Southeast Asia, a region that’s seen 
investment surge along with the push to diversify from China, which accelerated 
after Trump’s first-term tariffs in 2018. Last year, foreign direct investment 
into the region reached a record $246 billion, surpassing East Asia for the 
first time since the early 1990s.

Perhaps the biggest gainer from “China+1” has been Vietnam 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/trump-riles-trade-partners-with-tariffs-that-undercut-china-1?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
, which became the regional growth champion on a wave of electronics 
production, churning out smartphones and laptops for US markets. It’s now 
threatened by a 35% US tariff, which would put the country “at a competitive 
disadvantage,” according to Travis Mitchell, executive director of the American 
Chamber of Commerce in Vietnam.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-11-26/breaking-down-us-plans-for-new-tariffs-video?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
To be sure, it’s not easy to uproot factories and move them somewhere else, so 
many businesses that moved production to economies like Vietnam will likely opt 
to stay put. Still, Trump has just added a new layer of uncertainty for 
companies.

It’s hard to “make decisions on pricing, sales forecasts, production planning 
and future investments,” says Mitchell at the American chamber, “without 
knowing what tariff rates they will be facing in the coming months.”

Need-to-Know Research

With public debts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-26/ai-crisis-would-collide-with-political-mire-scope-ratings-says?cmpid=BBD112626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261126&utm_content=11898>
 high and rising, economists and policymakers are devoting a lot of attention 
to fiscal matters. The debate typically relies on “outcome data” — such as 
budget results — rather than actions, according to the International Monetary 
Fund. That’s because “systematic cross-country evidence on what governments 
actually do, and why, remains scarce,” the IMF wrote, and it’s seeking to fill 
that gap.

A dozen-strong team of IMF economists has constructed a Global Fiscal Actions 
dataset 
<https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2026/english/wpiea2026221-source-pdf.pdf>
, which uses large language models to identify and classify discretionary 
actions by authorities in 142 countries since 1990. To make sure its AI model 
was interpreting the data right, the fund asked a couple of its staffers to 
doublecheck a sample.

“The model agrees with two IMF economists on the direction of fiscal actions 
about nine times in ten,” it reported, “roughly as often as the two economists 
agree with each other.”

The new resource will be invaluable for future research into questions like 
how fiscal policy affects inequality, or whether actions taken in the name of 
debt sustainability prove more lasting than others, the IMF team wrote in a
VoxEU article 
<https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/inside-fiscal-policy-new-dataset-government-actions>
.

They also cited some initial findings. One is that fiscal spending actions 
tend to be “procyclical” — or reinforcing the current direction of the economy 
— outside of the developed world, though they’ve become less so in all country 
groups over the past decade. Tax actions, on the other hand, are much less 
correlated with the business cycle.
