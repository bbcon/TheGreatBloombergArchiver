When the Levee Breaks <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hCSnBaoqAzY>

What’s going to break and when will it happen? Bond yields have continued 
their global march higher to start the week. In the latest landmark, the yield 
on 30-year inflation-protected Treasuries (TIPS) has risen to 3.28%, the 
highest since the Treasury began continuous issuance in 2004 (30-year TIPS had 
been discontinued for a few years before that):

Inflation-linked bonds 
<https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2008/200805/index.html> have been 
dogged by liquidity problems, but this looks like the biggest yield US 
investors have ever demanded for very long-term lending over and above 
inflation. Such moves directly drive up the cost of innumerable other 
transactions. Many feared that 5% on the 10-year, breached two weeks ago, could 
bea watershed moment 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-09-28/rockefeller-s-sharma-warns-of-ai-crash-as-10-yr-hits-5-video?cmpid=BBD092926_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260929&utm_content=9394>
. And still there’s no obvious crisis in any nook of the market yet.

US stocks have been moving sideways, with the main S&P 500 benchmark only 1.5% 
below its August record. It’s still up 12.2% for the year, despite the drastic 
tightening of the bond market. Stocks in the rest of the world, as measured by 
FTSE, are down 3.7% from a high earlier this month, but this is still a far 
milder move than the eruption in bonds.


Big Take

Bond Yields Come for the Stock Market
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/audio/2026-09-28/big-take-bond-yields-come-for-the-stock-market-podcast?cmpid=BBD092926_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260929&utm_content=9394>
There are signs of stress, but largely only signaling a retreat from unusually 
easy conditions. The Treasury’s successful effort to limit bond volatility, a 
strategy that effectively eases financial conditions by making it cheaper to 
use bonds as collateral, has been reversed. The MOVE index declined in a 
remarkably smooth way for much of last year, but this sell-off has jolted it 
upward. That said, this remains fundamentally orderly compared with the way 
bonds reacted to the pandemic in 2020 and the crisis around Silicon Valley Bank 
in 2023:

As for the mainstream credit market, there is no sign of additional pressure, 
at least on the overall index level. The option-adjusted spread payable on 
Bloomberg’s index of US investment-grade compared to Treasuries has barely 
moved over the last few weeks and remains at historically low levels. The 
Treasury sell-off is increasing companies’ borrowing costs, but not prompting 
any rethink - as yet - on how much extra risk they carry:

The generalized move higher in yields across the world has had limited impact 
on the dollar. It’s rallied in the last few weeks. The Bloomberg’s DXY dollar 
index, comparing the greenback to a basket of advanced and emerging currencies, 
is back above its 200-day moving average - but it’s still well below the level 
when President Donald Trump returned to power at the start of last year. The 
dollar has certainly not enjoyed the kind of emphatic change of trend witnessed 
in bonds:

Emerging markets are showing the clearest signs of strain, particularly in 
foreign exchange. Mexico’s peso, unavoidably exposed to the US, has experienced 
the sharpest moves, while the broader JPMorgan EM FX index, a popular 
benchmark, has dropped to a low for the year:

Marc Chandler of Bannockburn Global Forex explains that the peso has been hit 
by the unwinding of the dollar carry trade, in which investors borrow in 
dollars and park elsewhere - with Mexico one of the most popular destinations. 
As yields have risen, both in the US and Japan, long the most popular venue for 
borrowing at low rates, so traders have sold pesos to close their trades. 
Recent price activity suggests the carry trade in the dollar was almost as 
significant as in the yen:

For now, Chandler suggests the question is: “What’s going to break?” As it’s 
the surprising strength of the US economy that has driven the ramp-up in 
yields, he says a likely trigger for a bond turnaround would be a negative 
surprise in the American data. At present, the real-time GDP tracker run by the 
Atlanta Fed indicates that the US is growing by more than 5% and faster than 
China:

The turn of the month will bring the usual welter of data, with personal 
consumption expenditures, ISM purchasing manager indexes and US non-farm 
payrolls all due by the end of this week. If bonds’ slide is to be arrested any 
time soon, it will probably because something in these numbers disappoints.

There’s an Awful Lot of Coffee...

Before the US midterms, Brazil is about to give us quite an electoral opening 
act. With the first round of its presidential election due Saturday, and a 
runoff round two weeks later if necessary, prediction markets are a) totally 
unsure who will win, but b) increasingly convinced that the incumbent Luiz 
Inacio Lula da Silva, a left-winger, stands a very real chance of losing his 
job to the right-winger Flavio Bolsonaro:

As you’d expect from anything Brazilian, the characters involved are all 
larger than life. Bolsonaro’s father, Jair, was president for four years until 
losing to Lula in 2022 in an election whose result he refused to accept and 
attempted to overturn. The elder Bolsonaro spent time in prison for his role in 
an alleged coup attempt, just as Lula, also president from 2002 to 2010, was 
incarcerated for his part in a massive money-laundering scheme.

Lula has made clear that he is worried by announcing a 15% increase in social 
welfare payments earlier this month, which would come into effect after the 
election - much more pleasing for voters than for international capital 
markets. As prediction markets move against him, he has also announced aban on 
online betting 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-25/lula-to-ban-online-betting-offer-debt-relief-before-election?cmpid=BBD092926_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260929&utm_content=9394>
, which appears to be a popular policy afterBrazilians reacted negatively 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-25/as-debt-crushes-voters-lula-paints-online-gambling-as-villain?cmpid=BBD092926_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260929&utm_content=9394>
 to massive gambling promotions during the World Cup.

For opinionated observations on a fascinating election by British transplants 
named John now living in New York, tryJohn Oliver’s segment on Bolsonaro vs. 
Lula <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MIYCek92ICQ> from Last Week Tonight 
<https://www.youtube.com/@LastWeekTonight>, or my interview on Brazil and Latin 
America withthe Podgecaste podcast 
<https://podgecaste.substack.com/p/understanding-latin-americas-economic?utm_source=post-email-title&publication_id=3043849&post_id=217270955&utm_campaign=email-post-title&isFreemail=true&r=1tnx7&triedRedirect=true&utm_medium=email>
. For now, it will suffice to look at the economic and political implications.

At least at first, the market response to the last two elections - won by 
Bolsonaro’s father in 2018 and lost in 2022 - shows that equities are likely to 
sell off on a Lula victory this time. William Jackson, chief emerging markets 
economist at Capital Economics, said:
In the 100 working days (roughly four months) after the 2018 first round vote, 
Brazilian equities had outperformed peers by 20%; after the 2022 vote, they had 
underperformed by 15%. Similar moves under Flávio/Lula seem reasonable, 
although it’s worth noting that some positive news surrounding a Flávio victory 
has been priced in over the past couple of months as his polling has improved
Similar arguments, based on experience, apply to the currency. Robin Brooks of 
the Brookings Institution argues:
Markets will reward a Bolsonaro win with a materially stronger real. That’s 
not a personal judgement on Bolsonaro over Lula, but is based on recent sharp 
gains in the Colombian peso after the conservative candidate won that election 
a few months ago and 2022 price action for the real, which weakened as the odds 
of a Lula win became insurmountable.
The real has gained 3.8% on the dollar so far this year (while the overall 
JPMorgan EM index is down 2%), even though Brazil’s central bank has bucked the 
global trend by cutting rates. The likelihood that the opposition will make 
gains in the legislature, and the growing chance that Lula will exit, have much 
to do with this.

The US currently levies an extra 25% tariff on Brazil 
<https://br.usembassy.gov/fact-sheet-president-trump-directs-ustr-section-301-action-in-response-to-brazils-unreasonable-acts-policies-and-practices/>
, and the current administration is far more likely to lift this if the country 
chooses a leader more to its liking (Washington’s financial aid to Argentina 
last year, contingent on victory for right-wing President Javier Milei’s party 
in the midterm elections, is a precedent). That would help Brazilian assets.

Beyond that, though, it’s hard to see that Bolsonaro has much space to do 
anything that wouldn’t be done under another Lula term. Either president would 
need to bring public finances and inflation under control, and Bolsonaro would 
probably pursue what Oxford Economics calls “a faster, more front-loaded 
adjustment” than Lula, centered on cutting spending and administrative jobs.

That would be popular with markets, but would risk further denting Brazilian 
consumption. And with Lula fighting for his political life, it might not go 
down well with voters. A strong and volatile market reaction to the result, 
whatever it is, seems to be the safest prediction.
