Who’s Afraid of Big Bad Bond Yields?

As the world braces for a rise in US overnight rates to 4%, and a possible 
decisive move in US 10-year yields above 5%, the response of the stock market — 
or rather the lack of it — speaks volumes.

So far this year, the S&P 500 has gained 10.7% (not including dividends), 
while FTSE’s index for the rest of the world is up 12.1%, in dollar terms. They 
are 3.0% and 3.75% down respectively from all-time highs set in the last month. 
This isn’t a banner year, but it’s nothing any sensible person could complain 
about.

This performance has happened despite an 80-basis-point rise in 10-year 
Treasury yields, and a massive swing in the pricing for the terminal rate of 
fed funds, expected to be reached next summer, to 4.56% from 3.09%. It’s also 
withstood anenergy price shock 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-09-14/easing-the-diesel-crunch-is-harder-than-keeping-planes-in-the-air?cmpid=BBD091626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8937>
 nobody saw coming, with the price of Brent crude that can be locked in for the 
end of this year rising 65%, from $60 at the outset to $99.22 now.

All these negative developments have indeed had an impact on earnings 
multiples that investors will pay for stocks, which are down significantly over 
the last 12 months. But share prices are holding up far better than in any 
previous episode when valuations declined on this scale:

The reason, in a word, is earnings. Forecast profits have gone through the 
roof this year, mainly but not entirely due to the extraordinary investments in 
artificial intelligence (which should create costs for some as well as revenues 
for others). Rolling 12-month forward earnings for the S&P 500, and 
particularly the tech sector, have leapt forward in unprecedented fashion:

If higher yields lead to shares trading at a lower multiple of those earnings, 
that’s something that can be lived with. The more alarming possibility is that 
higher yields could mean that profits don’t come through as forecast. If the 
Federal Reserve eventually tightens the screws enough to halt the AI buildout, 
that would change things, but it would need to hike several times. And in any 
case, if higher rates curtail the capital expenditures the big AI companies are 
making, large investors would be happy. September’s edition of theBank of 
America Global Fund Managers Survey 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/bofa-says-risk-of-disorderly-bond-move-trims-excess-bullishness?cmpid=BBD091626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8937>
 found record numbers of respondents arguing that companies were investing too 
much. This is a stark reversal after many years when companies were held not to 
be investing enough:

In further evidence that a rate hike would be tolerable, financial conditions, 
as measured by Bloomberg, are about as easy as they ever are. The scale in the 
chart below refers to standard deviations compared to the norm. Positive 
numbers mean conditions are looser than usual, while negative numbers — as seen 
dramatically during the Global Financial Crisis and the pandemic — show that 
money is tight. On this basis, it looks as though financial markets should have 
little or no problem dealing with one rate hike, so stocks’ ability to weather 
the storm so far shouldn’t be a surprise:

Yet more evidence from the survey that the expected rate hike shouldn’t be too 
much of a problem comes from the finding that a strong majority of fund 
managers think that current policy too stimulative. Bear this in mind once 
markets begin to react to Kevin Warsh’s press conference today after the 
Federal Open Market Committee meeting:

But if the market seems not to have a problem with higher rates, President 
Donald Trump evidently does. His adviserPeter Navarro piled on the pressure 
<https://x.com/NickTimiraos/status/2095971045249278353> last week:
If that Federal Reserve raises rates right now, a careless rate hike, it would 
hit precisely the sectors America needs most to prosper. Factory construction, 
capital investment, machinery, housing, manufacturing. And I just hope that 
those clowns over there that Kevin Warsh is trying to gather together and do 
the right thing, get the picture.
It’s hard to see how Warsh can avoid a hike and still maintain his credibility 
as Fed chairman, but he’d prefer to avoid the likely presidential savaging that 
would provoke. Jon Hilsenrath, a long-time Fed analyst for the Wall Street 
Journal and now a consultant to StoneX Financial, expressed the dilemma this 
way:
Warsh is being forced to choose between two demanding overlords: 1) The 
President of the United States, or 2) A global bond market that demands a 
reasonable inflation-adjusted return on its investment. My guess, and the 
consensus in futures markets, is that Warsh fears the bond market more. That 
probably won’t please the president, either.
This all makes for an interesting handicapping of Wednesday’s market reaction. 
The evidence is that the financial system can handle a hike without difficulty. 
The unknown variable is the guidance for the future, with the market currently 
pricing two more hikes after this month, and an 80% chance of a third.

The Fed’s quarterly “dot plot” of rate predictions, which Warsh dislikes, 
could help communicate whether this is the start of a true hiking cycle. But 
the greatest weight will hang on his press conference. That’s when Robin Brooks 
of the Brookings Institution predicts fireworks:
The press will ask Warsh — over and over — if he’ll hike as much as markets 
price. There’s no way he can answer this to the market’s satisfaction and so 
this is where the wheels come off.
With Trump breathing down his neck, Warsh will likely come over as dovish. 
That would be great for stocks — which don’t need the sugar of cheap rates but 
will lap it up if it’s on offer. But the 10-year yield closed Tuesday above 5%, 
and bond investors would be unimpressed by any suggestion that this is a “one 
and one” hike. If the Treasury market doesn’t like what it hears, that would 
take yields up into territory unseen since the crisis two decades ago. And that 
will be interesting.

Small caps’ storied comeback 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-08-21/why-small-cap-etfs-could-break-out-video?cmpid=BBD091626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260916&utm_content=8937>
 is not linear. Since its peak a month ago, the Russell 2000 has plunged more 
than 6%, dropping below its 100-day moving average for the first time since 
April. This has coincided with growing expectations of hikes by the Fed, and 
underscores small caps’ sensitivity to rates. It’s difficult to tell how much 
steam they have to let out in this increasingly hawkish environment, but their 
strong start to the year — outperforming the S&P 500 year-to-date — provides a 
crucial buffer against rate pressures:

The adverse impact from rising rate expectations makes sense, as leveraged 
small-cap firms tend to carry more short- and medium-term floating-rate debt 
than their large-cap counterparts. But the durability of other factors such as 
improved earnings, a revised tax policy that allows 100% depreciation on 
research and capex, and deregulation will stiffen their resilience.

Royce Investment Partners’ Francis Gannon argues 
<https://www.royceinvest.com/insights/semiannual-letter> that the tailwinds are 
strong enough to weather a Fed tightening cycle, and that this drawdown is 
noise rather than signal. He notes that “nobody is even talking about” a 10% 
correction earlier this year and calls the current setup a“pause that 
refreshes.” <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L2d2a4Fsmng>
The valuation story in small cap is much better than large. The earnings 
story, I think, is pretty powerful and a lot of that is not necessarily priced 
in the market — so there lies the opportunity.
Small caps continue to trade at near-historic low valuations. While the S&P 
500 price-to-book ratio has risen almost 20% in the last five years, the 
Russell 2000’s has fallen, supporting the core argument of a reversion to the 
mean:

Jonathan Coleman 
<https://www.janushenderson.com/en-us/advisor/article/the-catalysts-behind-small-cap-outperformance/>
 of Janus Henderson argues that small caps had become exceptionally cheap 
relative to large caps in the four years leading up to April 2025, when they 
traded among the cheapest 5% of relative valuation observations in decades of 
data. The gap has narrowed since then, but it hasn’t closed. On a forward 
price-to-earnings basis, the relative valuation still looks historically 
appealing:

This extreme valuation disconnect has set a reversal in motion, which should 
be helped by improving fundamentals (although an aggressive Fed tightening 
cycle could change that). Regarding how long this could last, Gannon notes that 
small caps’ leadership phases typically run about a decade. As we’re only 16-17 
months into this one, there’s a long way to go:
Source: Royce Investment Partners
Continuing negative sentiment among investors is a perverse reason for 
optimism; the BofA survey identifies long small caps as an appealing contrarian 
trade.

But many risks reman. Surging energy prices are likely to translate into a 
further tightening of financial conditions. There’s also a possibility of 
margin compression, especially given small caps’ disproportionate exposure to 
energy as an input cost. Diesel prices are at an all-time high, weighing on 
logistics-heavy companies. As Bloomberg Intelligence’s Salih Yilmaz notes, 
these companies can generally pass on the increased energy costs to customers, 
but usually with a lag of at least a quarter — meaning near-term pressure on 
profits is unavoidable when the oil price spikes.

Additionally, BI’s analysis shows that when oil prices remain above $100 for 
more than 30 consecutive days, the Russell 2000 has historically posted 
negative returns over both three- and 12-month horizons. That appears to be a 
possibility once more and would risk derailing the small-cap rally.

Ultimately, an AI productivity boost could cushion the impact and allow higher 
margins, amplified by small caps’ typically lower operating margin base. But 
none of this is a foregone conclusion; small caps remain a levered play on an 
uncertain situation.

—Richard Abbey
