Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Jess Shankleman looks at the growing overlap in Europe between 
technologies developed for energy and military use.

Europe’s rush to re-arm 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-08/how-putin-s-russia-is-spurring-europe-to-rebuild-armed-forces-for-a-long-war?cmpid=BBBXT100726_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9787>
 is driving an enormous boost in defense spending, but that influx of 
investment could also bolster energy security — and the energy transition.

An increasing number of manufacturers are straddling the energy, security and 
industrial markets, reflecting the growing overlap between technologies 
developed for civilian and military uses.

A report by the nonprofit Loom Strategy Centre identifies 100 such companies 
<https://loomstrategy.org/loom100/>, which produce a wide range of technologies 
from batteries and grid infrastructure to fuel cells. It says buyers will go to 
the best supplier for the job, regardless of the sector they’re in.

“If a defense startup in Germany wants to build a new type of drone, a new 
type of missile, they don’t go to an old defense company,” says Nico Lange, a 
former chief of staff at Germany’s Defense Ministry and now an adviser to Loom. 
“They go to a carmaker or an automotive supplier.”

The research is challenging the assumption that there’s no longer enough 
fiscal room to fund the energy transition and defense at the vast scale each 
requires. It’s all about directing support toward dual-use capabilities.

Public money is beginning to follow, with a number of financial institutions 
taking on a broader mandate. The European Investment Bank Group, for example, 
which has positioned itself as the region’s“climate bank,” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-01-09/plan-to-kickstart-eu-s-green-deal-comes-with-1-trillion-in-cash?cmpid=BBBXT100726_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261007&utm_content=9787>
 quadrupled itsdefense and security financing 
<https://www.eib.org/en/press/all/2026-204-eu-finance-ministers-back-eib-group-strategy-for-a-stronger-more-secure-europe-in-a-more-stable-world?utm_source=chatgpt.com>
 last year.

Yet European governments, and the manufacturers themselves, do not always 
recognize and communicate the cross-sector value of key products, making 
efforts to scale up difficult.

Illustrating the obstacles to such buildouts, the Global Deep Tech Report 2026 
<https://www.munich-startup.de/en/news/europe-builds-45-percent-of-deeptech-startups-why-is-capital-flowing-elsewhere>
 says Europe accounts for 45% of “deep-tech” startups around the world, but 
only 17% of global funding flows into these companies.

If countries fail to leverage their strategic capability and provide more 
pathways from startup to industrial scale, they risk pushing essential 
manufacturing expertise elsewhere.

—Jess Shankleman, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

One of the fastest ways to power a US data center is getting slower, according 
toBloombergNEF <https://about.bnef.com/>. US buyers ordered 8.2 gigawatts of 
small- and medium-frame gas turbines in the first half of 2026, almost 15 times

is already shrinking, and no turbine type can now be delivered in less than a 
year.
