Food Prices

Anyone in the US who went shopping for the traditional Thanksgiving dinner 
over the weekend has likely noticed that food inflation is picking up again.

Economists say a jump in grocery prices in October government data was a taste 
ofthings to come 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-24/thanksgiving-dinner-costs-more-this-year-with-grocery-prices-rising?cmpid=BBD112526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11822>
. Real-time alternative data from the Bloomberg Price Index supports this, 
particularly for fresh holiday menu staples.

Compared with last year, red potato prices are up nearly 30%, for instance, 
while sweet potatoes and sweet corn rose more than 9%.

On a global scale, the worst fears of severe food crises and famines triggered 
by the US-Iran warhave not materialized 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-11-17/food-prices-are-up-but-forecasts-for-a-global-food-crisis-are-wrong?cmpid=BBD112526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11822>
. Despite extreme weather and rainfall linked to the El Niño phenomenon, food 
inflation remains relatively contained in many parts of the world, though the 
UN’s global index shows prices climbing at a pace close to 6%, the highest in 
more than a year.

But pressures 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-20/jpmorgan-warns-long-tail-of-el-nino-will-hurt-crops-water-supplies?cmpid=BBD112526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11822>
 from the energy shock and the bad weather are still making their way through 
supply chains. Tariffs also continue to contribute to food inflation, affecting 
US imports like seafood and materials used in packaging, says Ricky Volpe, an 
agribusiness professor at California Polytechnic State University.

Beyond groceries 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/thanksgiving-travel-slips-on-high-fuel-prices-aaa-says?cmpid=BBD112526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11822>
, the October consumer-price index showed the cost of essentials climbingacross 
the board 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-10/us-core-cpi-rises-more-than-forecast-0-3-supporting-rate-hike?cmpid=BBD112526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11822>
. On top of that, recent technology outages that disrupted US air traffic and 
banking led to spikes in the price of emergency items that consumers are
panic-buying 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-24/prices-of-bunker-essentials-spike-as-outages-spook-us-consumers?cmpid=BBD112526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11822>
 to prepare for the worst (think generators and, of course, toilet paper).

Unlike in France and parts of Europe, Americans aren’t taking to the streets 
to protest over the high cost of living.

Instead, voters expressed their frustration at the ballot box during this 
month’s midterm elections. But Democrats whoretook both chambers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-04/inflation-backlash-trump-fatigue-powers-huge-wins-for-democrats?cmpid=BBD112526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11822>

Need-to-Know Research

Coming up on nine months since the US-Israeli war against Iran erupted, the 
global economy is adapting to one of the biggest energy disruptions in modern 
history, according to Bloomberg Economics.

“Middle Eastern producers are getting their oil out, others are pumping more, 
and consumers are cutting usage,” BE’sZiad Daoud 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ATZXAs5qQ_I/ziad-daoud?cmpid=BBD112526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11822>
 wrote this week. The Middle East is exporting 75% to 86% of pre-war levels, 
thanks to maneuvers including possiblepayments to Iran 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-01/strait-of-hormuz-ships-paying-iran-yuan-and-crypto-tolls-for-safe-passage?cmpid=BBD112526_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261125&utm_content=11822>
. Meantime, International Energy Agency estimates show that the US, Canada, 
Brazil, Argentina and Guyana have added 1.6 million barrels a day. And 
consumers have cut demand by 3.7 million barrels, according to its calculations.

“Some of that loss is temporary, but other shifts could last,” Daoud wrote. 
“Before the Iran war, the world was already heading for an oil surplus,” he 
said. Now, “supply is exceeding demand again. For the first time since March, a 
slump in prices looks as plausible as a spike.”
