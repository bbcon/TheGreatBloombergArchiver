A ‘Perplexing’ Warsh Briefing

Last year, when he was discussing the Fed’s 2024 rate cuts, Kevin Warsh 
highlighted how they were received poorly in financial markets. Long-term 
yields went up, not down, hesaid 
<https://www.linkedin.com/posts/hoover-distinguished-visiting-fellow-kevin-ugcPost-7355751342901972994-4ifd/>
. “That’s a Bronx cheer — that’s markets and the business community saying you 
made a mistake.”

On Wednesday, Warsh, now Fed chairman, arguably got his own sample of a Bronx 
cheer (similar to blowing a raspberry, it’s a derisive noise associated with 
hard-edged New York baseball fans.) He led a majority of his colleagues in 
keeping interest rates unchanged, and markets decided they didn’t like it.

Longer-term Treasuries tumbled, with 30-year yields jumping to the highest in 
19 years 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-29/treasuries-jolted-as-fed-hold-trims-september-hike-bets?cmpid=BBD073026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7097>
. Stocks slid, with the S&P 500 closing down 1.5%. Those are thekinds of 
reactions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/fed-doubts-push-some-funds-to-eye-overseas-bets-over-treasuries?cmpid=BBD073026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7097>
 one might expect from a hawkish surprise by the Fed. Not a day when 
policymakers optedagainst raising rates.

Trying to explain the move, Fed watchers and market participants highlighted a 
disconnect between Warsh’s rhetoric and his actions. He emphasized that 
policymakers are determined to achieve their 2% inflation target. He alluded to 
“impatience” among households and businesses to get price gains down. “Where 
necessary and appropriate, we will not hesitate to act,” he declared.

After talking tough 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-07-15/kevin-warsh-talks-the-talk-on-inflation-while-leaving-the-walk-in-question?cmpid=BBD073026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7097>
 on inflation, Warsh was asked to explain why he wasn’t convinced by arguments 
put forward by the three dissenting members of the Federal Open Market 
Committee who wanted to hike. He didn’t offer a direct response. When the 
reporter pressed him, asking what role the soft June inflation report played, 
he said, “not much.”
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-07-29/warsh-says-fed-won-t-be-constrained-by-market-prices-video?cmpid=BBD073026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7097>
It was “ultimately a perplexing press conference,” said Robert Sockin, chief 
US economist at asset manager PGIM.

Since before taking the Fed’s helm, Warsh has viewed rhetoric as something of 
a policy tool in itself, explaining in a 2024 discussion, “Thehawkier you are  
<https://vimeo.com/952145190>— the more determined that you think that ‘this 
central bank is going to quash inflation,’ — you don’t have to have rates as 
high for so long.”

He’s been sounding hawkish 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-24/bessent-signals-confidence-in-warsh-sees-inflation-coming-down?cmpid=BBD073026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7097>
 from the get-go as chair. But now he’s kept rates unchanged for a second time 
without detailing why that was the right move.

“Warsh did not help himself today,” said Stephanie Roth, chief economist at 
Wolfe Research. “His communications style appears to be backfiring and the 
market is calling his bluff.”

Economic data, rather than markets, will be the ultimate judge, however. 
Policymakers will have two more months of data by the time they gather again in 
September. In retrospect, Warsh’s performance Wednesday could either look like 
prescient pushback against calls to hike — or a missed opportunity to get ahead 
of the inflation-fighting curve.

Anna Wong 
<https://bloomberg.com/authors/AVZEA8jmBvs/anna-wong?cmpid=BBD073026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7097>
, chief US economist at Bloomberg Economics, is betting on the former. 
“Conditioned on our forecast that the next two inflation and jobs prints will

Need-to-Know Research

The Middle East is an increasingly important trade and investment partner for 
Africa, and theIran war 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/audio/2026-07-30/odd-lots-how-the-iranian-economy-actually-works-podcast?cmpid=BBD073026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7097>
 isn’t likely to change that picture, according to Razia Khan, head of Africa 
and Middle East research at Standard Chartered and her colleague Frederick 
Foster. The “drivers are long-term and structural.”

While Africa’s exports are still skewed towards Europe and China, the Middle 
East is rising rapidly as a trading partner for the continent, Khan and Foster 
wrote in a note earlier this week.Gold 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/central-banks-bought-far-less-gold-than-thought-at-start-of-year?cmpid=BBD073026_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260730&utm_content=7097>
 and other commodities exports are rising from Africa, and the continent is 
increasingly reliant on the Middle East for fertilizer and energy imports, they 
said.

“Deepening trade ties between the Middle East and Africa are increasingly 
matched by FDI pledges, as well as actual flows” in foreign direct investment, 
Standard Chartered analysis shows. The United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Turkey and 
Saudi Arabia are the most active in the Africa investment corridor, Khan and 
Foster wrote. “We see continued economic integration across trade, FDI and 
capital flows over the coming decade.”
