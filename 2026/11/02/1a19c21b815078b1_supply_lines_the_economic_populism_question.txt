If the polls are right, this week’s US midterm elections are about to strike a 
blow 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-01/trump-banks-on-rallies-to-turn-out-maga-faithful-one-more-time?cmpid=BBD110226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10742>
 to President Donald Trump and his Republican party. That’s a big if, of 
course. But if the results track expectations then Trump won’t be the only one 
suffering a defeat. His brand of economic populism will be a loser too.

As we’ve written 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-10-26/trump-s-tariff-salesman-and-election-politics-of-trade?srnd=undefined&cmpid=BBD110226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10742>
 before in this newsletter, Trump’s tariffs have become linked to the angst 
about prices and affordability in the electorate ahead of this week’s election 
and become politically toxic. Which also fits a pattern in US political 
history, as Iflagged 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-16/iran-war-will-be-eclipsed-by-trump-s-tariffs-come-midterms?cmpid=BBD110226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10742>
 in Bloomberg Businessweek back in March: “As any student of American history 
could tell you, tariffs can be politically deadly,” I wrote then.

The story that’s developing doesn’t end with tariffs, though. The core tenet 
of economic populism is that it’s supposed to be popular. The basic idea is to 
ignore the advice of orthodox economists to curry favor with the wider public. 
And the key problem Trump and Republicans face is that Trumponomics is turning 
out not to be popular at all.

Podcast: Is Trump About to Lose Farm Country for the Republicans? 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-08/podcast-is-trump-about-to-lose-farm-country-for-the-republicans?cmpid=BBD110226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10742>

Part of that is the context and a story of the US economy that should by now 
be familiar. Yes, there’s an AI boom fueling growth. And yet the Iran war and 
tariffs have brought inflation back to life, eroding real wages and causing the 
Federal Reserve to start raising interest rates again. All as the broader US 
labor market is stuck in neutral. My colleagues Reade Pickert, Jeffrey 
Sparshott and Julia Fanzeres do a good job of diving into all that in a short 
Bloomberg Originalsdocumentary 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-29/why-the-us-economy-feels-worse-than-it-looks-video?srnd=homepage-americas&cmpid=BBD110226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10742>
 published last week.

Affordability is what people often seize on. But the angst is really broader 
than that.

Here are three facts I find striking:

 * There are now 1.2 million fewer men 
<https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/LNS12000001> working in America than there 
were when Trump returned to office in January 2025. Which matters given that 
Trump’s core constituency has been men.
 * So far this year the US has added 72,000 manufacturing jobs 
<https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MANEMP>. And yet in that same period it has 
lost 23,000 nonsupervisoryproduction workers 
<https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CES3000000006>, those actually on the 
factory floor. Which matters if one of the main goals of Trump’s economic 
populism has been restoring factory jobs and the core constituency is supposed 
to be the workers rather than the managers.
 * There are 27,900 fewer people working in factories in Michigan 
<https://fred.stlouisfed.org/graph/?g=pH9M> than there were the month Trump was 
first elected in November 2016. Which matters in one of the states that Trump 
has from the very beginning identified as a potential beneficiary of his 
populism.
There are plausible reasons for all of those things. And yet having to explain 
away facts isn’t really the populist way. Which gets to Trump’s brand.

In a new podcast <https://eig.org/newbazaar/is-economic-populism-cooked/> 
Cardiff Garcia of the Economic Innovation Group sits down with pollster Kristen 
Soltis Anderson to discuss arecent survey 
<https://eig.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/EIG-State-of-the-American-Worker-2026.pdf>
 of American workers taken for the Washington-based think tank.

Equity Stakes

The bottom line is that the findings were not good for Trump. As in other 
polls, the president rates poorly on the economy. His tariffs are not only 
unpopular but associated with higher prices. Those surveyed also take a dim 
view of other ostensibly populist policies Trump has pursued such as having the 
government takeequity stakes 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-05/us-exploring-government-partnerships-with-ai-firms-trump-says?cmpid=BBD110226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10742>
 in US companies.

Which is why the podcast episode has a provocative title: “Is Economic 
Populism Cooked?”
 
<https://www.cato.org/blog/trump-equity-portfolio-hits-40-democrats-have-plans-it>
The Cato Institute’s tally of US government equity stakes under Trump.
The answer to that broader question is probably no. There have been plenty of 
signs in the leadup to Tuesday’s election of candidates seizing on other 
populist targets like AI and data centers. A majority of workers polled for the 
EIG survey said they were more likely to vote for candidates in this week’s 
election who were ready to fight health insurance firms, big banks and AI 
companies.

Explainer: How a Blue Wave Could Complicate Trump’s Presidency 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-01/2026-midterm-elections-what-a-democratic-win-would-mean-for-trump?cmpid=BBD110226_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261102&utm_content=10742>

It’s still too early to write off Trump’s brand of economic populism. But even 
if Republicans stage a major upset this week and retain control of Congress, 
it’s unlikely to be because his brand of economics is popular or that it’s 
delivering the promised results. That’s something candidates eyeing the 2028 
election are likely already noticing.
