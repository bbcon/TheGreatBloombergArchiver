Earnings Season Is Almost Here

A moment of truth is at hand. To summarize what this newsletter has been 
saying more or less every day for a month, bond yields are rising fast, but 
it’s not hurting the stock market because earnings are genuinely impressive. 
Providing growth surges on, we’re fine. If not...

The concern, of course, is what happens if it falters. We’re about to get our 
next bulletin, when the third-quarter earnings season gets going on Tuesday 
with the big banks reporting. The second quarter saw record year-on-year growth 
of 33%, unprecedented except for periods when companies were recovering from 
outright declines.

Earnings growth tends to run in cycles, but what’s happening at present sticks 
out. This is the rolling annual change in trailing 12-month earnings per share:

This doesn’t happen away from big rebounds. If we look at the performance of 
each quarter compared to the equivalent period of the previous year, as 
measured by Bloomberg’s Graph Fundamentals function, this latest boom is even 
more pronounced:

And yet the expectation (almost certainly correct) is that this will continue 
in the third quarter. This is from Deutsche Bank AG’s Bankim Chadha:
We see earnings growth sustaining at record levels in Q3 (34%). The consensus 
(27%), in contrast, expects a broad deceleration, setting up for another 
quarter of above-average beats.
Ben Snider of Goldman Sachs also expects that most companies will beat 
expectations (as is almost always the case given investor relations 
departments’ skills in setting a bar that they can beat). As Points of Return 
has said frequently, this is centered on a few companies:
Analyst estimates show the beneficiaries of AI infrastructure spending 
accounting for over 50% of S&P 500 EPS growth this quarter alongside 
hyperscaler capex growth of 116%.
BCA Research offers this chart of growth in trailing 12-month EPS by sector to 
demonstrate just how much is owed to AI. This is particularly true outside the 
US:

The rise in energy prices, which has an immediate positive impact on oil 
companies’ profits and a slower and more diffuse negative drag on everyone 
else, has also helped make this cycle look so good.

So, AI can explain why this time is so different. ChatGPT arrived eight months 
into a Federal Reserve tightening cycle and thwarted what would otherwise 
likely have been a profits recession. Since then, as we all know, the gains 
have been remarkable. But even if we acknowledge that much is riding on AI, are 
things as good as they seem?

The equity strategy team at Societe Generale SA is cautious for several 
reasons. First, amid growing concentration, market-cap weighting can “distort 
the signal” - when companies with higher growth rates outperform, their 
earnings forecasts make up a greater share of the total. Second, there are base 
effects after major downturns that can exaggerate growth.

Further, there is a problem with short-termism. Analysts generally have 
long-term capital market assumptions, but don’t publish five-year earnings 
forecasts, or submit them to aggregators. According to SocGen, there are no 
such predictions for 150 STOXX 600 constituents, so there’s no true consensus.
High long-term EPS growth expectations have historically been a warning 
signal. STOXX 600 performance shows an inverse relationship between long-term 
EPS growth expectations and subsequent 12-month returns. Low expectations, 
often seen during crises, tend to precede strong returns, while high 
expectations tend to precede negative returns. This suggests the series behaves 
more like a contrarian indicator than a reliable earnings signal.
For all the angst over the risks involved in AI, the innate tendency to 
extrapolate its returns to date into the future remains.

A broader issue becomes clearer if we look at the longer term. Throughout the 
20th century, profit margins were regarded as one of the purest mean-reverting 
series in the economy. Dragged by the opposing forces of labor and capital, 
they would oscillate. So far this century, however, margins have done nothing 
but rise:

Measured alternatively with the National Income and Product Accounts, part of 
the GDP calculation, profits are currently taking a bigger share of the economy 
even than on the eve of the Great Crash in 1929:

The series comes with a lag, so we can assume the profit share on this basis 
is comfortably at a record by now. It’s prudent to ask whether this can 
possibly be sustained. Corporate earnings management being what it is, big 
growth is guaranteed, but with so much riding on the third-quarter numbers, 
they might not be enough to keep the stock rally going.

Consumer Paradox

Barely a month to the US midterms, and affordability is as much of a political 
flashpoint as ever. There are plenty of justifications for this: Diesel and 
gasoline prices are up by at least 50% this year and the 30-year mortgage rate 
has surged to 7.38%, the highest since President Donald Trump returned to 
office. The Fed’s resumption of rate hikes underscores the sense of urgency. At 
least in the short term, consumers seem convinced inflation will get worse 
before it gets better. The New York Fed’s latest survey ofone-year inflation 
expectations 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/one-year-inflation-expectations-reach-three-year-high-ny-fed?cmpid=BBD100826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9835>
 raced to 3.9%, a three-year high:

Estimates for three years ahead remain relatively calm, but the shorter-term 
numbers still suggest the kind of inflationary psychology that a central bank 
cannot ignore. Pessimism also shows up in the recent Conference Board Consumer 
Confidence, which plunged to a 12-year low amid rising fuel prices, higher 
borrowing costs, and hiring concerns.

Actual consumer spending activity tells a different story. The most recent 
retail sales data 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-16/us-retail-sales-rebound-more-than-expected-in-broad-advance?cmpid=BBD100826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9835>
 jumped the most since March and remains higher than at the start of the year:

The New York Fed survey partly solves that riddle, with respondents seeing a 
lower probability of losing their jobs and higher odds of voluntarily quitting. 
Does the widening divergence between consumer spending and sentiment signal 
anything sinister? Oxford Economics’ chief US economist Michael Pearce argues 
that broad measures may be obscuring that the households driving much of the 
expenditure are more confident:
About 40% of consumer spending is done by 20% of households, but the consumer 
sentiment data you’re looking at is kind of population-weighted rather than 
spending-weighted. The higher the income, the better confidence is holding up .
Bank of America’s Shruti Mishra says the bank’s proprietary data show spending 
is growing less “K-shaped 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-04-07/the-k-shaped-economy-s-defining-statistic-has-some-problems?cmpid=BBD100826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261008&utm_content=9835>
.” After the 2024 election, higher-income households outperformed as wealth 
gains accrued mostly to them, while people on lower incomes faced higher 
housing, food, energy and borrowing costs. That gap has narrowed meaningfully 
over the last few months:

Further, the S&P 500 sub-indexes of consumer staples and discretionary stocks 
have held up this year. Discretionary shares have trimmed their first-quarter 
losses while consumer staples are up by 6%:

Consumers aren’t out of the woods. Around 42% of the New York Fed’s 
respondents said their situation was worse than a year ago, against 18% saying 
it had improved. Mishra warns that higher energy costs could reverse the recent 
convergence in spending growth and eventually pass through to food inflation.

Wage growth remains anemic, particularly for the lower paid. According to the 
Atlanta Fed’s wage tracker, people in the bottom quartile (the lowest paid 25% 
of the population) are getting lower wage rises than those in the top quartile, 
and have been for almost two years - an unusual situation:

As the midterm elections approach, US consumers seem ever more fixated on 
affordability. That’s understandable.

— Richard Abbey

Perception, Reality and Inflation

The minutes for last month’s Federal Open Market Committee meeting, which 
voted unanimously to raise rates, are now with us. It’s not surprising they 
reveal little disagreement. But Oxford Economics’ regular diffusion index based 
on words in the report suggests that the central bankers now see risks more 
skewed toward inflation (and lower unemployment) than at any time since the 
study was initiated in 2008. As inflation was almost three times the current 
level back in 2022, that’s surprising:

Wall Street’s panic about imminent rate hikes is dimming, but one more rise 
this year is still deemed almost a certainty (96.4%, according to Bloomberg 
World Interest Rate Probability function). The minutes refrain from naming 
names or giving precise numbers, but suggest that that’s right. The key 
passages are:
“A number of participants viewed a higher path for the target range as 
necessary based on their modal outlooks rather than on risk-management grounds.”
“Several participants stated that they viewed the current policy rate as not 
restrictive or only mildly restrictive.“Most participants assessed that another 
increase in the target range for the federal funds rate would likely be 
appropriate by year end.”
There’s almost certainly another hike to come this year. After that, the Fed 
wants some wiggle room.

Survival Tips

Shortly after you receive this, the Nobel prize for literature 
<https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/lists/all-nobel-prizes-in-literature/> will 
be announced. It’s an impossibly difficult award to judge, as it tries to cover 
all languages and must contend with changes in critical sentiment and popular 
taste. It excludes genre fiction (no Agatha Christie or Raymond Chandler), but 
often goes to people who’ve never written a novel (Bertrand Russell, Winston 
Churchill andBob Dylan <https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poets/bob-dylan> have 
little in common beyond their Nobel literature prizes). Here’s my list of the 
top 20 writers who have been overlooked since the prize began in 1904 (they 
aren’t given posthumously): Anthony Burgess, Anton Chekhov, Joseph Conrad, F. 
Scott Fitzgerald, E.M. Forster, Graham Greene, Thomas Hardy, Henry James, James 
Joyce, D.H. Lawrence, Iris Murdoch, Vladimir Nabokov, George Orwell, Sylvia 
Plath, Marcel Proust, J.D. Salinger, Leo Tolstoy, Mark Twain, Evelyn Waugh, and 
Virginia Woolf. That’s just the ones who’ve passed away and can never be 
honored. Who have I missed and who most deserves to be honored now while 
they’re still alive? Nominations welcome.
