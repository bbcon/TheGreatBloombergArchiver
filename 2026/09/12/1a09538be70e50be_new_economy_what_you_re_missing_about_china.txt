This newsletter has regularly benefited from the analysis aired on the 
ChinaTalk podcast <https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/chinatalk/id1289062927>
, a show that examines the Asian nation’s technological rise and, more broadly, 
its geopolitical rivalry with the US. For this week, I reached out directly to 
ChinaTalk founder and host Jordan Schneider to get his individual perspective 
on these issues, some of the most pivotal of our time.

Schneider, 36, kicked off the ChinaTalk project almost a decade ago as a kind 
of educational tool while pursuing a master’s degree at Peking University’s 
Yenching Academy. “I thought it would be more likely that the people who wrote 
books I liked would talk to me if I said I was inviting them on a show,” he 
says. Writing an accompanying ChinaTalk newsletter also served as “a forcing 
function for me to do Chinese homework.”

Shortly after Schneider’s arrival in Beijing, President Xi Jinping removed 
term limits for China’s leadership, part of his broaderconsolidation of power 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2023-03-04/china-puts-private-enterprise-on-notice-as-congress-begins-new-economy-saturday?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8724>
 within the Communist Party and the state. “It was a bit of a scene-setter for 
maybe the direction this all was going to go,” Schneider says. As for the tech 
angle of his work, that came in part from stints at Didi Global Inc., China’s 
largest ride-hailing platform, and Kuaishou Technology.
Jordan SchneiderPhotographer: Martin Bentsen
“When I was living in China, the most exciting jobs for foreigners were 
working at Chinese technology companies on their international expansion 
teams,” he says. Looking at the landscape today, where the US and China are 
locked in a battle for tech supremacy and each government has taken steps to 
raise barriers against the other, Schneider says his primary reflection is 
sorrow.

“Having stared at this very closely, it’s sad. I think that’s the kind of 
emotional note which is missing from a lot of this coverage,” he says. A whole 
generation of Chinese technologists and founders who had little interest in 
politics have effectively had toresign themselves 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-02-14/xi-to-meet-with-business-leaders-such-as-jack-ma-reuters-says?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8724>
 to being strapped to Xi’s machinations. They’re not driven by “keeping the 
party strong and having China bully its neighbors, but the system is such that” 
many are pushed into loyalty, he says.

As to China’s arc of expanding its influence at America’s expense, Schneider 
offers some sanguine observations, as detailed below.

Schneider says he fundamentally disagrees with those who argue that Xi’s 
autocracy and prioritization of national security will hobble China’sinnovation 
and entrepreneurial growth 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2024-12-14/china-s-autocracy-2-0-may-soon-start-to-wane-bloomberg-new-economy?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8724>
.

“If you are a Chinese founder, there is some sort of discount factor and fear 
that, yeah, you could end up being thrown in jail or your industry, which once 
got a green light,now gets a red light 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2021-06-09/why-china-s-cracking-down-now-on-education-tech-firms-quicktake?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8724>
,” he acknowledges. “That’s a discount factor.” But it doesn’t mean people give 
up on aiming to succeed in science, engineering and business. “This is not the 
Soviet Union in 1964,” he says.

Schneider draws on an analogy from 17th century China, when Manchus from 
beyond the Ming Dynasty’s borders crossed the Great Wall, seized control and 
set up the Qing Empire. Some of the old dynasty’s bureaucrats opted to “turn 
into a monk, sit on a mountain and paint all day,” Schneider says. But most 
decided to stay on, perhaps thinking, “I’m interested in bureaucrat-ing. That’s 
my passion. And maybe I can teach these Qing barbarians a thing or two.”

Schneider also singles out China’s university system for “really upping its 
game 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-10-27/bankers-replaced-by-tech-phds-as-xi-sparks-new-fund-talent-war?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8724>
” over time. “There was a lot of structural pessimism about fake papers and the 
ability of China to educate a new generation that could do science and research 
on par with the rest of the world,” he says. That’s changed to where today, the 
talent emerging is capable of leveraging “the promise of the scale that the 
Chinese market has into building things that the world has never seen before.”
Booster T2 robots perform in formation during the opening of the World

Schneider describes a cohort of technologists who, in college and graduate 
school, talk of wishing the internet were freer and being annoyed with the 
Communist Party, but go on to recognize the environment they’re operating in. 
Once they enter the working world and their businesses “grow big enough where 
they become part of the state’spush to rejuvenate 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-08/pentagon-accuses-alibaba-baidu-byd-of-aiding-china-s-military?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8724>
 its technology and science power, all that stuff goes away.”

Despite Xi’s moves to harness China’s massive industrial capacity and rapidly 
advancing technological capabilities to strengthen the nation’s military, 
Schneider says he’s not unduly concerned about the position of the US and, more 
broadly, the West. He says the argument that the US must avoid a war with China 
because China is guaranteed to win is “a little presumptive.”

“Yes, there are obvious scale advantages that China has,” says Schneider, 
whose podcast regularly features geostrategic discussions (in June, hespoke 
with Paul Kennedy 
<https://www.chinatalk.media/p/paul-kennedy-on-great-powers-past>, author of 
Rise and Fall of the Great Powers.) “But let’s remember that the only two 
allies that China has are North Korea and the Russians.” The US, by contrast, 
has many treaty allies that also “have a strong interest in preserving the 
status quo.” Washington, “in most futures, will have more friends than the 
Chinese when it comes to any military conflict.”

While China has steadily asserted its position in the Western Pacific, from 
the South China Sea to the waterseast of Taiwan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-06/china-piles-pressure-on-taiwan-with-fresh-patrols-off-east-coast?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8724>
 and even, with a recentmissile test 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/pacific-leaders-criticize-china-s-recent-ballistic-missile-test?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260912&utm_content=8724>
, in the Pacificnortheast of Australia 
<https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/06/china-missile-test-south-pacific>
, Schneider is sanguine about Beijing respecting the territorial integrity of 
boundaries that have been in place since the 1940s and ’50s.

“My meta point is, I think there there is a hierarchy of needs when it comes 
to what Xi cares about,” he says. “There is a sort of threshold and level of 
risk that Xi has told the PLA to to take on, and a threshold which Xi told the 
PLA to not go over,” he says, referring to the People’s Liberation Army.

“We’ve had a status quo which has worked really well for China on a lot of 
different dimensions,” he says. “I got the ‘under’ on Polymarket forthe Taiwan 
invasion
