How to be optimistic at COP

By Akshat Rathi 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUdOobfNzkQ/akshat-rathi?cmpid=BBD111026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11221>

This will be my sixth COP climate summit. Every year, attendees lament that 
progress on tackling climate change has beenachingly slow 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-09/the-world-will-be-exceptionally-hot-in-coming-months-wmo-says?cmpid=BBD111026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11221>
, and many say the global confabs have failed to deliver any real progress at 
all.

This year, even with inflation, debt and wars topping the global political 
agenda, I have a reason to be optimistic: electrification.
Attendees in a crowded hall at the COP31 climate conference in Antalya, 
Turkey, on Tuesday, Nov. 10, 2026.Photographer: Kerem Uzel/Bloomberg
Not specifically solar or wind power, but simply converting anything you can 
to electricity, be it from coal, gas, hydro, nuclear or — preferably, of course 
— sunshine or blustery days. It’s why COP31’s Turkish hosts are seeking a 
global pledge to increase the global share of energy consumed coming from 
electricity to35% by 2035 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/in-a-divided-world-cop31-aims-for-agreement-on-electrification?cmpid=BBD111026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11221>
, versus just under a quarter today.

“In almost all circumstances, electrification is a good thing,” says Chris 
Bowen, Australia’s minister of climate change and energy and COP31’s president 
of negotiations.

One issue that comes up every time is the conflict between wealthy 
industrialized countries and the rest of the world. The poor feel justifiably 
cheated that rich nations achieved economic dominance by torching more than a 
trillion tons of fossil fuels over the past 200 years, and now they’re asking 
everyone else to burn less.

So here’s some good news: Increasing the share of energy coming from 
electricity is a smart economic bet for both the rich and the poor, and it 
helps lower emissions across the board. The idea is that running whatever you 
can on electricity — particularly transportation, home heating and factories — 
is more efficient than directly burning fossil fuels for a given application.

And if you can shut down your coal or gas plant someday in the future in favor 
of renewables, you’ll ratchet your emissions down to almost zero.

Researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology say driving an 
electric car in the US, where most power is generated by gas, results in40% to 
60% less carbon emissions 
<https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ae0c23> than using an 
equivalent gasoline vehicle. That’s because electric motors convert 90% of the 
energy they consume into motion, whereas an internal combustion engine converts 
less than 30%. Or, as BloombergNEF’s Colin McKerracher once told me, most 
traditional cars are “justsound and fury 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-11-17/primary-energy-vs-final-energy-why-replacing-fossil-fuels-won-t-be-so-hard?cmpid=BBD111026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11221>
, signifying nothing.”

Similarly, heat pumps cause about half as much carbon 
<https://janrosenow.substack.com/p/heat-pumps-and-evs-cut-emissions> output as 
natural gas boilers to achieve the same level of comfort, even after accounting 
for energy losses at a gas-fired power plant and moving the electricity long 
distances through cables. “You have these huge efficiency advantages of 
electrification,” says Jan Rosenow, an Oxford climate policy professor who 
co-authored the study.
A heat pump being installed at a house in Reading, UK.Photographer: Carlotta 
Cardana / Bloomberg
Of course, as prices for EVs and heat pumps keep falling, cars and homes are 
the low-hanging fruit. But today’s elevated prices for diesel and natural gas 
are opening up the next frontier for electrification. Sales ofheavy duty trucks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-09-29/eighteen-wheeler-e-trucks-are-china-s-answer-to-soaring-global-diesel-prices?cmpid=BBD111026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11221>
, for instance, are growing as batteries become cheaper and lighter. And 
industrial production ofchemicals and foods 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-10-31/europe-s-fossil-fuel-problem-has-an-electric-solution?cmpid=BBD111026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11221>
, which are made with relatively low levels of heat, can now be done with 
electric equipment.

Harder will be cement and steel (they require extremely high levels of heat) 
and shipping and aviation, where the energy density of today’s batteries (the 
power per pound) is a tiny fraction of liquid fuels’.

And here’s some more good news (tucked into some really bad news): With the 
wars in Ukraine and the Middle East seriously crimping global flows of oil and 
gas (admit it: had you heard of the Strait of Hormuz before this February?) 
policymakers areaccelerating the shift to renewables 
<https://www.iea.org/data-and-statistics/data-tools/2026-energy-crisis-policy-response-tracker?tab=Structural+policies>
 as they seek to cut their reliance on fossil fuel imports.

The Philippines has introduced incentives for solar installations. Bangladesh 
and Cambodia have cut tariffs on electric vehicles. And at least a half-dozen 
European countries have added new funding for heat pumps.

“The crisis in energy markets is a product of a broader geopolitical crisis,” 
says Kristian Ruby, secretary-general of industry group Eurelectric. 
“Electricity is our way of insulating our economies against the roller coaster 
prices of fossil fuels, against wars in geographies we don’t control.”

Iran’s COP31 message

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD111026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11221>

Iran is harnessing international climate talks in Turkey to highlight the 
impact of a deadly US missile strike on an elementary school, using an 
exhibition space typically focused on global warming to depict the incident.

The country’s national pavilion inside the COP summit shows images of 
schoolchildren killed in the southern city of Minab and includes a mock 
classroom with strewn backpacks, desks and a chalkboard intended to look like 
the aftermath of the missile attack.
Iran’s national pavilion at the COP31 climate talks in Antalya, Turkey, on 
Nov. 9, 2026.Photographer: Jennifer A. Dlouhy/Bloomberg
Visitors who don a virtual reality headset can also watch a four-minute film 
that depicts a fictional boy’s school day before the site was struck, and 
portrays US President Donald Trump as directly ordering the Feb. 28 attack.

An investigation into the incident is ongoing, and the US does not target 
civilians, said a senior Trump administration official, who asked for anonymity 
because the inquiry isn’t public. The US government doesn’t have an official 
delegation at COP31 following the country’s withdrawal from the Paris Agreement.
Iran’s pavilion at the COP31 climate talks in Antalya, Turkey, on Nov. 9, 2026.
Photographer: Jennifer A. Dlouhy/Bloomberg
Top administration officials have denied intentionally targeting the school. 
Pentagon officials discovered that flawed intelligence, outdated imagery and an 
overreliance on AI contributed to the attack, according to a Bloomberg News
investigation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-iran-school-attack/?cmpid=BBD111026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11221>
.

More than 120 children were killed in the strike at the school, which was 
previously part of an Iranian military facility — one of 1,000 sites hit during 
the initial February barrage.

Hot off a victory in Brazil’s election, President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva is 
looking for another win, tacking the elephant in the room at recent climate 
talks: transitioning away from fossil fuels.

Last year, as Brazil hosted COP30, at least 80 countries backed development of 
a roadmap to implement an earlier global pledge to shift away from polluting 
fuels – the primary cause of global warming. That wasn’t enough to secure 
support in the summit’s final decision amid heavy opposition from petrostates 
and the need for consensus in the UN process.

Read More: Brazil Puts Forward Plan to Wean World Off Fossil Fuels 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-03/brazil-puts-forward-global-plan-to-wean-world-off-fossil-fuels?cmpid=BBD111026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11221>

After outlining a detailed new plan for roadmaps last week, Brazil presented 
the proposals again on Tuesday in front of a packed conference plenary hall, 
alongside previous COP presidents, former US climate envoy John Kerry and 
outgoing UN Secretary General Antonio Guterres.

Guterres touted a windfall tax on energy companies to help fund those efforts, 
and won a standing ovation from delegates.

“They must plan to retire and repurpose fossil fuel infrastructure, cut 
methane and protect workers and communities,” Guterres said. “Taxing fossil 
fuel windfalls can help pay for all this – and shield families from the next 
price shock.”
An event at the COP31 climate conference in Antalya, Turkey, on Tuesday, Nov. 
10, 2026.Photographer: Kerem Uzel/Bloomberg
Also at the talks today:

The US midterm elections that swept Democrats to control of the House, Senate 
and a majority of the country’s governorships should be a wakeup call for 
President Trump on climate and the country’s clean energy export potential, 
said former US special envoy John Kerry.

Kerry said Trump’s moves are discouraging renewable deployment inside the US, 
and mean exporters are ceding ground to China in the market to supply energy 
tech.

“He’s missing the point. He’s missing the competitiveness with China. He’s 
missing the marketplace for goods that could be produced in the United States,” 
Kerry told reporters. “He walked away from the battlefield. He totally took 
America out of that sector.”
The China pavilion at the COP31 climate conference.Photographer: Kerem 
Uzel/Bloomberg
COP is where all the voices of the world come together – literally this year.

When a traditional music performance at China’s national pavilion threatened 
to drown out a panel on electrification at the nearby UK pavilion this 
afternoon, Fiona Howarth, the founder of Octopus Electric Vehicles, sang her 
remarks to the soaring Chinese soundtrack, winning a round of applause.
Attendees leave after attending the COP31 climate conference in Antalya, 
Turkey, on Tuesday, Nov. 10, 2026.Photographer: Kerem Uzel/Bloomberg
It seems every COP begins with grumbling about the logistics — this year, 
drama is centered on the challenge of just getting there. Some attendees 
endured three-hour commutes in official shuttles on Tuesday, while light-rail 
cars were so jam-packed new riders couldn’t board in the final few stops.

The anxiety is set to intensify Wednesday, with the start of a leaders’ summit 
bringing heads of state to the talks. An official advisory has warned anyone 
attending COP31 to plan to arrive between 5 a.m. and 7 a.m. Wednesday.

Why emissions fell

By Eric Roston 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQdgGkZhizQ/eric-roston?cmpid=BBD111026_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261110&utm_content=11221>

Global carbon emissions are falling this year, a drop explained more by the 
recent oil shock than by countries’ gradual transition to clean energy.

Carbon dioxide pollution from burning fossil fuels will decline by 0.4% in 
2026, to 38.2 billion metric tons, this year’s Global Carbon Budget estimates. 
The main driver is the US-Israeli war on Iran and its impact on the oil market, 
according to the network of 140 researchers who compile the annual report.

Oil-related emissions fell 1.9%, to 12.6 billion tons. The analysts consider 
it a short-term drop likely to change as oil markets recover in the months and 
years ahead.

Lead author Pierre Friedlingstein said although the decline is encouraging, “a 
reduction driven partly by geopolitical shocks is not a sustainable climate 
solution.”
