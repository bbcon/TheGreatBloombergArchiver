Rethinking on the Fed

Since Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh on July 29 highlighted an increase 
in US Treasury yields, saying they had moved “quite a bit,” they have climbed
quite a bit more 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-27/the-bond-market-is-getting-closer-to-sounding-alarm-on-economy?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>
.

Yields on 10-year Treasuries — which Warsh has called “the most important asset
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-24/us-treasury-yields-hit-5-as-wall-street-faces-new-rate-regime?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>
 anywhere in the world” because it’s the benchmark off of which other borrowing 
costs are determined — as of Friday’s close surged by more than half a 
percentage point since the Fed chief’s July observation.

Just under a third of that move came last week. A key part of the reason: a 
fundamental rethinking about Fed officials’ policy path. Michael Feroli, 
JPMorgan Chase’s chief US economist, said it was “arguably the biggest 
contributor to rising yields” last week.

Warsh’s comments in his Sept. 16 news conference following the Fed’s first 
interest-rate hike in three years kicked off the repricing. Whereas in July he 
had suggested financial conditions had tightened, this month he said, “I would 
behard-pressed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-27/bessent-urges-fed-to-keep-open-mind-on-us-inflation-outlook?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>
 to describe broadfinancial conditions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-17/trump-denied-rate-cut-as-federal-reserve-targets-war-stoked-inflation?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>
 as restrictive. This view was widely shared by the committee.”

Financial conditions can be measured in various ways. But the fact that 
Treasury yields had continued to increase between Warsh’s July 29 and Sept. 16 
press briefings clearly wasn’t the most important element in the Fed chief’s 
mind. US equities had climbed in that interim, however, while credit flows to 
corporate borrowers remained strong, withstanding higher rates.

To the extent Warsh may be setting aside higher yields in assessing financial 
conditions, that may reduceconstraints on their rise 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-25/us-30-year-yield-tops-5-5-in-vacuum-after-sentiment-gauge?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>
.

Something else has prompted a bit of a rethink about the Fed path: signals 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-25/fed-s-hammack-says-yields-reflect-growth-us-debt-and-rate-path?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>
 on how policymakers now view the implications of supply shocks.

“Over much of the past 20 years, policymakers generally looked through 
energy-driven inflation shocks,” Barclays economists including Jonathan Millar 
wrote in a note Friday. (The thinking was, a surge oil prices would trigger a 
one-time impulse to inflation, and wasn’t something to hike rates over.) But 
“recent communications suggest a somewhat different emphasis,” Barclays said.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-09-27/inflation-keeps-pressure-on-the-fed-video?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>
New York Fed President John Williams — a member of the so-called troika 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-09-07/fed-latest-warsh-s-speech-contrasts-with-new-york-fed-president-s-take?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>
 of key policymakers — last week said the central bankcan’t ignore 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-24/fed-s-williams-says-more-work-needed-to-bring-down-us-inflation?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>
 supply shocks, and should ensure they don’t become entrenched in inflation. 
“We still have a lot of work to do” to get inflation down, he also said.

By the end of the week, Citigroup economists Andrew Hollenhorst and Veronica 
Clark concluded that the Fed has “a more hawkish reaction function” in setting 
policy, in messaging that it will react to higherenergy prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-23/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-sept-24?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>
 with higher rates. This will “make it hard for the market to price-out 
aggressive rate hikes until data convincingly show either slowing inflation or 
softening activity.”

Key data in coming days 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-28/key-us-data-this-week-seen-bolstering-case-for-october-rate-hike?cmpid=BBD092826_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9346>

The Week Ahead

US hiring probably remained solid for a second month in September as economic 
momentum builds, even in the face of mounting cost pressures.

Economists estimate the jobs report out Friday from the Bureau of Labor 
Statistics will show payrolls rose about 90,000 after climbing in the prior 
month by the most since March. The unemployment rate is seen remaining at a 
one-year low of 4.1%.

The catalyst for firmer hiring recently has unsurprisingly been the economy 
itself. Demand metrics highlight an extended and feverish pace of capital 
spending as well as an improved consumer spending trajectory.

Need-to-Know Research

Central banks are less able or willing to raise rates when debt levels are 
high, according to a newstudy 
<https://www.adb.org/sites/default/files/publication/1174871/asian-development-outlook-september-2026.pdf>
 by the Asian Development Bank that sheds light on the impact of fiscal 
dominance. Even when officials do tighten monetary policy, the transmission to 
the economy is dulled, the ADB said.
Source: Asian Development Bank
A deterioration in the fiscal balance by 1 percentage point is associated with 
policy rates that are 16 basis points lower on average than warranted by 
macroeconomic conditions, the ADB said in a study spanning three decades of 
data from 85 advanced and emerging economies. In a high-debt environment, that 
gap is much larger at 32 basis points.

The passthrough of monetary policy is also weaker. A large public debt pile 
means risk premia affect yields more than the policy rate. Highly indebted 
households and firms also tend to prioritize repairing their balance sheets, 
instead of adjusting their consumption and investment to changes in interest 
rates, the ADB said.
