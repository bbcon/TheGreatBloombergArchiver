Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
EMEA metals and mining team leader Mark Burton looks at a historic squeeze in 
the copper market and why investors are betting against it anyway.

One of the biggest squeezes ever seen 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-17/shanghai-exchange-intervenes-after-historic-copper-squeeze?cmpid=BBBXT111726_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11511>
 in the copper market has arrived just as investment funds turn decidedly cold 
on the outlook for the metal.

Just two months ago, copper was trading at all-time highs near $15,000 a ton 
on the London Metal Exchange, with sky-high expectations for usage in data 
centers luring investors at the same time as tariff-related trade dislocations 
tightened supplies in the underlying physical market.

The unraveling of the AI trade has since sent prices tumbling more than 10%, 
with previously bullish investorsbailing out <bbg://screens/LME%20COT%20CA> and 
some funds taking an outright bearish view. But the strains on spot supplies 
have only grown more acute in top consumer China.

This week they erupted in dramatic fashion on the Shanghai Futures Exchange, 
with copper buyers facing the biggest squeeze in more than 20 years.

Typically, the emergence of such a massive backwardation — where spot metal 
trades above futures — would be a signal for investors to turn bullish, given 
that it points to a surge in demand for increasingly scarce inventories.

Big backwardations were precursors to major rallies at the start of China’s 
industrial supercycle in the early 2000s and the country’s recovery from the 
Covid-19 pandemic.

But so far the squeeze in China has done little to lift the mood, with copper 
on the London Metal Exchange slumping more than 6% this month even as China’s 
spot market started to surge. And as spot prices spiked on SHFE on Monday, some 
of the market’s largest brokers were adding to bearish positions in later-dated 
contracts.

That creates a rare trading dynamic in which backwardations are growing as 
prices fall, and it’s not limited to copper. Aluminum is also facing thebiggest 
squeeze in decades 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-17/aluminum-squeeze-intensifies-lme-traders-scrambling-for-metal?cmpid=BBBXT111726_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261117&utm_content=11511>
 on the LME after a sharp slide in prices, while tin and zinc are facing 
similar dynamics as the mood among investors sours.

It’s a trading landscape that’s fraught with risk for bearish investors, who 
typically can’t deliver metal against their contracts. Instead, they must buy 
them back or roll them forward as they approach expiry, and the backwardation 
represents a surging cost for them to do so.

And that can make the squeeze self-reinforcing. If enough bearish investors 
rush to buy back expiring contracts, they can create even steeper 
backwardations. The result is a counterintuitive dynamic where the investors 
most bearish on copper are the ones pushing prices higher.

—Mark Burton, Bloomberg News
