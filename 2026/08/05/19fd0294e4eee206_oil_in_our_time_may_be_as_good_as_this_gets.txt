Realpolitik, From the Strait to the Street

The stock market’s relationship to war and peace can be troublesome. Just look 
at the Dow Industrials in 1938 and 1939, as an ever-more belligerent Hitler 
brought Europe sliding ever further into war. The impact on Wall Street wasn’t 
totally predictable:

Germany’s annexation of Austria in 1938, and a year later of the parts of 
Czechoslovakia that the allies hadn’t already ceded, brought predictably 
negative responses. But the September 1938 Munich agreement, which Britain’s 
prime minister hailed as “peace in our time” (in return for surrendering Czech 
territory to Hitler), got a warm reception. Traders were relieved that the 
prospects of war had receded, even at the cost of appeasing a dangerous 
dictator.

The author Scott Sumner quotes the New York Times account 
<https://bloom.bg/3Ue2gIA> of the dreadful episode as follows:

From a strictly market viewpoint the news of the decision of the Czech 
Government to capitulate to the demands that it cede the Sudeten area to 
Germany was favorable. Prices, quite naturally, improved as the threat of war 
seemed to recede. 

When Britain and France declared war a year later after Hitler invaded Poland, 
stocks rallied again. A global cataclysm was underway, but at least there was 
clarity. For the most cold-blooded exposition of the cliche thatit’s 
uncertainty that markets really hate, even more than war, 
<https://bloom.bg/3TY7kRn> this is unbeatable. 

The deal taking shape <https://bloom.bg/4xFWVsn> over the Strait of Hormuz 
might yet run it close. The news is thatIran and Oman <https://bloom.bg/4fPxuwX>
 have thrashed out an arrangement to reopen the narrow waterway in return for 
charges that would not be called tolls.

The oil market takes this seriously. Brent crude is down 11% since the market 
opened on Monday morning — although that move hasn’t yet fed through to 
gasoline prices at the pump in the US:

Jean Ergas of Tigress Financial Partners candidly described the emerging deal 
as “Munich 1938. Oil in our time.”

The US decided to pull back from a planned offensive over the weekend, and 
this was taken as a clear signal that it was not prepared to escalate. While 
President Donald Trump might well complain about a deal that involves charging 
for the Strait, Tina Fordham of Fordham Global Foresight argues that all that 
matters is whether he attempts a military solution, as the approaching November 
midterm elections will make it ever harder to do so politically. She said: “The 
closer we get to midterms without a major US military escalation in Iran, the 
more likely Tehran is tosalami tactic <https://bloom.bg/4ha3MoN> its way to de 
facto monetizing the Strait of Hormuz.”

Her timetable for a Persian version of “The Emperor Has No Clothes” is as 
follows:

 * 
Iran and Oman will agree a mechanism for reopening the Strait that will 
involve some kind of ecological and management fees.

 * 
Trump will object loudly. 

 * 
Everyone else will comply quietly. 

 * 
Periodic US threats will continue. 

It’s hard to find people who disagree with this. “If there is any adage that 
has been confirmed this year,” says Marko Papic of BCA Research, “it is that 
beggars can’t be choosers.”

The White House is unwilling to mount a massive surge of military assets to 
the region while the American public is unwilling to incur economic pain for 
the sake of the sanctity of freedom of navigation. As such, the most likely 
outcome of the current impasse is that some vessels transiting the Strait of 
Hormuz — but not all — pay some form of a toll to Iran.

In the long run, this is terrible news. It’s tantamount to a surrender by the 
US. But the Munich precedent is clear. The picture is clarifying, uncertainty 
is reducing, and the price of enabling a dictatorial regime is steep but 
payable. No wonder the market is rallying.

A Reset, and a Record

With one bound, the stock market was free. The S&P 500 is back at an all-time 
high, its first in two months, after gaining 1.8% on Tuesday. As Points of 
Return pointed out yesterday, phenomenal earnings growth 
<https://bloom.bg/4gcsrbd> will do that for you.

It’s startling to set new highs so swiftly after momentum stocks suffered a 
major crash and semiconductors went through a drastic correction. The record 
suggests confidence that that reset is now complete. As this dashboard shows, 
tech — particularly semiconductors and the Magnificent Seven platform groups — 
is still sitting on massive outperformance in the ChatGPT era, but it’s 
plausible that a healthy correction has now run its course:

Did Big Tech boil over so spectacularly because of a technical accident? 
Figures on hedge fund leverage suggest that’s possible. Excessive borrowing 
appears to have extended far beyond the small Situational Awareness fund, whose 
blowup and rescue by the Citadel Group attracted much attention last week. 
Figures from the Office of Financial Research cover only up to the end of 
March, but they suggest the hedge fund sector had levered up massively ahead of 
the recent turbulence:

Citadel might have made an enlightened move that stopped problems from 
spreading to big players. Ian Harnett of Absolute Strategy Research points out 
that the exchange-traded fund tracking Goldman Sachs’ hedge fund VIP stocks — 
including hedge funds’ most popular picks — appears to have led both the 
decline and the rebound. It’s also overlapped with the fortunes of the AI 
hyperscalers. So there’s at least some circumstantial evidence that the market 
swoon and recovery is the story of over-levered hedge funds getting into and 
out of a mess:

Broader macro factors have also helped. Inflation expectations, as measured by 
the swaps market, have collapsed in the last two months — whether 
coincidentally or otherwise, starting to fall just as Kevin Warsh took over at 
the Federal Reserve. With inflation forecast to drop below the Fed’s 2% target, 
worries about higher interest rates ahead will naturally tend to abate:

The big fall in inflation expectations has much to do with the situation in 
the Middle East, on which there is much news. (See above.)


A K-EM Story

Investor optimism for emerging markets isn’t what it was. Bullish sentiment 
has given way to tentative optimism as the energy shock continues to ripple. 
Idiosyncratic factors, including the divergence between net oil importers and 
exporters, and the windfall for the countries providing chips for the AI 
buildout, mask uneven stresses. Before the war, the MSCI emerging markets 
index, weighting each country equally, had surged by about 15%. Five months 
later, it’s only just regained its pre-war peak:

The power of semiconductors is evident, and excluding technology would leave 
the MSCI below water for the year. But the emerging world has still overcome a 
bad year for China:

This picture changes materially once the AI supply chain is given greater 
prominence. When Taiwan and South Korea are weighted in line with their full 
market cap, MSCI’s index is up by more than 34%, despite the war. It’s one of 
its strongest starts ever:

The exposure to AI has allowed EM equities to shine, and this trend, 
BlackRock’sEgon Vavrek forecasts <https://bloom.bg/4gdbYDK>, should persist at 
least for the rest of the year. As with the developed world, growth in profits 
more or less assures this. EM earnings grew roughly 8% in 2025, but are on 
course to rise 35% this year ― more than any other region:

This is chiefly attributable to chips. Earnings in Taiwan, home of TSMC 
<https://bloom.bg/4yUIimc>, are expected to grow 34%, while the market expects 
220% (!) growth from South Korea <https://bloom.bg/4g92ARz>. Vavrek says there 
is a bit more to the Korean story than chips:

The government is pushing a program of corporate reform ― similar to that seen 
in Japan ― to increase shareholder value. And many South Korean companies ― 
such as the shipbuilders ― have benefited from increased geopolitical 
fragmentation and the desire of some Western countries to reduce reliance on 
China.

However, the concentration of EM growth in Asia’s technology sector carries 
risks. To endure, growth will require a lasting solution to the Iran conflict 
and a revival of the manufacturing cycle beyond Asia.

That would broaden earnings momentum, which Oxford Economics’ Tianchen Peng 
argues need no longer be concentrated in AI beneficiaries. Strategists at 
Oxford Economics downgraded EM equities to neutral largely because of the lack 
of clarity on Iran. Even before the conflict, emerging markets faced fiscal 
pressure. Debt-service costs surged to a 20-year high of more than 11% of 
government revenue — an increase of over six percentage points in the past 15 
years — reflecting heavier debt burdens and the sharp rise in global bond 
yields, as Oxford Economics illustrates:

The conflict has nevertheless added another layer of strain. If the current 
hopes for a Hormuz deal are confirmed, emerging markets would be major 
beneficiaries.

High debt-service costs imply years of politically painful fiscal 
consolidation. But Oxford’s Max Shirley argues they’re partly offset by 
stronger institutions, healthier external balances, and deeper domestic 
financial markets, which have left many emerging economies more resilient.

—Richard Abbey



 Survival Tips 



Who would you rather be: the Beatles or the Rolling Stones? It’s a profound 
question that some will recognise as a quote from a Metric song (here’sthe 
lyric video <https://bloom.bg/4xlCVL5>) called Gimme Sympathy 
<https://bloom.bg/3UcnO8u> (citing what are possibly the Stones’ greatest two 
<https://bloom.bg/4bqIa3Y> songs <https://bloom.bg/4xk2zjq>). It concludes by 
asking to play George Harrison’sHere Comes the Sun <https://bloom.bg/4xmnYIR>. 
What I hadn’t understood before hearing the lead singer explain it in concert 
last week is thather answer might well be the Stones <https://bloom.bg/452PD5D> 
— better not to get too consumed by fame and go too close to the flame. Rather 
than be brilliant for eight years and burn out, maybe settle for being a more 
erratic outfit that keeps going for 60. Then listen to “Here Comes the Sun,” 
which the Beatles recorded when they knew they’d come to their end. The Beatles 
versus Stones question comes in a throwaway pop song, but it’s avery thoughtful 
one <https://bloom.bg/4yPFEy2>. Any thoughts out there?
