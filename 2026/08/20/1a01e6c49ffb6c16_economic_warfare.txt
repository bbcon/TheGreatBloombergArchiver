President Donald Trump is promising an “economic D-Day” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/trump-says-us-starting-economic-warfare-operation-against-iran?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7977>
 against Iran, threatening sweeping consequences for any country, bank or 
business that continues to provide Tehran with an economic lifeline. The 
warning marks an effort to turn up the financial pressure after weeks of 
military strikes and aUS blockade 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-13/trump-says-us-will-be-reimbursed-20-rate-for-hormuz-traffic?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7977>
 have failed to force Iran’s surrender. The obvious target is China, which buys 
the vast majority of Iranian oil, making Trump’s threat potentially as much a 
test of relations with Beijing as another escalation against Tehran.

The pivot also hints at the limits of the military campaign. With US resources 
stretched, the war increasingly unpopular and high gas prices becoming a
political headache 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-14/us-readies-iran-penalties-as-trump-downplays-midterms-impact?cmpid=eveapac&utm_campaign=eveapac&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260820&utm_content=7977>
 ahead of the midterms, economic warfare offers Trump another lever, though 
it’s not clear how much sanctionable ground remains after decades of pressure 
on Iran. Tehran is already suffering from soaring inflation, a plunging 
currency and sharply constrained oil exports. The gamble now is whether 
squeezing harder finally brings Iran back to the negotiating table, or simply 
widens the economic fallout.
