AI vs Economists

The debate over the impact of artificial intelligence on the trajectory of 
productivity gains is raging at the highest echelons of policy and academia — 
fromNobel laureates 
<https://www.nobelprize.org/nobel-prize-conversations-artificial-intelligence/> 
to central bankers. But for econ grads, the issue is far more immediate: 
whether they can get a job.

Jason Furman, the former White House chief economist now at Harvard 
University, caused a stir last month when hesaid 
<https://x.com/Neil_Irwin/status/2113668825766043779?s=43&t=xAlp-BM8zaDFVEcg4s2xpQ>
, if he were running the White House Council of Economic Advisers today, he 
would sadlychoose AI <https://x.com/jasonfurman/status/2116898253312836082> 
models over hiring 20 current and aspiring PhDs.

He later wrote a longer piece 
<https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2026/ai-could-do-my-first-job-not-my-last>
 acknowledging his comment was provocative. But the main takeaway stood: the 
kind of work the CEA entails, from tracking data to drafting reports, could 
mostly be done by AI models now.

It didn’t go unchallenged. Claudia Sahm, a former Federal Reserve and CEA 
economist, argued that human junior staff are more likely to challenge bad 
ideas, which is a core mission at the White House agency — making that point in 
a Bloomberg Opinioncolumn 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-11-05/ai-can-improve-economic-policy-but-human-judgment-remains-essential?cmpid=BBD111126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11264>
 and an “Ode to the Juniors 
<https://stayathomemacro.substack.com/p/ode-to-the-juniors?utm_source=post-email-title&publication_id=280281&post_id=224035538&utm_campaign=email-post-title&isFreemail=true&r=354e1&triedRedirect=true&utm_medium=email>
” Substack post.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-08/why-ai-is-sucking-up-the-world-s-wealth?cmpid=BBD111126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11264>
Where Furman and Sahm agree is that AI is a personal productivity booster for 
established economists 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-30/fed-s-williams-quips-economist-jobs-are-safe-as-ai-shift-unfolds?cmpid=BBD111126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11264>
. Furman recently launchedEconvitals <https://econvitals.org/about/>, an 
AI-generated macroeconomic dashboard featuring daily briefings. It’s an 
experiment, which he said was vibe-coded with the help of two Harvard College 
students.

Sahm leans on AI for research and coding, work that she would have either done 
herself in the past — or not done at all. She doesn’t have a research 
assistant, she says, so AI isn’t replacing anyone.

For Gad Levanon, chief economist at the Burning Glass Institute and author of 
theLabor Matters <https://gadlevanon.substack.com/> Substack, AI has resulted 
in “a dramatic increase in the number of pieces I write and some improvement in 
their quality.”

His AI “research team of one” pulls data, iterates charts, tightens copy and 
stress-tests his arguments. “What’s changed the most is how much of my thinking 
actually becomes published work,” he says.

Need-to-Know Research

As El Niño intensifies 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/what-is-a-super-el-nino-how-it-affects-weather-and-food-production?cmpid=BBD111126_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261111&utm_content=11264>
 and concern mounts about the damage it could do, Oxford Economics compiled a 
vulnerability index to assess which countries and industries are most at risk. 
It concluded that Ecuador, Peru and Indonesia top the list.

The study examined both the likelihood of adverse weather and the structure of 
economies, where dependence on “vulnerable sectors like agriculture and mining” 
tends to increase exposure. Another element in the mix is how countries 
generate their electricity. “Power grids reliant on hydropower and nuclear can 
be particularly susceptible to drought, while heat can disrupt thermal 
generation through cooling-water constraints and reduced efficiency.”

Among developed nations examined by Oxford Economics, the US has medium levels 
of exposure in its agriculture and food sectors. Still, advanced economies tend 
to lean more heavily on “less weather-sensitive services,” reducing their 
aggregate exposure, Associate Director Sean Metcalfe wrote.
