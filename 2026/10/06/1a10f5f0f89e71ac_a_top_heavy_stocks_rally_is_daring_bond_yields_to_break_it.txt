Tech Breadth

Landmarks coincide once more. The 10-year Treasury yield 
<https://open.spotify.com/episode/3gDyV464fSKgvFZ2W2LkTE?si=X4dEC0KvQjO_XPGbIxi_-A>
, the most important number in global finance, has now broken through its high 
from 2007, when a bond sell-off triggered the Global Financial Crisis. It’s now
the highest since 2002 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-05/rising-us-bond-yields-spark-crisis-of-confidence-in-france?cmpid=BBD100626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9682>
:

That, evidently, is scary. The latest impulse upward came from the ISM 
Services report for the US. Services inflation is proving intractable, and the 
proportion of supply managers complaining about rising prices climbed to its 
highest since the post-pandemic surge. With the exception of a few months at 
the top of the oil price spike in 2008, and in the brief spasm that followed 
Hurricane Katrina in 2005, the reading is the strongest since the series began 
in 1997:

Bond yields 
<https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/could-high-us-bond-yields-spark-a-crisis-in-europe/id1578096201?i=1000793315934>
 are assumed to have an inverse relationship with stocks, so a move of this 
magnitude should be a problem for equities. It hasn’t been. The Nasdaq-100 also 
set a landmark Monday, rising 0.9% to top 31,000 for the first time. On the 
face of it, the big tech companies are proving immune to the rising cost of 
money.


Big Take

The Global Bond Selloff is Getting Messy
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/audio/2026-10-05/big-take-the-global-bond-selloff-is-getting-messy-podcast?cmpid=BBD100626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9682>
It isn’t as simple as that, of course. The Nasdaq-100’s advance (and with it 
the broader US stock market) grows ever narrower, and ever more dependent on a 
small group of gigantic companies. That’s clearest if we compare the 
Nasdaq-100, which is weighted by market capitalization (so Nvidia, the largest, 
is 8.6%. Apple is 7.2% and so on), with the equal-weighted version, in which 
each stock accounts for 1% regardless of size, and with the equal-weighted S&P 
500.

In the era of the AI boom, which we can date to OpenAI’s launch of ChatGPT in 
November 2022, the Nasdaq-100 has doubled the price return of its 
equal-weighted version, which in turn has roughly doubled the return of the 
equal-weighted S&P 500. The overall market has advanced by any of these 
measures, to be clear; but we would not be in the territory of successive 
all-time highs without the leadership of a few:

The imbalance has intensified of late. The stock market hit its low for the 
year on March 30, when there were fears that the Iran war would escalate out of 
control. Then it pivoted and rallied as geopolitical worries eased - the S&P 
500 is up 22.7% since then. But remarkably, 199 stocks, almost 40% of the 
whole, have declined.

Records are being set because the biggest companies continue to grow, and they 
are more than enough to pull up the index. In the following chart are the ones 
that made the largest positive and negative contributions to the S&P 500 since 
March 30 in terms of index points:

Some significant firms are losing ground, but the massive returns for tech are 
swamping that effect.

’Twas not always thus. In the year leading up to the ChatGPT launch, the 
market was steadily broadening - albeit mainly via declines for the leaders 
rather than gains for the laggards. Once the AI excitement was unleashed, the 
market narrowed again:

There’s reason in the apparent madness. Profits and revenues for those 
behemoths have surged in the last year, while forecasts have risen even more. 
This is what has happened to projections for the NYSE FANG+ index, which 
includes the largest hyperscalers plus companies like Palantir Technologies 
Inc. and Broadcom Inc.:

The problem could ultimately prove to be one of distribution. Corporate tax 
cuts, as seen last year, directly raise profits while cutting into tax 
revenues, thus allowing stocks to rise and bonds to fall in tandem. Meanwhile, 
lenient regulation for the biggest AI groups allows them to hoover up earnings 
from others. Opinion polls ahead of the US midterms suggest that voters are 
getting angry about this. Ultimately, the greatest risk to the simultaneous 
advance in tech stocks and long bond yields may be political. For now, the 
momentum behind both trades continues unabated.

Coffee in Brazil

It was pretty hard to predict that Flavio Bolsonaro would do quite so well in 
the first round of presidential voting in Brazil. Against expectations, he beat 
incumbent President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva into second place, and now seems 
overwhelmingly likely to win the run-off in two weeks’ time. Until a few weeks 
ago, as betting on Polymarket shows, Bolsonaro’s chances of prevailing seemed 
very slim:

The elections for the legislature need no second round, and so the scale of 
the victory for Bolsonaro and the right is already clear. They now hold an 
outright majority in the upper house, and occupy a far stronger position in the 
lower house after squeezing the center, as Oxford Economics illustrates in this 
chart:

As for Bolsonaro, the prediction markets, if anything, understated his 
chances. He gained 47.06% of the first-round vote to Lula’s 45.16%, which 
doesn’t sound overwhelming. But every first-round winner since Brazil adopted 
this format has gone on to victory. And polls suggest that a majority of the 
supporters of all the eliminated candidates will swing behind Bolsonaro. TS 
Lombard provided this chart:

At 81, Lula is one of the world’s very greatest political survivors and it is 
always unwise to count him out. But it’s hard to see how he could surmount 
these odds, particularly as the mood throughout the continent appears to be 
against incumbents and in favor of the right.

If the political result was hard to predict, the market reaction to it wasn’t. 
Investors were delighted, and bid up Brazil’s Bovespa index to its highest in 
dollar terms since 2011:

Lula has in the past been good for investors, presiding over a historic boom 
in his first two terms from 2002 to 2010. Remarkably, Monday’s rally in the 
real against the dollar was the biggest in four years - but slightly smaller 
than the gain after the first round in 2024, which Lula won:

Elizabeth Johnson, who manages TS Lombard’s Brazil research team, asserts that 
the prospects for fiscal reform have driven the market reaction. With new 
control over the legislature, Bolsonaro might at least have a shot at his 
target of cutting government spending by 1.5% of GDP. Lula, she said, had had 
serious challenges convincing the markets he was serious about fiscal 
consolidation. Now, it looks like it will be Bolsonaro’s problem.

A Three Square Luxury

As the energy shock grabs attention, the factors driving food inflation are 
flying under the radar. In recent weeks, record diesel prices have prompted 
developed economies to tap their reserves to try to cushion the blow. Diesel, 
which Société Générale’s Michael Haigh describes as the “fuel and the cure of 
inflation,” deserves all the attention.

But rising agricultural commodity prices show the Iran war is having an 
inflationary impact in other ways. The Bloomberg Agricultural Spot Index rose 
the most in the last quarter since the onset of the Ukraine war four years ago:

When the Iran conflict broke out in late February, disruptions to fertilizer 
shipments raised food-security risks, and farmers turned to other parts of the 
world. But this isn’t just about the war. Extreme weather, Points of Return 
notedhere 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TI3PVIKGIFQB?cmpid=BBD100626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9682>
, could soon move to the forefront of central bankers’ concerns. El Niño is 
coinciding with fresh tensions in the Black Sea region, the so-called food 
basket for cereal grains such as wheat and corn. The effects of the climate 
phenomenon are setto turn a sugar glut 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/super-el-nino-is-set-to-turn-sugar-glut-to-deficit-this-season-sweetener-food-prices?cmpid=BBD100626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9682>
 into deficit as drought gripsAsia 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TMFNZBKIP3ID?cmpid=BBD100626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9682>
:

The price pressure stems from escalating retaliatory drone and missile strikes 
by Russia and Ukraine on commercial cargo vessels, port infrastructure, and 
energy facilities. With central bankers cognizant of the inflationary impact 
resulting from the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, these fresh risks will need 
to be addressed.

For the US, it will become clearer how much they have fed into the consumer 
price index (CPI) when September’s print is released next week. However, if the 
warning from the United Nations’Food and Agriculture Organization 
<https://blinks.bloomberg.com/news/stories/TM9SWVKIP3J1?cmpid=BBD100626_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261006&utm_content=9682>
 that last month’s food prices rose by the most in four years, it may only be a 
matter of time before a meaningful policy response is given:

The upcoming CPI print may not capture the full impact of the agricultural 
price surge. At present, inflation in the cost of eating out in restaurants is 
ahead of wage growth, but overall food inflation is still below the rise in 
earnings:

Bloomberg Intelligence’s Charles Allen argues that a return to persistent food 
inflation would erode consumer confidence by making it hard to plan purchases 
of discretionary items. It’s also plausible that price rises could gather 
momentum later in the year. BI’s Diana Rosero-Pena says that it takes at least 
three months for groceries to reflect rising agricultural prices. That would 
imply renewed pressure on the Fed.

Nick Kounis of ABN Amro says that increases would likely be dampened by ample 
inventories and retailers’ reluctance to pass higher costs on to consumers. 
That assertion aligns somewhat with Oxford Economics’ Bernard Yaros’ projection 
that, outside energy, food inflation will peak later in 2027, but at a higher 
rate than previously thought.

The bottom line is that an easing of affordability problems looks destined to 
remain elusive.

—Richard Abbey
