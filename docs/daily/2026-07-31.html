Daily Brief

Friday, July 31, 2026

US

  • Amazon cloud-computing revenue accelerated for a fifth straight quarter; Apple warned of chip supply shortages and guided weaker-than-expected revenue, with CEO Tim Cook calling the memory cost issue a "100-year flood."
  • Nasdaq 100 futures rose 1.2% and S&P 500 futures gained 0.5% as AI-related chip stocks extended gains; Microsoft added nearly half a trillion dollars in market value on Thursday, its largest single-day gain since 2008.
  • 30-year Treasury yields hit their highest level since 2007, reflecting investor skepticism about the Fed's inflation resolve.

Earnings and Corporate

Amazon's AWS segment extended its run of accelerating cloud growth to five consecutive quarters, lifting shares 12% in early trading and reinforcing the view that enterprise AI spending remains intact despite broader market turbulence. Apple offered a contrasting signal: the company warned of chip supply shortages and guided revenue below consensus, with Tim Cook attributing pressure to memory costs — a reminder that AI hardware demand is creating upstream supply dislocations that even the world's largest consumer technology company cannot fully absorb.

Markets and Rates

The week's most consequential market development in fixed income was the move in 30-year Treasuries to their highest yield since 2007, a signal that long-duration investors are questioning whether the Federal Reserve under Chairman Kevin Warsh is sufficiently committed to containing inflation. Warsh's press conference on Wednesday reportedly included a suggestion that the Fed may reassess its inflation assessment framework — language markets interpreted as ambiguous rather than hawkish. The S&P 500 and Nasdaq recovered ground as leveraged unwinds in AI trades appeared to stabilize, though the week's volatility — encompassing the collapse of hedge fund Situational Awareness — underscored how concentrated and fragile positioning had become. ---

Asia

  • China's manufacturing PMI unexpectedly contracted in July for the first time in five months, missing consensus, while construction fell to its weakest reading since the onset of the pandemic — both prints adding to evidence of broadening economic deceleration.
  • South Korea's Kospi surged 18% on Friday, partially recovering from a 17% three-day collapse, as forced selling in chip stocks appeared to exhaust itself.
  • The Bank of Japan held its benchmark rate at 1%, as expected, but upgraded its economic assessment, warned of upside inflation risk, and drew a dissent from one board member favoring an immediate hike.
  • Chinese chipmaker CXMT surged 466% in its trading debut, becoming China's largest onshore-listed company.

China: Macro and Technology

The July PMI contraction — China's first in five months — was the week's most consequential regional data release, arriving alongside a construction sub-index at its weakest since the pandemic. The combination suggests that AI investment and export resilience have not been sufficient to offset weakness in domestic demand and the property-linked construction sector. Beijing is likely to face intensified pressure to expand fiscal stimulus. Against this softening backdrop, CXMT's market debut was a symbolic counterpoint: the domestic DRAM chipmaker's 466% first-day gain channeled massive capital into China's semiconductor ambitions, with spillover benefits reaching investors from provincial governments to DeepSeek's founder. The US tightened curbs on foreign-made robotics this week — widely read as targeting China — extending the pattern of American export controls meeting Chinese technological progress in an escalating cycle.

Japan: BOJ and the Yen

The BOJ's hold at 1% was fully anticipated, but the hawkish framing — including an upgraded economic view and a dissenting vote for an immediate hike — pushed markets to price virtually every future meeting as live. Separately, Japanese authorities intervened in currency markets to support the yen, which had weakened to levels last seen in 1986. The intervention appears to have had US backing. Prime Minister Takaichi's political position is under pressure: the weak yen fuels imported inflation, eroding her approval ratings, while rising yields complicate her plans for fiscal expansion including a proposed cut in food sales tax. ---

Europe

  • Eurozone CPI rose in July, coming in above the prior month's reading and reinforcing expectations for further ECB rate increases.
  • Wildfires in France and Spain reached exceptional scale — the Gironde fire burned approximately 420 square kilometers, evacuated more than 220,000 people, and forced firefighters to defend the western suburbs of Bordeaux.
  • UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham proposed sharing income tax revenues with regional mayors as part of a devolution agenda.

Inflation and Monetary Policy

Euro-area inflation picked up in July, a print that reinforces the ECB's case for additional rate tightening. The data arrives at a moment when the Bank of England is navigating a separate challenge: the institution's messaging has grown increasingly pointed in attributing UK inflationary and labor market pressures to US trade policy. With both central banks leaning toward further restraint, the policy environment for European growth remains tight.

Wildfires and Economic Consequences

The scale of this summer's European wildfires has moved from a climate story to an economic one. The Gironde fire in France and the merged Madrid-area blazes in Spain forced mass evacuations and destroyed homes and businesses at a scale European firefighting capacity was not designed to handle. Insurers and reinsurers contacted by Bloomberg are not treating this as a transient shock: they anticipate structurally higher premiums, larger catastrophe losses, and a need to shift risk to capital markets via catastrophe bonds. Climate researchers note that Europe is warming faster than any other continent, and that conditions this summer were made at least 20 times more likely by climate change in Spain. The economic cost — spanning property damage, business interruption, and infrastructure risk — is likely to compound as a second heat spell builds across the continent. ---

LatAm

  • Colombia's central bank is expected to raise interest rates for a second consecutive month at its upcoming meeting.

Regional Monetary Policy

The most notable Latin American development in this period is Colombia's anticipated second consecutive rate hike, reflecting persistent inflationary pressure that is keeping the Banco de la República in a tightening posture even as some of its regional peers have moved toward easing. The broader regional picture reflects divergence: commodity-linked economies face varying terms-of-trade dynamics as oil prices surged roughly 20% in July on Middle East conflict escalation, a tailwind for oil exporters including Colombia and Brazil but a headwind for energy importers. No significant market-moving macro data prints for Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, or Chile were reported in this period. ---

Global

  • Oil logged its largest monthly gain since March, with Brent rising approximately 20% in July as the US-Iran conflict disrupted Middle Eastern energy exports; WTI futures traded near $83–84 per barrel.
  • The US-led Board of Peace reached an agreement for Hamas to disarm, a potential breakthrough in Gaza, though implementation timelines remain uncertain.
  • US and Chinese officials held a senior-level call laying groundwork for a Trump-Xi summit planned for September.

Oil Markets and the Iran Conflict

The roughly 20% monthly gain in Brent crude is the dominant global macro story of July, with direct implications for inflation, central bank policy, and growth across both importing and exporting economies. The US-Iran conflict continues to disrupt Middle Eastern exports, US commercial crude inventories have fallen to their lowest since September 2018, and the national average gasoline price has risen above $4 per gallon. Washington has explicitly ruled out another major Strategic Petroleum Reserve withdrawal beyond the 172 million barrels announced in March — a constraint made more binding by the SPR sitting at its lowest level since the early 1980s. US refiners are running at record utilization rates and posting exceptional profits; Valero reported its most profitable quarter on record. The conflict's resolution timeline remains highly uncertain: Bloomberg New Energy Finance's base case for European gas storage projections assumes Strait of Hormuz reopening in mid-September, a scenario that cannot be taken as given.

Geopolitics and AI

The Hamas disarmament agreement announced by President Trump represents a potentially significant diplomatic development in the Middle East, though officials cautioned that implementation details remain unresolved over the coming weeks. Separately, US-China tensions over artificial intelligence intensified: Beijing warned Washington against sanctioning Chinese AI companies ahead of the September summit, while the US moved to tighten robotics export controls. The cycle — Chinese technological advance followed by American policy response — shows no sign of abating, with consequences for global semiconductor supply chains, AI valuations, and the trajectory of bilateral relations.
