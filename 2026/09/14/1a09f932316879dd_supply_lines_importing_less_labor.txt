Geopolitical power comes from many different sources, from innovation to 
military brawn to resource riches. Throughout its 250-year history the US has 
had one consistent economic superpower though: a relentlessly growing 
population and an unmatched capacity to attract people.

Like many things, that’s a facet of the US that is changing dramatically. As 
my colleague Catarina Saraiva and I write for Bloomberg Businessweek magazine 
in astory out today 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/features/2026-trump-immigration-crackdown-sun-belt-slowdown/?cmpid=BBD091426_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8848>
, you can see it happening in cities like Houston. The consequences, though, 
are much bigger than the story of a single place.

Like many other Sun Belt cities, the Texas metropolis has over the past half 
century built an economy centered in large part on population growth. The 
Houston metro area has added 3 million people (almost 40% of its current 
population of almost 8 million) this century alone.

Much of that growth has come in the form of international migrants who make up 
a third of the local labor force. But as President Donald Trump pushes through 
the biggest change in US immigration policy in 60 years, the flow into Houston 
is slowing dramatically.

The most recent comprehensive Census numbers available show the population of 
Harris County, the biggest county in the Houston metropolitan area, increased 
by just under 1% in the year ending July 1, 2025, the slowest growth since the 
pandemic.

Source of Growth

The number of immigrants arriving in Harris County from outside the US had 
already fallen by more than 40% in that year to July 2025. And since then 
there’s plenty of evidence things have only slowed further amid Trump’s 
crackdowns. Which is why Houston is confronting the very real possibility of 
what would be the first decline in its population since a 1980s oil bust.

In the words of Pia Orrenius, a labor economist at the Federal Reserve Bank of 
Dallas, Houston is a “poster child” for what happens to US cities when you cut 
off immigration.

“Their population growth has entirely been due to international immigration,” 
she says. “What we’re worried about now going forward is, given a world of 
net-zero immigration, that source of growth, especially for Harris County, is 
going to be gone.”

More recent numbers from the Bureau of Labor Statistics show that in the first 
half of this year, Houston experienced its biggest contraction of potential 
workers this century, outside the pandemic.

Houston’s story is evidence of a major shift in America’s economic story.

Of the 14 Southern and Western states including Texas in the Sun Belt, 12 saw 
their populations grow by less than 1% and 13 recorded growth below historical 
trends in 2025, according to our analysis of Census data. Labor force data for 
the first half of this year suggest other Sun Belt metropolitan areas, such as 
Phoenix and Nashville are seeing the same trend as Houston.

Trump’s immigration push, combined with a birth rate at a historic low and an 
aging population, means the US as a whole faces the possibility of the first
population decline 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-30/trump-immigration-crackdown-could-shrink-us-population-for-first-time?cmpid=BBD091426_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8848>
 in its recorded history much sooner than demographers anticipated. All of 
which presages worker shortages, lower tax revenue, struggles to fund 
government programs like Social Security and slower economic growth.

All that also raises questions about the future of American power.

If America’s secret sauce for a long time has been its capacity to attract not 
just a vast pool of labor but the best and brightest from all over the world to 
its universities or to establish businesses, then what happens next? And what 
might that mean for the rest of the world?

US pundits have for years already been pointing to 
<https://www.foreignaffairs.com/articles/china/2021-10-01/end-chinas-rise> 
China’s already shrinking population as evidence that its powers have peaked 
and of daunting domestic problems.

That too has global implications. One solution to China’s contribution to 
imbalances in the global economy that is often mentioned is boosting its 
domestic consumption. But if the pool of Chinese consumers is shrinking that 
becomes harder. Which explains partly why Chinese companies are so eager to 
grow overseas and the genesis beyond government policy of what is often called 
China Shock 2.0.

China Shock

That in itself has repercussions beyond China. A new analysis 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-10/europe-is-trying-to-gauge-how-much-pain-it-can-take-from-china?cmpid=BBD091426_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260914&utm_content=8848>
 from Bloomberg Economics shows that the market share lost to Chinese 
manufacturers in recent years cost the 27-nation bloc 0.7% of its gross 
domestic product — or almost $150 billion — in 2025. A year earlier, the 
estimated impact was 0.5%.

Part of the economic allure of China for decades has been the promise of a new 
pool of global consumers. But Chinese consumption pales in comparison to the 
US’s. So what happens when the population decline happens in the world’s 
largest economy (the US) as well as its second-biggest (China) as plenty of 
other large economies confront the same?

Once upon a time the American answer to the looming demographic problem was 
its superpower to import talent. These days, though, we are starting to learn 
what happens when you shut that historical allure down in cities like Houston. 
They are being forced to confront the need for a very different economic model. 
Eventually it will be the US doing so. Which matters for American power. And 
the world.
