Why did COP31 talks collapse?

By John Ainger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD112226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261122&utm_content=11699>
,Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD112226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261122&utm_content=11699>

A little over two weeks ago, delegates from nearly 200 countries arrived in 
Antalya, Turkey, in search for a response to the inevitability that global 
temperatures would breach the key 1.5C goal of the Paris Agreement. By the 
bitter close of COP31, a divide over fossil fuels — the primary cause of that 
warming — proved to be unbridgeable.

So how did he world’s most prominent forum for addressing climate change end 
in paralysis 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/un-climate-talks-slide-toward-collapse-as-nations-reject-deal?cmpid=BBD112226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261122&utm_content=11699>
? These four factors combined to produce the deadlock.
Negotiations at COP31 in Antalya, Turkey on Nov. 21, 2026.Photographer: Kiara 
Worth/UN Climate Change
1. An enduring split over fossil fuels, and a new rupture over carbon capture

The key outcome of this year’s summit, the so-called cover decision, collapsed 
after the European Union and its allies said they couldn’t support a deal that 
pandered to the fossil fuel industry.

At its heart, the no-deal faction took issue with two things. First, in a 
familiar fight, accelerating the transition away from fossil fuels didn’t 
receive a single direct mention in the text. That meant the text wouldn’t go as 
far as the landmark deal struck three years ago in Dubai at COP29, which 
delivered a consensus around that pledge for the first time.

But arguably the bigger issue was how the text referred to carbon capture and 
storage, a technology that some see as diverting attention from the need to 
ditch oil, gas and coal. The final text put forward by the COP31 presidency 
called for increasing the technology to “gigaton” scale by 2035. That proved 
the final straw for EU negotiators.
Murat Kurum, president of COP31 and Turkey’s environment and climate minister.
Photographer: Michael Nagle/Bloomberg
“We will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuel,” said Darragh O’Brien, 
climate minister for Ireland, which holds the rotating EU Presidency.

2. A dead end on money issues

The perennially-thorny issue of climate finance was always going to rear its 
head again at this COP, especially after another summer of climate extremes 
highlighted the growing need for funds to help countries adapt to warming 
temperatures.

But with government budgets strained and the world’s biggest economy, the US, 
absent from the talks and pulling out of the Paris Agreement, other developed 
countries had little left to offer. Wopke Hoekstra, the EU’s climate chief, 
said in advance of the summit that the prospect of new money on the table was
“dead in the water.” <>
Wopke HoekstraPhotographer: Chris McGrath/Getty Images Europe
Nevertheless, countries were working towards a deal that would create a 
process to ideally reach an agreement at next year’s COP32 summit in Ethiopia 
to make good on a promise last year to work towards tripling adaptation finance 
for developing countries. Where that stands now after the decision fell through 
remains to be seen.

“Over the past two weeks, we have been offered next to nothing, even as we 
have said again and again, our needs are so great, the costs are so high,” said 
Kathy Jetnil-Kijiner, a representative from the Marshall Islands. “The 
financing gap keeps growing.”

3. Fresh infighting over climate science

Over three decades of climate talks, negotiators have clashed on many issues. 
The science underpinning climate change however, has rarely come into question. 
This yearthings were different <>.
Climate change activists protest at the COP31.Photographer: Chris 
Ratcliffe/Bloomberg
Scientists at the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, whose reports 
underpin climate policy, have been unable to find an agreement on whether the 
next assessment should be conducted by 2028, in time for the next key “global 
stocktake” evaluating the world’s progress. That proved to be one of the 
biggest bones of contention in Antalya, with India, Saudi Arabia and Kenya 
maintaining their opposition to publication on that timeline.

Initial decision drafts had seemed to offer a strong political signal to get 
the IPCC reports ready in time. Those drafts ended significantly watered down 
by the time the closing plenary started Sunday, and that became one of the key 
reasons talks ended in acrimony.

4. A double-presidency format didn’t help

COP31 was an experiment in how UN summits should be organized. After the 
longest conference on record and a failed cover decision, it’s fair to say it 
didn’t work.

After fractious talks over who would host at last year’s summit in Brazil, 
Turkey won the right to host while Australia got the consolation prize of being 
“president of negotiations.”
Chris Bowen, Australia’s climate change and energy minister.Photographer: 
Kerem Uzel/Bloomberg
Over the two weeks, the co-presidents from Turkey and Australia put out 
repeated statements attesting to their strong working relationship and even 
held out a prospect that COP31’s structure could be a model for future summits. 
But country negotiators found the process exasperating and cumbersome. The 
shiny venue, abundant food and flashy announcements did little to hide the 
disquiet behind the scenes.

A draft cover decision text was presented to delegates on Thursday, drawing 
heavy criticism. It then took over two further days to revise, as both 
presidencies weighed in and met with different delegations in turn. 
Through-the-night talks Saturday eventually proved too late to save face, and 
the deal collapsed.

“The failure of this COP to deliver ambitious, fair, science-based outcomes 
falls squarely on the shoulders of richer nations, petrostates, and the 
disgracefully incompetent duo of the Turkish and Australian COP presidents,” 
said Rachel Cleetus, climate program director at the Union of Concerned 
Scientists.

Inside the last hours of COP31 talks

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD112226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261122&utm_content=11699>
 andJohn Ainger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD112226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261122&utm_content=11699>

Ruptures over fossil fuels dominated the final days of the talks. A draft 
proposal omitted calls for an accelerated transition away from the polluting 
energy sources. Germany, the EU and Pacific nationssharply criticized the move 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-22/un-climate-talks-slide-toward-collapse-as-nations-reject-deal?cmpid=BBD112226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261122&utm_content=11699>
, while Panama’s environment minister Juan Carlos Navarro accused the summit of 
being held hostage by a “petroleum mafia.”

Saudi Arabia, the world’s third-largest oil producer, led a push for language 
encouraging rapid deployment of technology to capture, utilize and store carbon 
dioxide emissions. On Sunday, a representative from the country, speaking for 
the 22-member Arab group of nations, defended the approach. “It makes no sense 
that solutions — concrete solutions, which is what this COP is supposed to 
represent — are being taken off the table,” he told the conference.

A Russian delegate said the country opposed fossil fuel language on national 
security grounds and took a swipe at the EU’s carbon levies. “Climate policy 
should not turn into trade policy by other means,” he said.

Tensions ran high in a final session Sunday. Zanetor Agyeman-Rawlings, Ghana’s 
environment minister, chided the COP31 president, Turkey’s environment minister 
Murat Kurum, for having “rudely cut off” her remarks, saying it exemplified 
“disrespect” throughout the talks. And at one point, Kurum instructed UN 
climate change chief Simon Stiell to “please sit down.”
The COP31 Closing Plenary Session in Antalya, Turkey, on Nov. 22.Source: 
Anadolu/Getty Images
It’s also a blow to Australia, which led negotiations in a unique 
power-sharing arrangement with Turkey. “An obvious issue is the transition away 
from fossil fuels where some parties insisted that it must be mentioned,” said 
Chris Bowen, Australia’s climate minister who oversaw negotiations at COP31. 
“Other parties insisted that if it was mentioned, they would veto.”

Norway, Vanuatu, Panama and European Union nations refused to endorse the 
package, as countries one by one registered their complaints with the 
jettisoned proposal and the process that produced it. That delivered a fatal 
blow to negotiations that had been encouraged to produce a more decisive global 
response to escalating climate threats.

“It is unconscionable that we would have a climate pact that makes no 
reference to fossil fuels — and no reference to transition away from fossil 
fuel either,” Ireland’s climate minister, Darragh O’Brien, told the talks 
Sunday, speaking on behalf of the EU.
Delegates at the UN climate talks in Antalya, on Nov. 22.Source: UNFCCC
Closed-door negotiations failed to bridge divides between developed and 
emerging economies. Poor and particularly vulnerable nations bemoaned that rich 
nations refused to help speed the delivery of funding to adapt to climate 
change. Meanwhile, UN scientists warned last month that the most stringent 
commitment of the 2015 Paris Agreement — a pledge to limit global temperature 
rise to 1.5C — is out of reach.

“The moment required bold ambition, and we have been working tirelessly to try 
to build consensus,” said Miatta Fahnbulleh, the UK’s secretary of state for 
energy security and net zero. “It is immensely disappointing we have been 
unable to do so.”

The standoff means the COP31 summit — still technically underway Sunday 
afternoon, as some nations delivered final remarks — is concluding with only 
minor advances on less contentious items. The outcome is likely to stoke fresh 
debate about whether the diplomatic process designed to steer the world’s fight 
against climate change is still up to the task.

Panama’s COP31 star

By Azman Usmani 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AYPlpafOt_4/azman-usmani?cmpid=BBD112226_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261122&utm_content=11699>

The sharpest voice at this year’s UN climate summit emerged from Panama, the 
carbon-negative Central American nation of roughly 4.6 million people.

Until Wednesday, the country’s environment minister Juan Carlos Navarro was a 
largely under-the-radar figure at the COP31 talks. He slipped into a midweek 
press conference without a security detail, or the knot of journalists that 
trails the event’s established figures.

His opening words — “I do not bear good news” — changed the temperature in the 
room, and by the time he had finished an address, he had become the 
conference’s most vocal opponent of weak action to shift away from fossil 
fuels. Navarro accused delegates of being “held hostage by the petroleum mafia” 
and of falling in love with their fossil-fuel “kidnappers.”
Colombia’s Environment Minister Juan Carlos Navarro.Photographer: Kiara 
Worth/UN Climate Change
Navarro’s attack was theatrical, yet its timing was astute. The following day, 
negotiators produced a draft text of an agreement that omitted any reference to 
fossil fuels, sharpening the anger of countries vulnerable to climate change 
and advocates of a faster shift to renewables.

After his Wednesday intervention, the choreography around Navarro reversed. 
Reporters waited in corridors and pressed recording devices toward him whenever 
he stopped. At an event on Friday, French Ecological Transition Minister 
Monique Barbut introduced him as “the most eloquent speaker of us all.”

The minister’s breakout on the global stage at COP31 follows a long domestic 
record as a leader in environmental activism. Navarro founded conservation 
group ANCON in 1985, served two terms as Panama City mayor and ran for 
president in 2014. He later co-founded a solar-energy company and led Panama’s 
solar industry association before becoming environment minister in 2024.

In a further escalation of Panama’s opposition to polluting fuels toward the 
end of the Antalya summit, Navarro sent a letter to the joint Turkish-Australia 
presidency setting out a series of red lines and calling for oil production to 
be capped at current levels. “While we listen to endless oratory, the planet is 
being destroyed,” Navarro said.
