Heat’s cost in Europe

By Olivia Rudgard 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWBnLoYN_tg/olivia-rudgard?cmpid=BBD102326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261023&utm_content=10453>
 andLaura Millan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD102326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261023&utm_content=10453>

Europe’s summer of extreme heat caused an estimated 50,000 deaths in cities 
across the region — the highest number on record — and wiped roughly 0.17% from 
European Union GDP from April through September, or close to €17 billion ($19 
billion).
Visitors use umbrellas to shelter from the sun during high temperatures in 
central Paris, in May.Photographer: Benjamin Girette/Bloomberg
Those findings, from two separate studies, reveal the extent of Europe’s 
vulnerability to rising temperatures in what’s likely to be one of the hottest 
years in modern history.

The estimated mortality toll comes from a study released Friday, led by 
scientists at Imperial College London, which determined that almost two-thirds 
of the deaths linked to high summer temperatures would not have happened 
without climate change. A recent study from Bloomberg Economics, meanwhile, 
measured the hit to Europe’s GDP at two-and-a-half times the average loss for 
the period over the previous decade.

The overall mortality level, triggered by a series of record-breaking heat 
waves, was far higher than those in the next most deadly years. The figure 
compares to about 29,000 heat-related deaths in cities in 2022 and about 28,000 
in 2003, according to the research,which is due to be published in Nature Health
 next week 
<https://www.imperial.ac.uk/grantham/publications/all-publications/heat-related-urban-mortality-attributable-to-human-induced-climate-change-in-europe-in-summer-2026.php>
.

“We had these repeated heat events that — even without the extra heat from 
anthropogenic warming — would’ve been dangerous, but with that extra boost from 
anthropogenic warming became really, really dangerous,” saidClair Barnes 
<https://profiles.imperial.ac.uk/c.barnes22>, extreme weather and climate 
change researcher at Imperial and the study’s lead author, in an interview.

European countries experienced extreme temperatures throughout the summer. 
Heat waves began in May and continued into September, with temperature records 
broken by significant margins in many countries. Average temperatures in 
Western Europe were nearly 3C higher than the summer average between 1991 and 
2020, with some days as much as 12C higher.

Across the continent, vital infrastructure suffered. Wildfires 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-europe-summer-wildfires/?cmpid=BBD102326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261023&utm_content=10453>
 destroyed homes,hospitals 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-16/-horrific-conditions-grip-uk-hospitals-unprepared-for-heat?cmpid=BBD102326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261023&utm_content=10453>
 overheated, transport broke down and cooling systems infridges 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-12/so-long-sausage-rolls-heat-upends-britain-s-high-street-habits?cmpid=BBD102326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261023&utm_content=10453>
 and air conditioners failed.

French GDP was 0.2% smaller in the second quarter due to extreme heat, 
according 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/terminal/TN7KBZKJH6V4?cmpid=BBD102326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261023&utm_content=10453>
 to Ulrich Eberle, author of the Bloomberg Economics report. Germany’s economy 
was 0.1% smaller over the same period, while Spain saw a 0.05% heat-related GDP 
dent.

Climate toll

Two-thirds

The share of this summer’s excess mortality toll that can be tied to climate 
change, up from about 40% in 2003, according to a study by Imperial College 
London and the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine. The study covered 
854 cities in 30 countries.

Attribution challenge

“We often see elderly patients become confused or have strokes or have 
cardiac-type events where heat may have made a significant contributing factor 
to the death.”

Andrew Hartle

An intensive-care consultant at Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust.

Hartle was commenting on the difficulties in calculating heat deaths.

2026 heads for heat record

By Eric Roston 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQdgGkZhizQ/eric-roston?cmpid=BBD102326_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261023&utm_content=10453>

A strong El Niño is already worsening droughts and floods that are pushing up 
food prices and fueling wildfires. The event is now expected to make 2026 the
hottest year <https://berkeleyearth.org/september-2026-temperature-update/> on 
record, overtaking 2024, according to a monthly analysis released Thursday by 
the research nonprofit Berkeley Earth.

Another scientific body, the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric 
Administration, forecasts that 2026 is stillunlikely 
<https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/global-climate-202609> to supplant the 2024 
heat record but will come a close second.

El Niño — a natural feature of global weather marked by occasional heating of 
the eastern tropical Pacific — temporarily exacerbates human-caused global 
warming by pushing more ocean heat and moisture into the atmosphere for months. 
The current pattern is similar to previous, historically strong El Niños in 
1997 to 1998 and 2015 to 2016, which brought dryness to Indonesia and the 
Philippines and parts of the Americas, and wet conditions to the US South and 
West. Only now, it’s hotter.

There’s some evidence that the Pacific hasn’t produced such conditions in a 
millennium, said Zeke Hausfather, research scientist at Berkeley Earth, citing 
a topic that climate researchers haverecently debated 
<https://news.ucsb.edu/2026/022779/el-nino-has-become-more-intense-over-last-40-years-previous-1000-years>
.

“It may well be possible that this could be the strongest El Niño event in a 
thousand years,” he said.
