Monetary Policy and Bond Markets

Treasury Secretary Bessent's dual-front market management strategy sharpened in focus on Wednesday. His expanded bond buyback program, framed as an effort to relieve what he characterized as a "fever" in the bond market, was set to disclose its size in a first round of operations. The 10-year yield held near 4.81%, close to multi-decade highs. Critics, including Stanley Druckenmiller — who mentored Bessent in his hedge fund career — have argued publicly that "governments defending prices against fundamentals always lose," a warning that underscores genuine uncertainty about whether the intervention will prove durable or merely delay an adjustment.

Trade Policy

Washington moved quickly after Ottawa's retaliatory tariffs, imposing new restrictions on Canadian dairy and alcohol imports effective within three weeks, alongside procurement exclusions for Canadian firms. Bloomberg Economics estimated a worst-case scenario — full USMCA collapse with reciprocal retaliation — could deliver a 2% permanent hit to Canadian GDP. The escalation coincides with Ottawa and the EU accelerating talks on a broader bilateral trade and security arrangement, a dynamic that may progressively complicate Washington's negotiating position.

Japan: Yen and Policy Outlook

The yen's appreciation appears qualitatively different from prior interventions. Unlike the July 31 joint US-Japan operation — which drew on Japanese FX reserves at a historically large pace but ultimately faded — the current move has occurred without official purchasing of bonds or reported reserve drawdowns. Analysts at Gavekal describe the yen as "more undervalued than any major currency in modern history," and note it has strengthened despite a strong US payroll print that would historically have driven yen weakness. The structural shift appears driven by unwinding of leveraged short positions and growing conviction that Japanese fundamentals — current account surpluses, rising real wages, and inflation expectations above 2% on five-year breakevens — justify a stronger currency independently of interest rate differentials. Core inflation excluding food and energy, while below 2%, is at its highest in three decades. Real earnings are rising and nominal wage growth is the strongest in decades.

Potential Policy Double-Punch

Market participants are monitoring a possible 2014-style simultaneous announcement: a BOJ rate hike alongside a GPIF reallocation toward domestic government bonds. The $2 trillion fund held an unusual meeting last month to discuss its portfolio, and Prime Minister Takaichi's government has signaled openness to a domestic allocation shift. Such a combination could materially reinforce yen strength, though the BOJ's rate path "remains uncertain," as Bloomberg noted. ---

ECB: Lone Tightening Cycle

The ECB stands as the only G7 central bank currently on a tightening path, a distinction that frames Thursday's widely anticipated 25-basis-point move in a complex light. Oxford Economics argues the September hike will be the last, on the basis that underlying inflation pressures remain insufficiently broad to build another Governing Council majority for further action. Core inflation has continued to ease, and services price growth has moderated. However, the latest surge in gas and electricity prices introduces meaningful upside risk to updated inflation forecasts, and overnight index swaps still imply a terminal rate near 3% by end-2027. Deutsche Bank raises the possibility that AI-driven investment and Germany's defense and infrastructure spending may be lifting the neutral rate, partially justifying market pricing — though the evidence remains tentative. President Lagarde faces questions about the ECB's reaction function, her own tenure, and the political rise of the far right.

Regional Macro Context

Latin America's macro backdrop on September 9 was shaped primarily by external factors rather than domestic data surprises. The move in Brent crude to $100 per barrel carries differentiated implications across the region: net oil exporters including Colombia and Brazil face revenue support, while net importers face renewed inflationary pressure through energy and logistics costs. The US-Canada trade war escalation, and the associated uncertainty around USMCA, has indirect relevance for Mexico given the integrated North American supply chain — though no specific Mexican policy response was announced. Brazil's sugar industry faces structural competitive pressure from cheap imports, underscored by Tongaat Hulett's near-liquidation in South Africa illustrating the global dynamics of the sector. The broader commodity environment, with diesel supply tightening globally alongside crude, will remain a key transmission mechanism into regional inflation and growth trajectories in the months ahead. ---

US-Iran Escalation and Energy Markets

The US military's destruction of five IRGC tankers marks a significant escalation in a conflict now in its seventh month, with Brent crossing $100 for the first time since July. Riyadh simultaneously halted several southern energy facilities after Houthi strikes, while Iran threatened a more aggressive posture toward US forces. Pakistan's defense minister warned that attacks on Saudi Arabia could activate the Mecca Defense Alliance. The cumulative effect — Persian Gulf output disruption, Russian refinery strikes, and a tightening diesel market — is producing a supply shock that goes beyond crude headlines. Bank of England Governor Bailey specifically flagged crack spreads as an inflation indicator, signaling that policymakers are tracking refined product prices as carefully as crude benchmarks.

Global Capital Flow Realignment

The potential repatriation of Japanese institutional capital — from both the GPIF's anticipated reallocation and broader "whale" asset manager repositioning — represents a structural shift in global funding. Bessent's concern that Japanese bond market volatility could spill over into US Treasuries reflects how interconnected these flows have become. If Japanese institutions rotate meaningfully into domestic bonds, the rest of the world faces a gradual withdrawal of what has been a reliable source of low-cost external financing.
