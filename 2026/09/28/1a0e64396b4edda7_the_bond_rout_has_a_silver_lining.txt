Twilight of the Bonds

The ground has shifted beneath the bond market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-09-25/the-big-one-is-rumbling-in-the-bond-market?cmpid=BBD092826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9356>
. Higher yields are in many ways a symptom of strength. A higher cost of 
longer-term money could have deleterious effects further down the track, but 
with stocks close to records and the US economy humming along more strongly 
than thought, it’s hard to say that they have been felt yet.

If anyone is hurt so far, it would be people who had been investing in long 
bonds in the hope of making a decent return. Using as a proxy the TLT 
exchange-traded fund, which tracks US Treasuries of 20 years or longer, these 
investors have been cleaned out. The remarkable rise amid the low inflation and 
government interventions that marked the post-crisis decade, and the spike 
during the pandemic, look in hindsight like an investment bubble:

The good news is that there are few better times to buy than when the air has 
been expelled. Yielding more than 5%, 10-year Treasuries haven’t been so 
attractive in decades. They have also withdrawn all the support that they gave 
equity market valuations during the 10 years after the Global Financial Crisis.

To see this, compare stocks’ earnings yield 
<https://www.investopedia.com/articles/investing/120513/comparing-pe-eps-and-earnings-yield.asp>
 (the inverse of the price/earnings ratio, or the percentage of market cap that 
earnings represent each year) and the 10-year Treasury yield. Whichever pays 
the higher yield is more attractive. The orthodoxy was that equities would 
naturally be more expensive, because of their growth potential (even if they 
are riskier). For about 20 years this was turned on its head as stocks earned 
more, making them a screaming buy. Now, the gap is in favor of bonds again in a 
way that hasn’t been seen in a quarter century:

That implies heavy weather ahead for stocks, even if their multiples have come 
down to take account of the higher bond yields. A subtler way to look at this 
is the Yale economistRobert Shiller’s excess CAPE yield 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2020-12-11/investors-risk-being-shipwrecked-on-shiller-s-cape?cmpid=BBD092826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9356>
, which compares the cyclically adjusted earnings yield (based on the real 
average earnings for companies over the previous 10 years) with the 10-year 
Treasury. As the following chart fromShiller’s website 
<https://shillerdata.com/> shows, this has been breathtakingly effective in 
predicting stocks’ excess returns over the ensuing 10 years. It currently 
implies the S&P 500 Index will only beat bonds by 1% annually over the next 
decade:
Source: Professor Robert J. Shiller, shillerdata.com
This measure has had a bad few years of late, with stocks performing far 
better than it had predicted. That might be because of persistent intervention 
in markets. Either way, bonds represent a more valid alternative than they’ve 
done in a generation - and investors will have to scrutinize the rosy 
projections for S&P earnings very carefully (which they should be doing 
anyway), as the bond market no longer provides a cushion in the event that 
profits disappoint.

ESG: The Recovery?!?

After more than four years of net outflows 
<https://www.accenture.com/content/dam/accenture/final/corporate/corporate-initiatives/sustainability/document/Powering-Sustainable-AI.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com>
, US sustainable funds staged a comeback and attracted positive flows in the 
second quarter. This looks like a sign that one of the biggest trends (or fads) 
of the teens has at last hit bottom. The question now is whether it can mount a 
sustainable (pun unavoidable) recovery.

Globally, Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG)-related exchange-traded 
funds drew in more than $70 billion, nearly triple the same period last year. 
This is a striking reversal for an investment strategy that had fallen out of 
favor.

But it’s not clear yet whether ESG has become a style of investing - like 
value - that goes in and out of fashion, or whether it was a fleeting craze. 
That will depend on what is driving this recovery, and it appears to be a 
revival of interest in renewable energy. Strip out flows to clean-energy funds, 
and ESG investing is still suffering outflows:

Green energy has over the last decade performed like a classic investment 
bubble, buffeted by geopolitics. It boomed after the pandemic and tanked as the 
Ukraine war showed the need for conventional energy supplies. Since President 
Donald Trump returned to the White House with an agenda to back fossil fuels, 
it’s enjoyed a resurgence:

Beyond the supply shock from Iran, renewables owe their fresh appeal to the 
surge in electricity demand from the AI data-center buildout. However, ESG 
purists point out that they have huge carbon footprints and heavy water usage, 
a debate that roiled last week’sClimate Week 
<https://www.netzeroinvestor.net/news-and-views/resilience-and-pragmatism-voices-on-the-ground-from-new-york-climate-week-2026>
 in New York.Tufts University’s Jennifer Morgan said 
<https://thebusinessjournal.com/climate-week-ai-data-centers-energy-prices/> AI 
was even more vilified in climate talks than the fossil fuel industry:

US hyperscalers are running up against massive electricity deficits, which 
only renewable energy can promptly meet at scale. Thus, the fresh excitement 
may be more about pragmatism than any conviction that green energy can combat 
climate change.

About half of the almost 700GW of renewable power capacity installed globally 
last year was in the US, according to the International Renewable Energy Agency 
and the Global Renewables Alliance. With relatively short lead times compared 
with other sources, it’s well positioned to capture some of the urgent demand 
for additional electricity:
Source: CarbonBrief
As a result, clean-energy ETFs are increasingly seen as a proxy for AI. Sonia 
Kowal 
<https://www.responsible-investor.com/us-sri-firm-zevin-asset-management-names-kowal-as-president/>
 of Zevin Asset Management says:
We are thinking about how these data centers will be powered and how that’s 
going to happen, especially with gas turbines in short supply. You can’t run 
these things on diesel generators forever. The future is renewable, and with 
costs coming down so fast, it’s kind of a no-brainer.
Last year, global electricity demand grew about 17%, while data-center demand 
surged 50%, according to the International Energy Agency. In the US, more than 
a fifth of data centers’ electricity comes from renewables, and the IEA expects 
this to rise even more.

Further expansion by hyperscalers will likely mean more carbon emissions. Data 
centers’ greenhouse gases now sit close to those of the aviation industry, and
Accenture expects 
<https://www.accenture.com/content/dam/accenture/final/corporate/corporate-initiatives/sustainability/document/Powering-Sustainable-AI.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com>
 them to rise 11-fold this decade, reaching 3.4% of the global total by 2030. 
The environmental conviction behind renewables is hard to reconcile with their 
growing role in powering the carbon-intensive data centers.

That gives ESG investors a dilemma. Bill Davis, founder of Stance Capital, a 
sustainable investment firm, says this scenario presents a “reset for everybody 
that looks” at it:
The reality is, up until this point, companies like Microsoft, Meta, and 
Google were trying to be carbon-neutral or carbon-negative by some future point 
down the road. I don’t see how they can do it with the AI buildout.
AI is bound to complicate value judgments. Beyond renewables’ favorable 
economics – relatively cheaper unit cost – and their shorter lead times for 
meeting data centers’ power needs, there’s a fundamental counterfactual: If 
clean energy wasn’t supplying data centers, what fills the gap? 
Carbon-intensive power is the likely answer.

That might justify fund managers like Davis in looking at relative strength, 
rather than absolute goodness. But after multiple“greenwashing” scandals 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-04-02/deutsche-bank-s-dws-settles-german-greenwashing-probe?cmpid=BBD092826_AUT&utm_campaign=authers&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260928&utm_content=9356>
, that will put the onus on fund managers’ disclosures to ensure that investors 
know what they’re buying.

For all of the pushback that AI receives from ESG investors, Erika Karp, 
president of Green Alpha Investments, concedes that “anyone that doesn’t 
somehow invest in AI is going to be left behind.” She argues for exposure to 
sectors likely to benefit from AI, rather than those developing it.

That raises the risk of greenwashing. Kristin Hull of Nia Impact Capital 
points out that large companies that invest in decidedly non-green data centers 
remain dominant holdings in green ETFs. The more AI becomes intertwined with 
the clean-energy story, the harder it may be for investors to tell exactly what 
they are buying.

—Richard Abbey
