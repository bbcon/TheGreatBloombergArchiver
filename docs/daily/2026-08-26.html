Daily Brief

Wednesday, August 26, 2026

US

  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's bond-buyback push appears to be working a week after launch: the 10-year yield has eased to 4.64% from 4.75%, and the 30-year swap spread has narrowed to its tightest since February.
  • Nvidia reports earnings after the close, with analysts expecting revenue to nearly double year-over-year to roughly $92 billion; the print is being treated as a barometer for the AI trade.
  • Canada doubled retaliatory tariffs on US steel and aluminum to 50% and added duties covering $20 billion of annual US exports, prompting the White House to weigh further penalties against Ottawa.
  • Fed Chair Kevin Warsh speaks Friday at Jackson Hole, with scrutiny intense after his July press conference triggered the bond market's worst selloff in years.

Treasury Market

The "Bessent put" shows tangible, if modest, signs of working. Traders say the knowledge of a large buyer has made them reluctant to press yields higher, and Citadel's Frank Flight reversed a prior bearish call to see conditions ripe for a bond rally. Skeptics remain: Stanley Druckenmiller, an early Bessent mentor, called the purchases a mistake, and the 30-year yield still sits just 15 basis points from its highest since 2007. Richmond Fed President Tom Barkin separately warned rising federal debt will eventually force a "reckoning."

AI Trade and Earnings

Ahead of results, SoftBank is weighing a bond sale of up to $20 billion to help repay a bridge loan tied to its OpenAI stake, underscoring how AI financing competes with sovereign issuance for capital. Barclays has flagged the AI trade's exposure to voter backlash over data-center buildout, and options markets show rising anxiety into the midterms.

Asia

  • Stronger-than-expected Australian core inflation for July prompted traders to boost bets on a fourth Reserve Bank of Australia rate hike this year, a sharp reversal after the RBA cut rates in 2025.
  • Hong Kong's trade deficit narrowed to its smallest in more than a year as July exports rose almost 51% year-over-year, beating economist estimates, with shipments to mainland China hitting a record on AI-linked demand.
  • China's tax authorities are hammering listed firms in a clawback tied to President Xi Jinping's revenue push: Heilongjiang Agriculture, part of China's third-largest farming conglomerate, was served a bill equal to 120% of its 2025 net income, ending a two-decade run without a first-half loss.
  • Markets have all but priced in a Bank of Japan rate hike next month.

Central Bank Divergence

Asian policy is tilting hawkish even as regional bond markets draw support from Washington's bond-buyback program, with sovereign debt in Japan and New Zealand advancing alongside Treasuries. Australia's inflation surprise adds to hawkish signals ahead of Japan's expected September hike. Thailand's central bank, by contrast, held its rate steady for a third meeting, while Indonesia's incoming governor pledged to preserve independence.

China Corporate Pressure

Beyond the tax clawback, investor unease over Alibaba's $10 billion Hong Kong share sale is persisting despite co-founder Jack Ma and senior executives buying stock; shares remain down almost 6% from before the deal. Existing Shein investors have agreed to a six-month lock-up on new IPO shares to signal confidence. Student protests over exam-system changes continued in Bihar, India, a month after similar unrest forced the education minister's resignation.

Europe

  • ECB Executive Board member Isabel Schnabel said interest rates must rise further, warning that the prolonged Middle East conflict and a surprisingly resilient euro-zone economy pose upside risks to inflation, which she said is likely to exceed 2% for an "extended period."
  • European natural gas has overtaken oil as the top inflation concern for regional bond traders; prices are near five-month highs and winter contracts cost more than double year-ago levels, pushing 10-year German and UK yields to levels not seen in decades.
  • France enters a new season of budget brinkmanship widely seen as President Macron's riskiest yet, with a post-holiday cabinet meeting and the first of several credit-rating reviews due Friday.
  • German exporter sentiment rose to a 4½-year high in August, the latest evidence of a recovery taking hold in Europe's largest economy.

ECB and Inflation Risk

Schnabel's comments reinforce a hawkish ECB tilt, cautioning that waiting for energy costs to feed into wages before acting would leave policymakers "behind the curve." Gas is playing an outsized role: it accounts for 21% of the EU's energy mix and 25%-35% of the UK's, and the Netherlands' network operator has warned the country will miss its winter storage target, leaving the region ill-prepared entering the heating season.

Political Risk

France's fiscal standoff, with parliament gridlocked, adds to a backdrop in which Russia is expanding hybrid attacks — cyberattacks, drones, arson — against countries supplying Ukraine. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz warned perpetrators "will pay the price," though the EU has yet to shift its defensive posture.

LatAm

  • Mexican sovereign bonds are trading with spreads more typical of high-yield credit following a $130 billion government bailout of state oil company Pemex, reflecting investor concern over the fiscal burden of supporting the indebted producer.
  • Brazil is set to increase diesel imports from the US in the coming weeks as its planting season approaches and the country remains cut off from Russian supplies it previously relied on, according to tanker-tracking data cited by Bloomberg.

Fiscal and Energy Strains

Coverage of the region was limited today, but the two available data points point to related pressures. Mexico's Pemex rescue is weighing on sovereign credit even after the bailout, a sign investors remain skeptical of the country's broader fiscal trajectory. Separately, Brazil's exposure to disrupted Russian energy flows — a consequence of the wider Russia-Ukraine conflict — is pushing it toward greater reliance on US diesel exports, which are themselves running at record levels amid tight global refining capacity.

Global

  • Brent crude fell below $87 a barrel, extending this week's decline to about 8%, and WTI slid 1.9% to $80.81, as Iran and Oman discussed an "interim framework" to resume shipping through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Central banks are diverging sharply: Australia and the ECB struck hawkish tones on resilient growth and energy-driven inflation, a Bank of Japan hike is largely priced in for next month, and Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Friday Jackson Hole remarks are awaited as a US policy signal.
  • Wheat and corn prices are at multi-year highs as Ukraine and Russia, which together supply more than a quarter of global wheat exports, have curtailed shipments amid mutual attacks on ports and vessels, raising food-security warnings from Egypt to sub-Saharan Africa.
  • MSC, the world's largest container line, is partially resuming Red Sea and Suez sailings, joining Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd in a tentative normalization of routes largely avoided since late 2023.

Energy and Inflation

The oil pullback looks fragile rather than durable: hopes for a Hormuz reopening have been raised and dashed before, and European gas — with winter contracts costing more than double year-ago levels — has overtaken oil as bond investors' top inflation concern. US refiners, running at their highest rates since before the pandemic, are deferring maintenance to capture record margins, leaving fuel markets little cushion against outages.

Food Security

The UN has warned that when food becomes unaffordable, vulnerable populations face a stark choice: "starve, move, or rebel," echoing dynamics behind the 2010-11 Arab Spring. Global food prices remain below the 2022 peak reached after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, but officials call the standoff over Black Sea grain exports more precarious given added strain from war-driven fertilizer costs and heat-damaged harvests.
