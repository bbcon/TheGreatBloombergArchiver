US

  • Fed rate decision due Wednesday with markets pricing ~33% probability of a hike, up sharply from near-zero earlier this month amid oil-driven inflation concerns
  • Weekly jobless claims fell to the lowest level since 1969 last week, reinforcing the picture of an exceptionally tight labor market that complicates the case for rate cuts
  • S&P 500 futures up ~1% and Nasdaq futures up ~1.6% in early Monday trading as oil's retreat eases near-term inflation fears
  • Hyperscaler earnings season begins this week (Apple, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft), with markets increasingly skeptical about AI capex discipline after Alphabet's mixed signal

Monetary Policy

The FOMC meeting on Wednesday has become genuinely uncertain. Brent crude briefly exceeded $100/bbl last week before this weekend's ceasefire pause sent it below $90 on Monday morning, temporarily reducing the urgency for a hike. Markets are pricing roughly a one-in-three chance of an increase — a dramatic repricing from earlier this month. The tension is real: several Fed officials had already set a low bar for additional tightening, and jobless claims at their lowest since 1969 suggests the labor market offers no cover for easing. The oil price whipsaw complicates rather than resolves the calculus, with the Fed likely to hold while it assesses whether the Middle East pause proves durable.

AI and Equity Markets

A notable pivot appears underway in the market's attitude toward AI capital expenditure. Despite overall earnings remaining strong, hyperscaler shares have gone essentially nowhere for 12 months, with gains having rotated into semiconductor names. Alphabet's beat last week was met with selling after it signaled further capex acceleration, dragging both hyperscalers and chip suppliers lower. This week's reports from Apple, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft will be closely watched for any signs of capex moderation. It is too early to declare the AI trade exhausted, but the market is increasingly asking whether deployment economics will justify the scale of investment. ---

Asia

  • China's industrial profits rose 15.1% YoY in June, slowing from 21.1% in May and the weakest print of the year, underscoring an uneven recovery
  • Bank Indonesia Governor Perry Warjiyo resigned suddenly for unspecified "personal reasons," triggering immediate selloffs in the rupiah, Indonesian equities, and bonds
  • China's Q2 GDP grew 4.3% in real terms, the slowest pace since late 2022

China: Uneven Recovery

The CXMT debut was spectacular by any measure, but it occurred against a backdrop of slowing industrial momentum. Profits in the integrated circuit sector surged roughly 2,580% in the first half of 2026 and electronics overall nearly doubled — yet the broader industrial economy is decelerating. Q2 GDP growth of 4.3% was the softest since Covid-Zero restrictions were still in effect, and fiscal spending data from the Finance Ministry shows expenditures declining 12% year-on-year. Local governments have used only 47% of their special bond quota in H1, suggesting fiscal impulse may prove smaller than budgeted. A meaningful new stimulus remains possible but appears far from imminent.

Indonesia: Institutional Shock

Warjiyo's abrupt resignation introduces meaningful policy uncertainty into Southeast Asia's largest economy. His four-decade career and steady hand during the Prabowo administration's turbulent early period had been viewed as a stabilizing anchor. Senior Deputy Governor Destry Damayanti assumes the acting role, but investors immediately repriced Indonesian assets lower. Whether this represents a one-off personnel change or signals deeper erosion of central bank independence remains to be seen. ---

ECB Policy

ECB Governing Council member Kazimir publicly stated the central bank must hike at least once more to bring inflation durably to target. This is consistent with the broader G7 dynamic: the Bank of England and Bank of Japan decisions later this week, alongside the Fed, will collectively signal how much weight major central banks are placing on renewed energy-driven inflation. None is widely expected to act immediately, but the tone is shifting hawkish at the margin. Euro area growth and inflation data due this week will provide additional context.

LatAm

  • Brazil recalled its ambassador to Argentina after President Milei launched an attack on President Lula and a Brazilian Supreme Court justice, marking a significant deterioration in bilateral relations
  • No significant macro data releases from the major Latin American economies in this period

Brazil-Argentina Diplomatic Rupture

The recall of Brazil's ambassador represents the sharpest escalation yet in the Lula-Milei relationship, which has been strained since Milei's election. The immediate trigger was Milei's public criticism of both Lula and a sitting Brazilian Supreme Court justice, which Brasília characterized as interference in its domestic affairs. The diplomatic rupture carries economic as well as political consequences: Brazil and Argentina are Mercosur's two largest members, and sustained tension complicates the bloc's ongoing trade negotiations, including the long-delayed EU-Mercosur agreement. How quickly the dispute is contained — or whether it deepens — will depend partly on whether Milei moderates his rhetoric, which his track record suggests is uncertain. ---

Global

  • Brent crude fell as much as 7.4% to below $90/bbl Monday morning after the US paused its 13-day strike campaign against Iran for a second night; Iran signaled restraint and engaged Oman in Strait of Hormuz talks
  • G7 central banks — Fed Wednesday, Bank of England Thursday, Bank of Japan Friday — face a common dilemma: oil-driven inflation resurgence against slowing growth
  • European wildfires have displaced over 300,000 people across France and Spain, with the Gironde blaze threatening Bordeaux; climate scientists link intensifying fire seasons to anthropogenic warming
  • El Niño conditions may push monthly global average temperatures above 2°C above pre-industrial levels for the first time on record

Oil Markets and the Hormuz Question

The weekend ceasefire pause produced an immediate relief rally, but energy market analysts are cautious about its durability. The five-month conflict has already produced structural shipping disruptions: Asian buyers are rerouting Saudi crude via the Suez Canal and around Africa, adding weeks to transit times. Iran has explicitly stated there is no change to the status of the Strait of Hormuz, and Houthi forces have demonstrated the capacity to extend pressure to the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb. Global crude inventories are low, and any renewed escalation could push Brent back above $100 rapidly. The market ceasefire may be fleeting.

Monetary Policy Divergence

The simultaneous hawkish pivot across the Fed, ECB, Bank of England, and Bank of Japan — each confronting energy-driven inflation from different starting points — represents a notable moment of G7 policy convergence, even if the degree of tightening varies. Japan's Finance Ministry jawboning of domestic pension funds toward JGB investment adds an unconventional dimension, reflecting the fiscal strain of elevated yields on the world's largest sovereign debt burden. Treasury Secretary Bessent's articulation of US government "market signaling" as an investment direction tool suggests similar financial repression considerations are present, if not yet explicit, in Washington.
