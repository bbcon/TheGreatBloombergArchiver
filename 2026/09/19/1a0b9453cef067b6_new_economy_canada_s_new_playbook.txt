Hi, I’m Erik Schatzker 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AB7kAcfmyqc/erik-schatzker?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260919&utm_content=9098>
, editorial director of Bloomberg New Economy, coming to you from Toronto. It 
was here in my hometown that I served as master of ceremonies and moderator at 
the inaugural Canada Investment Summit. This event, the first of its kind in 
Canada and headlined by Prime Minister Mark Carney, brought together 300 
business leaders, including more than 150 of the world’s biggest investors and 
financiers, for a gala dinner and full-day program built to showcase the 
country’s appeal as a destination for global capital.

France has one. So do Russia, China, the US, the UK, Qatar, Bangladesh, South 
Africa, New Zealand, Kazakhstan, the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Spain, 
South Korea and — as of five days ago — Canada.

There are now at least 15 countries with glossy, high-level, state-sponsored 
conferences that convene the world’s top investors for what you might call a 
sovereign sales pitch. Next month, the first Invest in Germany Summit in Berlin 
will add another to the list.

This is a sign. As geopolitical priorities shift from interdependency to 
security and domestic self-sufficiency, the wealthiest economies have started 
chasing the same development dollars emerging markets long have sought. And 
they’re using a playbook first written in the late 1990s by Russia’s St. 
Petersburg International Economic Forum and the China International Fair for 
Investment & Trade.

Think of all these conferences as beauty pageants where global capital is the 
judge. For any government that wants a piece of the action, the first order of 
business is coaxing the right people to show up — people with enough firepower 
to move the needle economically.

It helps if you’re Mark Carney. At his Canada Investment Summit in Toronto 
this week, the Canadian prime minister drew on relationships he built as a 
Goldman Sachs banker, governor of the Bank of Canada and governor of the Bank 
of England to attract C$120 trillion ($85 trillion) in represented capital.

I talked to dozens of the financial heavyweights in attendance, including 
BlackRock’s Larry Fink; Kazuto Uchida, president of Japan’s Government 
Investment Pension Fund; APG Chair Annette Mossman; Manny Roman, chief 
executive of Pimco; and Temasek CEO Dilhan Pillay. Some in the crowd had never 
been to Canada before.

“They did a great job getting a lot of serious global investors in the room,” 
said Karen Karniol-Tambour, co-chief investment officer at Bridgewater 
Associates and a summit attendee.

Carney’s pitch to all of them was simple. Canada, in contrast with the US and 
others, has the fiscal flexibility to catalyze investment with government 
policy because it hasn’t run up massive deficits. To illustrate this point, he 
used the summit stage to announce an immediate change in tax rules designed to 
encourage spending on aircraft, fiber-optic cables, Canadian-made vehicles, 
computer equipment, bridges and roads.

The expanded write-off will cost the government an estimated C$36 billion in 
lost revenue over five years. But as a result, according to figures released by 
Carney’s Department of Finance, Canada’s marginal effective tax rate on new 
business investment drops from 13% to 6.4% — the lowest among Group of Seven 
countries and half of what companies pay in the US.

Karniol-Tambour said Carney’s math is sound. She ran the numbers before the 
summit and found the country has more space to borrow — a lower ranking on 
Bridgewater’s “gauge of fiscal strain” — than almost every country but 
Switzerland.
Source: Bridgewater Associates
While Canada has many additional attributes, including rule of law, political 
stability, low-cost energy, vast reserves of mineral wealth and a highly 
educated workforce, it’s also become mired in a damaging trade war begun by US 
President Donald Trump.

The standoff, however, has galvanized public support for Carney. By acting 
now, while Canadians are broadly behind his agenda, he might be able to seize 
an advantage before other peer economies implement pro-investment reforms of 
their own.

Canada itself does not lack capital, mind you. On the contrary, its top 
pension managers are some of the world’s largest and most sophisticated asset 
owners. But they’re also fiduciaries; if the best returns can’t be found at 
home, they’ll invest elsewhere.

And that’s what they’ve done. Even CPP Investments and PSP Investments, the 
pension funds that functioned as the official hosts of the summit, have more 
than 40% of their combined C$1.2 trillion in assets in the US. Carney 
undoubtedly hopes to see those numbers change in the years to come.

“Global investors have a lot of choices,” Karniol-Tambour said. “They came 
looking for a top-down signal that Canada is going to create investable assets.”

So in principle it made sense to spend millions of dollars of taxpayer money 
to convene a conference making the investment case for Canada. The only 
question now is whether it worked.
