Jinping wascomprehensively expanding 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2016-07-07/xi-boosts-party-say-in-china-s-18-trillion-state-company-sector?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>

<https://www.bloomberg.com/politics/articles/2018-01-17/china-s-communists-will-take-more-stakes-in-private-companies?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>
, it was pointed out to me that telling readers an authoritarian state was 
doing authoritarian things risked stating the obvious.

There’s something similar with the narrative of China’s two-speed economy. By 
now, it’s pretty well established that the Chinese president’s emphasis on 
advanced manufacturing and emerging technologies means industrial growth is 
robust, even as domestic demand has beenleft to languish 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-15/china-s-consumption-and-investment-wilt-as-factories-power-ahead?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>
. Each new batch of data confirms the story. Retail sales soft. Industrial 
production solid.Gangbuster exports 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/china-export-growth-rebounds-as-ai-boom-offsets-extreme-weather?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>
.

And yet it’s worth taking a closer look, as the scale of divergence between 
Xi’s “new productive forces” and the rest of the economy is widening. Last 
year, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had speculated there could be a “big, 
beautiful rebalancing” of the world’s two largest economies, with China 
shifting toward domestic consumption. It’s clear now that’s not in the cards. 
The data, and this week’s largelysubstance-free 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-25/trump-xi-summit-goes-big-on-pomp-but-small-on-substance?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>
 US-China summit, clearly show that.

Instead, it’s becoming increasingly evident — in the words of veteran 
economist Larry Hu of Macquarie — that China’s economy went through “a regime 
shift” back in 2021. As detailed below, it’s deepening imbalance may lead to 
stark consequences for a world that’s failing to coordinate its response.

Hu and his colleague Yuxiao Zhang wrote in a note to clients about the August 
economic indicators that “the two-speed economy has become even more evident.” 
One of the widest divergences came in the automobile sector.

China’s domestic passenger vehicle sales are on course to drop 16% this year, 
to 19.5 million units — belowpandemic-era lows <bbg://news/stories/TLGDNDKK3NY9>
, according to BloombergNEF’s Passenger Vehicle Outlook. Exports, by contrast, 
have surged 73% this year so far, equivalent to more than half of domestic 
demand.

“Consumption is shockingly weak,” Beijing-based advisory firm Trivium China 
said of August’s retail sales. The 0.4% year-on-year increase doesn’t even 
match the pace of China’s inflation. (For comparison purposes,American retail 
sales 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-16/us-retail-sales-rebound-more-than-expected-in-broad-advance?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>
 climbed 6% last month.) Besides China’s slump in car sales, spending on 
furniture slid for the sixth month running while home renovation materials 
marked a 13th straight drop, Trivium economists noted.

And the reality may be even worse. Hui Shan, chief China economist at Goldman 
Sachs Group Inc., says that because of measurement challenges with smaller-size 
retailers, “official data may understate the true weakness.”

China’s moribund consumer spending is also seen in the relative decline of its 
imports in recent years. This, argues Frederic Neumann, HSBC Holdings Plc’s 
chief Asia economist, is the true “China Shock 2.0.”

China’s epic property bust 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/china-s-property-crisis-from-evergrande-collapse-to-spiraling-prices?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>
 is, of course, the biggest factor in this story. That began in 2021, when 
Beijing popped the leverage bubble inflated by the nation’s real estate 
developers. Not only did the resulting blast send construction and related 
industries into a tailspin, but it also hammered consumer confidence — 
particularly as home valuesstarted declining 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-09-07/fed-latest-warsh-s-speech-contrasts-with-new-york-fed-president-s-take?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>
.

Xi and his lieutenants have been selective 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-03-06/will-ai-disruption-force-xi-jinping-to-turn-china-into-a-welfare-state?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>
 in the economic stimulus they’ve applied since then, focusing on advanced 
manufacturing and high-tech industries where the goal is to catch up to — and 
then surpass — the US. In areas such as electric vehicles, that mission has 
already been accomplished — and handily so. The big challenge for China is 
these sectors don’t provide the scale of jobs that real estate did.

China’s official unemployment rate suggests little cause for concern. At 5.3% 
in August, it was little different than the pre-Covid average of 5.1%. But 
other data suggest “the labor market has deteriorated substantially,” Hu and 
Zhang say. They took a look at the employment and salary conditions of the 
roughly 5,400 non-financial companies listed on mainland stock exchanges.

What they found was that total employment rose just 1% to 2% annually in the 
past three years, well down from 6% in the 2010s. Salary gains slowed to the 
same range, versus about 8% in the pre-Covid decade. These companies tend to be 
“larger and more competitive” than non-listed firms, Hu and Zhang said, “so 
their already-soft labor data imply greater weakness in thebroader economy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-25/china-economy-6-charts-explain-why-how-economic-growth-is-slowing-down?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>
.”

A Bloomberg Economics analysis last month showed that while China high-tech 
has created “spectacular growth pockets,” its impact is limited. “Only a 
fraction of the population is enjoying the economic dividends of the tech 
transition, leaving the vast majority to absorb the pain of stagnation in 
legacy sectors,” economist Chang Shu wrote. (See her full note on the Bloomberg 
terminalhere <>.)

No wonder, then, that a China central bank survey shows 54% of household 
respondents said jobs were “hard to find” last year — the highest reading in a 
series dating back to 2013. And up from 36% in 2013, Hu and Zhang wrote.

Labor weakness makes China’s new “two-speed regime” self-reinforcing, they 
concluded. Suppressed wage growth only enhances export competitiveness.

The situation is already so imbalanced that HSBC’s Neumann calculates China’s 
trade surplus in manufactured goods, as a share of world GDP (taking out 
China), now exceeds 2%. That’s double Japan’s mid-1990s peak, he says.

There’s not much the rest of the world can do to force change, unless it 
adopts the sort ofunited front 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-01/bessent-misses-chance-to-coordinate-with-a-china-wary-europe?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260926&utm_content=9209>
 approach that President Donald Trump has demonstrated he’s not interested in. 
That leaves the US, Japan and Europe each subject to Beijing’s leverage over 
rare earths and other vital supply-chain components.

It’s a state of affairs that will see trade frictions become broader and “more 
structural,” even as China’s leverage means major escalation risks are 
contained, Societe Generale’s Wei Yao and Michelle Lam wrote this month.
