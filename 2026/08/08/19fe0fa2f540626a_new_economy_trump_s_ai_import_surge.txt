Since long before he became president, Donald Trump has publicly agonized over 
US trade deficits, framing them as the equivalent of foreign nations “ripping 
off” America. Lately, there’s been an extraordinary surge in one type of 
import, but his administration has largely let it slide.

Tallying up computers, accessories and semiconductors, June figures out this 
week showed a combined UStrade deficit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-04/us-trade-deficit-narrows-to-73-3-billion-on-drop-in-imports?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260808&utm_content=7465>
 approaching $400 billion over the past 12 months. That’s almost triple the 
total of two years earlier. The reason, of course, is the artificial 
intelligence investment boom. That figure doesn’t even capture the full 
picture, given that the buildings that house AI bots need plenty of other 
imported stuff too, like cooling systems and power equipment.

This is all being masked in the overall trade figures, where the deficit has 
been shrinking thanks to energy exports. Trump’s war on Iran has sentoil and gas
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/latest-oil-market-news-and-analysis-for-aug-7?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260808&utm_content=7465>
 prices soaring, and America sells plenty of both. But it marks a contrast with 
his administration’s announcement last summer of a drive to boost AIexports. 
For now, the main action is on the other side of the ledger.

AI trade patterns are in some ways an odd fit with the overall Trump strategy. 
The stated goal has been to get businesses to make things in the US instead of 
importing them, in part by erecting a broad tariff wall. But the deep-pocketed 
tech firms racing to build data centers have largely gotten a pass. In effect, 
they’ve been protected from Trump’sprotectionism 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-24/trump-tariffs-how-us-is-maintaining-import-duties-using-section-301?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260808&utm_content=7465>
.

The White House plan 
<https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/07/promoting-the-export-of-the-american-ai-technology-stack/>
 has an eye on the long term, citing the need for US leadership over decades to 
come, and aims to promote “full-stack AI export packages.” That’s a reference 
to the wide range of goods and services involved in AI, from chips and servers 
to algorithmic models, apps and cybersecurity systems.

American AI exports are indeed up a bit, but nowhere near as much as imports, 
which aren’t getting tagged with big tariffs. In aJune study 
<https://www.minneapolisfed.org/research/quarterly-review/trade-in-ai-related-products>
, Michael Waugh, an economist at the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, broke 
down US imports by their relevance to the AI buildout. He found that while 
there’s been a tariff hike for data center kit (largely a result of charges on 
things like power and cooling devices, not core computing), it’s a fraction of 
what most imports have been subject to.

There’s certainly been a switch-up in supply chains toward US-friendly 
countries — another piece of the administration’s trade plans. Equipment 
filling up American data centers is now most likely to come from Taiwan or 
Mexico, while the Chinese share has dwindled sharply, Waugh’s research found.

For one prominent backer of the Trump agenda, this isn’t enough.

The Coalition for a Prosperous America – a Washington-based group that lobbies 
for domestic manufacturing – argued in arecent analysis 
<https://prosperousamerica.org/americas-ai-boom-has-a-trade-policy-blind-spot/> 
that trade is a “blind spot” in the AI boom. It cited data showing that imports 
account for between 70% and 90% of the value of US AI investment, “a level of 
import intensity with few parallels in modern American industrial history.”

“Tariffs proved they can redirect trade flows. They have not yet proved they 
can rebuild domestic production,” the CPA said. “The computing stack needs to 
be reshored, not just rerouted.”

White House spokesman Kush Desai said the administration’s long-term agenda is 
paying off amid the increase in imports of goods needed for building out US 
industry. “As trillions of dollars in semiconductor, pharmaceutical, steel and 
other critical manufacturing investments continue materializing,” deficits will 
shrink, he said.

Reshoring computing is a tall order, of course, given the amount of money, 
technical expertise and skilled labor required to kickstart complex industries 
— not to mention the timescale.

It’s not unusual for an investment boom like today’s, driven by a technology 
shock, to suck in imports, according to Fed economists who surveyed recent such 
episodes in the US. They found the resulting deterioration in the 
current-account balance typically unwinds over “several quarters.” But they 
cautioned that this one might last longer.

“The surge in equipment imports driven by AI infrastructure investment shows 
little sign of abating,” Giuseppe Fiori, Colleen Lipa and Erik Nuenninghoff
wrote last month 
<https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/technology-shocks-the-ai-boomandthe-u-s-current-account-20260714.html>
 in a note published by the Fed. That’s “raising the prospect of a more 
significant and prolonged external imbalance.”

There are other areas where AI could help rebalance things, especially over 
the long run. One way to frame the whole strategy is that America will borrow 
from the world (what a trade deficit means) to buy goods and build hardware – 
then reap benefits by selling services like software.

In telecom, computer and information services, the US posted an annual surplus 
above $20 billion through March, roughly double the 2023 level. And the current 
account, as the broadest measure of international trade, includes other 
categories where AI could chip in — by boosting receipts on intellectual 
property, or the profits of US firms overseas.

One problem is that American AI looks like it’ll face stiff competition. Even 
on home turf, some US businesses have been switching to cheaper Chinese models, 
which the administration hasmused about 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/bessent-says-us-will-scrutinize-chinese-ai-models-for-ip-theft?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260808&utm_content=7465>
 restricting.

There’s a national security aspect 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-22/china-s-ai-for-all-offensive-defies-us-containment-playbook?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260808&utm_content=7465>
 here, just as there was when the Trump and Biden administrations pushed allies 
to bar Chinese firms like Huawei Technologies Co. from their telecoms 
infrastructure (a trick that might be harder to pull off with AI).

But the economic aspect is compelling, too. America’s leading AI companies 
have spent a lot of money. They’re going to need a lot of revenue, at home and 
abroad, to make this investment work. Swaths of the financial markets, and a 
big chunk of GDP growth, are at risk if they can’t. They might need some 
protection.
