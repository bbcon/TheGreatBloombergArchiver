AI’s other water cost

By Todd Woody 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AVl1IPrK6vs/todd-woody?cmpid=BBD101426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10065>
 andMark Chediak 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/APj9IOx5Yhk/mark-chediak?cmpid=BBD101426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10065>

Data center developers have started touting new technologies that slash the 
amount of water needed to cool hot-running servers indrought-stressed places 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-27/arizona-s-ai-boom-is-under-threat-by-dwindling-colorado-river?cmpid=BBD101426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10065>
 like Arizona. Those efforts, though, are not addressing the vast majority of 
the water consumed by the AI buildout.
The Stargate AI data center in Abilene, Texas.Photographer: Kyle 
Grillot/Bloomberg
Miles away from multiplying data centers, power plants are now consuming 
enormous volumes of water 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-05/why-do-ai-data-centers-use-so-much-water?cmpid=BBD101426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10065>
 to generate the electricity required to run artificial intelligence, including 
those more efficient cooling systems.

New research indicates that such indirect water consumption is rapidly 
growing. In a recentpaper <https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25437>, University of 
Michigan researchers estimated water used offsite would boost the total annual 
consumption of three large AI data centers planned for the state by 14 billion 
gallons.

“There has been much less attention to this indirect water usage, even though 
that oftentimes is significantly higher than the direct water usage and has 
impacts on watersheds and regional water resources,” saidShaolei Ren 
<https://shaoleiren.github.io/>, a professor at the University of California at 
Riverside whostudies data center water consumption 
<https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03271>.

At Meta Platforms Inc. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/META:US?cmpid=BBD101426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10065>
, one of the only developers to publicly divulge indirect water consumption, 
such use accounted for about 96% of the 21 billion gallons (80 billion liters) 
of water the company consumed in 2024 — nearly a 120% increase from 2021 — 
according to its latestsustainability report 
<https://sustainability.atmeta.com/resources/?utm_source=gemini>.

The remaining 4% was used on site, mostly at data centers, which also account 
for nearly all of Meta’s electricity consumption. “Transparency is a key pillar 
of Meta’s water stewardship program,” a spokesperson said in an email.

Computing-intensive AI data centers can consume 10 times as much electricity 
as conventional facilities, with most of that energy needed to power servers, 
according to researchers. To generate that electricity, fossil-fueled and 
nuclear power plants need large amounts of water to produce steam to run 
turbines.

JPMorgan’s transition rethink

By Alastair Marsh 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AQvVwWuhxcQ/alastair-marsh?cmpid=BBD101426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10065>

JPMorgan Chase & Co. 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/JPM:US?cmpid=BBD101426_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261014&utm_content=10065>
 has updated its approach to financing the energy transition, in a move the 
biggest US bank says is necessary to meet a historic surge in demand.
JPMorgan’s Heather Zichal at the Aspen Ideas: Climate conference in Chicago, 
Illinois, US, on Wednesday, July, 22, 2026.Photographer: Talia Sprague/Bloomberg
The decision, which entails making nuclear power eligible to be included in 
its $1 trillion green finance goal, is part of a broader rethink of the bank’s 
$2.5 trillion sustainable development target, saidHeather Zichal 
<bbg://people/profile/16225660>, who oversees JPMorgan’s center for energy and 
environment as well as its sustainability strategy.

“We don’t just set our strategy and ignore the practical realities of the 
world,” she said in an interview. “The name of the game around energy is 
completely changed.”

The bank has made clear it wants to dominate the financing opportunities 
around electrification, which includes becoming the go-to lender for grid 
projects. In his introduction to JPMorgan’s sustainability report, Chief 
Executive OfficerJamie Dimon <bbg://people/profile/1484062> said energy is a 
key plank of the bank’s $1.5 trillion Security and Resiliency Initiative, which 
he unveiled last year.

“Much of what strengthens energy independence may also advance 
sustainability,” Dimon said. “Efforts such as nuclear power, energy storage and 
grid modernization enhance resilience while helping clients decarbonize.”

Zichal, whose newly expanded role at JPMorgan was part of a reorganization 
announced last month that included the departure of the bank’s global head of 
corporate advisory, says financing a diverse range of energy sources remains a 
commitment. “But at the same time, we have to meet the moment,” she said.

A war-fueled oil and gas crisis combined with insatiable demand for energy to 
power AI data centers means sustainable finance, like every other corner of 
banking, needs to adapt, Zichal said.
