AI’s climate cost

By Matt Day 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AT6ZMF7p5Yc/matt-day?cmpid=BBD081826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7829>
 andMark Chediak 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/APj9IOx5Yhk/mark-chediak?cmpid=BBD081826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7829>

Data center developers are turning to bespoke natural-gas power plants, a 
development that promises todramatically increase carbon emissions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-18/data-center-gas-plants-to-boost-us-power-emissions-by-20?cmpid=BBD081826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7829>
 and make it harder for US technology companies to meet their lofty climate 
goals.

Ninety-nine proposed plants tracked by BloombergNEF would emit about 318 
million metric tons of carbon dioxide annually if run at industry-standard 
rates, according to a Bloomberg News analysis. The entire US electric power 
industry emitted about 1,485 million metric tons of carbon last year, according 
to Energy Information Administration data, meaning one slice of data center 
infrastructure has the potential to lift US power sector emissions by 20%, and 
as much as a third should the new plants run flat out.
Gas turbines at an AI data center in Abilene, Texas.Photographer: Kyle 
Grillot/Bloomberg
The data center building boom has already strained the US electricity system, 
prompting reliability concerns and moratoriums on new project approvals. With 
even greenlit facilities facing yearslong delays connecting to regulated 
electricity grids, data center developers are seeking alternatives. These 
include so-called behind-the-meter projects that can be permitted and built 
without the approval of utilities or the independent system operators charged 
with ensuring grid reliability.

“There is immense, immense pressure on the whole sector to get power, and get 
it fast,” said David Pomerantz, executive director of the Energy and Policy 
Institute, a utility watchdog that promotes renewables. “They’re sort of 
agnostic if it is clean or dirty.”

Not all of the proposed plants are likely to be built. The rush to capitalize 
on AI developers’ seemingly bottomless demand for computing power has produced 
some phantom projects and long-shot pitches.

Bloomberg’s estimate of the likely emissions is based on 126 gigawatts of 
total planned on-site gas generation capacity tracked by BloombergNEF, an 
energy research firm owned by Bloomberg LP. The carbon footprint was calculated 
using a range of usage from an industry average of 60% to 100% for 
round-the-clock deployment, and a gas burn rate of a typical single-cycle gas 
generator. Emissions will vary depending on the use and the fuel-efficiency of 
the generator. A single-cycle model is now one of the most common types planned 
although it’s dirtier than combined-cycle plants that data center developers 
want but are struggling to get owing to a yearslong backlog for turbines.
An aerial view of the natural gas plant under construction in Abilene.
Photographer: Kyle Grillot/Bloomberg
The BNEF data includes projects backed by the leading AI labs, OpenAI and 
Anthropic PBC, upstart data center operators that fashion themselves as AI 
specialists, as well as cloud-computing giants and investor groups seeking 
tenants.

The projects tracked are spread across 22 states, from Alaska to Georgia. More 
than a third of them are in Texas, where ample oil and gas resources and a 
historically forgiving regulatory environment had developers rushing to erect 
data centers as fast as they can be built and powered. (Texas Governor Greg 
Abbott recently announced a pause in data center approvals.)

China’s new export wave

By Karoline Kan 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUiNxJ7IdMI/karoline-kan?cmpid=BBD081826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7829>

Successive heat waves have sent temperatures soaring across Europe 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-18/china-s-gree-haier-hisense-midea-tcl-see-ac-sales-surge-in-europe-heat-waves?cmpid=BBD081826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7829>
 this summer, and wildfires are consuming forests, fields and farms. As those 
calamities boost awareness of — and concern about — the realities of a changing 
climate, people from Seville to Stockholm are finally warming to the idea of 
air conditioning. And that has a quintet of Chinese manufacturers eyeing big 
profits.
Manufacturing the PortaSplit at a Midea factory in Guangzhou.Photographer: 
Liang Xu/Xinhua/Getty Images
But first the brands will have to get Europeans to recognize them. Chinese AC 
producers Gree, Haier, Hisense, Midea and TCL are hardly household names on the 
continent, even though their products have been a growing presence for more 
than a decade.

To raise their profile, the companies are tailoring products to local tastes 
and building deep networks of service providers and retailers. They’ve hired 
European content creators to promote their wares on social media. And, adopting 
a playbook from the likes of Sony, Samsung, and LG before them, they’re 
sponsoring sporting events and signing star athletes to endorsement deals. 
Hisense, for instance, was an official backer of this summer’s World Cup, TCL 
has partnered with the Spanish national football team and Midea has a contract 
with Norwegian footballer Erling Haaland.

Chinese companies control two-thirds of the global market for air 
conditioning, according to researcher Euromonitor International. And they’re 
growing fast, particularly in Europe. Exports of AC units from China to the 
European Union hit $3.8 billion in the first half of 2026, up 43% from a year 
earlier, customs data show. TCL says orders from Europe climbed more than 
two-thirds in the first half from the same period last year, with sales of 
portable units surging by more than 90%. Haier reports its sales in France, 
Germany and the UK more than doubled in June from the same month last year. And 
Midea says AC revenue in four key European countries rose more than 70% in the 
first half.

“Our local teams focus on what users in each market really need,” Zhao Ali, 
Midea’s Europe chief, told Chinese media in July, “then create products for 
that market.”

The ascent of the AC makers mirrors that of industries across China. A decade 
ago, Chinese manufacturers focused largely on their home market or on producing 
parts or finished products sold by big international brands — in air 
conditioning, names such as Carrier, Daikin and Toshiba. But the Chinese 
domestic market has become saturated: In 1995 there were only 8 AC units per 
100 urban households. Today there are 176.

Competing against climate change

By Nadia Neophytou 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AYcroifaGW8/nadia-neophytou?cmpid=BBD081826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7829>

It was the heat wave over the Fourth of July weekend 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-18/marathon-training-now-includes-heat-management-for-runners-as-temperatures-soar?cmpid=BBD081826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260818&utm_content=7829>
 that finally pushed Jen Dimascio-Donohue to hit the “checkout” button on an 
item that had been sitting in her cart for months. After a 7-mile run in 
Massachusetts, with temperatures above 80F by 10 a.m., she jumped into the 
ocean off Cape Cod to cool down. “I came home and ordered it immediately,” she 
says. “I felt, ‘I’ve reached my boiling point. Literally.’”
Runners rest after crossing the finish line in Los Angeles.Photographer: Dania 
Maxwell/Getty Images
“It” was a $234 cooling headband called Omius. Dimascio-Donohue, who’s 
training for the New York City Marathon, had been eyeing it since watching 
Sifan Hassan wear one while winning gold at the 2024 Olympics in Paris. The New 
York-based project manager says the band has “already paid for itself in my 
sanity,” and she’s since worked it into her regular runs, rewetting it every 30 
minutes or so to refresh the cooling effect.

Every marathon season, runners like Dimascio-Donohue strategize about more 
than just how many miles a week to train. They focus on details that can make 
or break race day: which nutrition gels they can tolerate, which shoes work 
best for their feet. Climate change has become a variable they can’t ignore.

New York City’s frequent heat waves, London’s extended dry spell and Hong 
Kong’s record-breaking summer have all brought the kind of high temperatures 
and humidity that make it harder for the body to cool itself. Training through 
miserable weather has long been a rite of passage for marathoners, but today’s 
extremes are affecting training schedules and race days in ways that can affect 
safety and performance.
