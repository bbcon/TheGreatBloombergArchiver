Winning back trust

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>

A decade ago, new college graduate Yassamin Ansari was running a climate 
conference meant to drive US momentum in the fight against climate change, even 
as then presidential-contender Donald Trump threatened to upend the country’s 
progress in combating global warming.

Ansari is still reassuring the world about American climate leadership, but 
now the Democrat from Phoenix, Arizona, is doing it as a lawmaker in Congress 
and one of18 House members 
<https://www.seecinstitute.org/press/press-release-with-a-new-congress-on-the-horizon-18-us-lawmakers-arrive-at-cop31-to-signal-americas-return-to-sensible-climate-policynbsp>
 who traveled to United Nations talks in Turkey to persuade foreign governments 
that the US can reclaim its place in the fight against warming. The Democratic 
delegation’s confidence mission was buoyed by midterm election victories 
putting the party in control of the House and Senate next year.
US Representative Yassamin Ansari speaks during a panel at COP31 in Antalya on 
Nov. 14.Photographer: Laurent Thomet/AFP/Getty Images
“The world has continued to move forward without us, and that is shameful,” 
Ansari said in an interview on the sidelines of the COP31 summit, following two 
days of meetings with foreign counterparts. “We recognize that it will take 
time to earn trust back, and we will work as hard as we can domestically over 
the next two years to deliver results for people.”

The US lawmakers brought a message of hope — with caveats. Democrats’ election 
gains mean they’ll be in a strong position to act as a check on Trump’s 
pro-fossil fuel agenda and ensure US development institutions support clean 
energy. They also hope to advance legislation bolstering renewables. But 
without a veto-proof majority in the House and Senate, it’s unclear how many of 
those measures can be enacted. And the US is set to remain out ofthe Paris 
Agreement <https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/the-paris-agreement> until 
at least January 2029. That will only change if a future president decides to 
reenter the accord and its underlying framework convention.

“We have to exert our leadership and our influence in the United States 
Congress and hold the president accountable,” said Representative Jonathan 
Jackson, a Democrat from Illinois. “We made previous commitments to be a part 
of the Paris accord we’ve got to lean into.”

Before attending COP31, the lawmakers committed to a six-month program of 
briefings and training by SEEC Institute, a nonpartisan, nonprofit group that 
supports the congressional Sustainable Energy and Environment Coalition.

Ansari, however, needed little introduction. She attended her first COP while 
working in the UN Secretary-General’s office in 2015, when a bilateral US-China 
statement of principles helped form the foundation for the landmark Paris 
Agreement. She’s now been to four more.

Ansari’s career, including developing heat relief and electrification 
initiatives as a member of the Phoenix City Council, has given her a deep 
understanding of the internal workings of international climate diplomacy, as 
well as the practical challenges of implementing those agreements on the ground.

At the UN climate talks in Turkey, volatile swings in US politics are further 
straining the hard work of forging world-uniting consensus on the issue. Even 
before Trump twice pulled the US out of the Paris Agreement, the country was 
seen as an unreliable partner by having failed more than two decades ago to 
ratify the underpinningKyoto Protocol 
<https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/the-kyoto-protocol> and partially 
reneging on pledges to steer billions of dollars to a UN climate fund. The 
latest retreat under Trump underscores how much US reliability rests on 
electoral politics.

While Ansari acknowledged that’s been an undercurrent of the conversations in 
Turkey, she said foreign leaders have made clear they want the US back in the 
COP process.

“Despite the whiplash that the United States has caused in this process, we 
are still welcomed, and I don’t feel any sense of resistance or animosity 
toward the United States as an entity,” she added.

India’s low-key COP

By Azman Usmani 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AYPlpafOt_4/azman-usmani?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>

India is hard to spot in exhibition halls at the annual United Nations climate 
talks, but difficult to avoid in its negotiating rooms.

Unlike dozens of other nations with flashy promotional pavilions, the country 
has no dedicated booth at the summit in Antalya, Turkey, and an official 
delegation of about 152 registered attendees is smaller than those of 
Bangladesh, Pakistan or Nepal.

A decision by Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s government in April to withdraw 
its offer to host the UN talks in 2028 and a revised emissions-cutting strategy
seen as lacking in ambition 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-25/india-s-delayed-climate-plans-set-modest-new-emissions-targets?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>
 have led some advocates to conclude that India, the world’s third-largest 
source of greenhouse gas pollution, is quickly retreating from a frontline 
position on climate diplomacy.

Yet, the nation remains an important voice in closed-door deliberations and a 
persistent advocate for the developing world’s demands for more climate finance 
and for industrialized nations to go faster and further in reducing emissions.

And at home, record additions of wind and solar are transforming the roughly 
$3.9 trillion economy’s coal-dominated power grid, with non-fossil fuel sources 
accounting forabout 30% <https://www.bnef.com/insights/40739> of the nation’s 
electricity generation, according to BloombergNEF.

“Developing countries cannot be asked to choose between climate action and the 
legitimate right to development, to decent living standards, health and 
education,” Environment Minister Bhupender Yadav told theCOP31 talks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/latest/cop-climate-summit?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>
 in a speech Wednesday. “Equitable access to the remaining depleted carbon 
budget is non-negotiable.”
Bhupender Yadav in 2021 in Glasgow, Scotland.Photographer: Ian Forsyth/Getty 
Images Europe
Despite India’s low public profile in Turkey, the nation is using its 
influence in negotiations to resist new targets or timetables for climate 
action that would add obligations on developing nations without offering 
commitments from richer economies to deliver additional finance or technology.

India is also at the center of two major disputes at the talks — on the timing 
of the next major global climate-science assessments, and over a proposed goal 
to accelerate electrification.

The country’s approach “should not be confused with disengagement from climate 
diplomacy,” said Nakul Sharma, a senior policy adviser at E3G, a climate think 
tank. “India remains an influential voice in developing country coalitions.”

By Laura Millan

<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>
,Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>
 andLili Pike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXt16hTqABg/lili-pike?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>

Outside of the Antalya talks, there’s a mounting popular backlash against the 
booming AI industry. Community fears about data centers abound: their strain on 
power grids; impact on electricity prices; use of water; and more recently, 
contribution to global warming.

Yet inside the summit, the issue is struggling to win traction, and there’s 
been little discussion of an initiativelaunched last month 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-28/turkey-floats-cop31-climate-pledge-saying-ai-part-of-solution?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>
 on the theme by host country Turkey. The voluntary pledge calls for efforts to 
ensure that the industry’s sustainability concerns are addressed, and AI is 
harnessed to help tackle global warming.

Germany, Brazil and firms including Alibaba and Nokia have backed the plan, 
and by Wednesdaytotal endorsements 
<https://app.powerbi.com/view?r=eyJrIjoiOTAwYzBhZDItM2UwMi00MzU4LThhNTEtMTJjOTQwYzk2MGNkIiwidCI6IjIzZTQ2NGQ3LTA0ZTYtNGI4Ny05MTNjLTI0YmQ4OTIxOWZkMyIsImMiOjl9>
 stood at 90, including 10 governments.

Read More: Which AI Chatbots Have the Highest Emissions? Here’s What We Know 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-08/what-are-ai-s-emissions-energy-use-what-we-know-about-claude-deepseek-openai?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>

While the AI sector has been low profile – of roughly 70,000 registered 
delegates, less than 100 had AI explicitly mentioned in their profiles – those 
who attended have pressed their case for how the technology can help in 
tackling climate change. They argue the sector can drive corporate efficiencies 
to ultimately reduce companies’ carbon impacts and help communities prepare and 
adapt to more frequent extreme weather by improving forecasting.

“The tool of AI is so powerful, it would be tragic if we didn’t use it to save 
climate as well,” Josh Parker, sustainability head at Nvidia, said ona panel 
<https://www.youtube.com/live/WN-VwwL8uic> in Antalya last week, arguing that 
AI is likely to drive net emissions reductions.

Climate campaigners are wary – and point to the long-standing COP lobbying 
efforts by the fossil fuel sector to sway outcomes in their favor. “We are 
seeing a familiar pattern: an industry with a significant environmental 
footprint being presented as part of the climate solution, while the measures 
needed to address that footprint are pushed aside,” said Jill McArdle, a 
campaigner at Beyond Fossil Fuels non-profit.

If COP31 has served as a forum to start airing views about the fast-growing 
industry, it’s likely next year’s talks in Addis Ababa will involve far more 
scrutiny of AI and its impacts.

“AI and energy transition can’t be competing priorities anymore,” said Zeynep 
Güven, a government affairs director at Microsoft in Turkey.

Nepal called for improvements on access to climate finance and for new 
investment in hazard warning systems after the deadly glacial collapse and 
flooding that killed more than 1,400 people.
Damaged properties following flash floods in Nepal’s Dhading district.
Photographer: Manan Vatsyayana/AFP
The nation, one of Asia’s poorest countries, has been left to contend with a 
recovery cost of $5 billion and continues to struggle to secure funding from 
bodies set up to assist with financing,Gita Chaudhary 
<https://dtp.org.au/community/gita-chaudhary/>, Nepal’s minister for 
agriculture, forests and environment, told the annual United Nations climate 
talks.

“We are struggling to access the resources,” Chaudhary said Wednesday at the 
summit in Antalya, Turkey. “This isn’t only a financing gap, it’s a fairness 
gap.”

Ireland became the latest nation to set out a formal roadmap to transition 
away from fossil fuels, joining countries including France, Denmark, Germany 
and Portugal in detailing proposals to overhaul existing energy systems.
The coal-fired Provence Power Station in Gardanne, France in 2022.
Photographer: Jeremy Suyker/Bloomberg
“The worsening effects of extreme weather events demand urgent action, but 
recent geopolitical events have also exposed the economic and strategic risks 
of ongoing fossil fuel dependency,” Ireland’s Minister for Climate, Energy and 
the Environment Darragh O’Brien said at COP31.

Campaign groups say the pledges need to be backed up by commitments to expand 
public finance for renewables, tax polluting sectors and scrap fossil fuel 
subsidies.

Steps away from where COP31 Presidency officials are crafting a decision text 
lies a cavernous sinkhole delegates would do well not to fall into.

The 3D floor installation by Greenpeace, entitled “Don’t Fall for Fossil 
Fuels,” depicts a power plant glowing red and billowing smoke. Nevertheless, it 
looks unlikely that the transition away from oil, gas and coal will appear in 
the final decision at this summit.

Cutting ties between pay and climate

By Ben Elgin 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AP8O5gRPysw/ben-elgin?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>

Corporate America is increasingly stripping carbon metrics from executive pay 
plans.

Just 17.7% of companies in the S&P 500 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/SPX:IND?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>
 tie executive pay to carbon emissions, down from a high of 30% in 2023, 
according to data provided to Bloomberg News by The Conference Board and 
analytics firm ESGAUGE. The retreat accelerated this year, when 40 fewer 
companies in the US stock benchmark included such measures in their pay plans.

The same thing is happening across a broader swath of corporate America. Among 
companies in theRussell 3000 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/RAY:IND?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>
, the share using carbon measures in executive pay has fallen nearly in half, 
from a peak of 10.3% in 2024 to 5.5% this year.

The numbers remain higher than five years ago, when many companies began adding
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2021-03-18/ceo-pay-tied-to-esg-sets-canadian-banks-apart-from-the-crowd?cmpid=BBD111826_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261118&utm_content=11526>
 such metrics to their pay plans.

“Priorities in the US have definitely changed,” says Shira Cohen, associate 
professor of accounting at San Diego State University, whohas studied 
<https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1475-679X.12481> links 
between executive pay and environmental, social and governance (ESG) metrics. 
“Companies appear to be reducing the visibility and explicitness of 
climate-linked pay.”

It’s too early to tell, though, whether this will lead to a pullback in 
corporate emissions-cutting efforts, according to Cohen. “It’s a setback for 
one mechanism for accountability,” she says, “not necessarily proof that we 
have weaker climate action overall.”
