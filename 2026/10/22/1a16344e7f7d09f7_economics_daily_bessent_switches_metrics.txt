Bessent’s Messaging Shift

In his second week in office, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent declared that 
he and President Donald Trump were focused on bringing down10-year Treasury 
yields 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-02-05/bessent-says-he-trump-focus-on-10-year-yields-not-pushing-fed?cmpid=BBD102226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10088>
. In a mission to “get the whole curve down,” the 10-year is the “important 
price to focus on,” he told Bloomberg in February last year.

That’s because the 10-year rate determines mortgages and “long-term capital 
formation,” he explained. “With the president’s policies of energy dominance, 
deregulation and non-inflationary growth, I think that the 10-year is going to
naturally come down 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-02-06/treasury-secretary-scott-bessent-on-doge-taxes-tariffs-china-federal-reserve?cmpid=BBD102226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10088>
,” he said.

Expanding energy supply will help lower inflation, he told Fox Business the 
same week. For working-class Americans, “the energy component for them is one 
of the surest indicators for long-term inflation expectations,” he had said. 
“We’re going to go into a good interest-rate cycle,” he also predicted.

Things haven’t panned out that way, of course. Trump’s war on Iran has seen 
energy prices soar, takingUS inflation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/us-consumer-prices-climb-by-most-since-april-on-fuel-costs?cmpid=BBD102226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10088>
 rates farther away from the Federal Reserve’s 2% target and kickstarting a new 
cycle of interest-rate hikes rather than reductions.

All that has driven up Bessent’s 10-year yield metric, to over 5% from the 
around-4.5% level he inherited. And it’s seen a shift in his messaging. Now, 
when he talks about borrowing costs, he regularly touts that US Treasuries are 
the “best-performing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/bessent-says-buyback-move-was-aimed-at-quelling-market-fever?cmpid=BBD102226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10088>
” major government bond market since Trump took office.

And indeed, from Trump’s inauguration through early this week Treasuries 
handed investors about a 4.6% return, Bloomberg indexes show. German government 
debt has seen a loss of about 1.6%. And Japan’s government securities have 
incurred a whopping loss of more than 9%.

But economists would argue this isn’t a metric that counts for the Treasury 
secretary. Getting bond investors good return on their money is a side issue. 
The point is to marshal the broader economy. And by that measure, Treasuries
aren’t helping 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-13/a-5-treasury-yield-is-raising-new-risks-for-markets-economy?cmpid=BBD102226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10088>
right now.

The jump in yields has taken 30-year mortgage rates up to some 7.4% 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-15/us-mortgage-rates-unchanged-at-7-4-as-housing-market-struggles?cmpid=BBD102226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10088>
. Aside from a few weeks in 2023, that’s the highest since 2000. As Bessent 
said last year, the point is to get yields down — not to ensure investors in 
Treasuries are getting a solid total return. Bonds can offer good returns while 
the economy does poorly. Back in 2011, Treasuries offered a terrific return of 
more than 9%. But GDP only rose 1.6% and the unemployment rate averaged 8.9%.

The Treasury declined to comment. White House spokesman Matthew Middleton said 
the president “has always been clear about temporary market disruptions as a 
result of the conflict in Iran.” Trump remains focused on efforts to cut fraud 
and waste in spending and to spur economic growth to get the USdebt ratio 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-17/us-interest-costs-hit-record-1-trillion-risking-debt-snowball?cmpid=BBD102226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261022&utm_content=10088>
 to GDP “trending in the right direction,” he said.

Need-to-Know Research

In Asia, the baby bust is taking a turn for the worse, according to a report 
by HSBC. The number of births in China, for example, has plunged 50% to just 8 
million in 2025, from a range of 15-17 million between 2004 and 2018. Even in 
emerging markets boasting large, young populations, fertility rates are fast 
declining in India, Indonesia, the Philippines and Vietnam.

“Fewer babies today mean fewer workers tomorrow,” and potentially less output 
and productivity, said economists Frederic Neumann, Ines Lam and Abanti 
Bhaumik. It also means that more and more older people will need to be taken 
care of by a smaller pool contributing to pensions. “This is not some change 
projected in several decades hence… It’s all kicking in now,” they said.

To be sure, the world as a whole will be considered “super-aged” by 2040, 
meaning 20% of the working-age population is 65 years or older. But by then 
much of Asia will also be more aged than the likes of the US or the UK. And 
worse, “not only are they ageing, but they are doing so more quickly than many 
societies elsewhere,” the HSBC economists said.
