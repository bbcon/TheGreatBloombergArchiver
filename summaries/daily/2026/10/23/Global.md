**Energy Markets and the Iran Conflict**
The Strait of Hormuz remains the central node of global energy risk. Brent's rise to $114 reflects both the ongoing US-Iran military conflict — which began in February and has persisted far longer than either Washington or Jerusalem anticipated — and the escalating Houthi campaign against Saudi infrastructure, including the evacuation of Aramco's Dhahran headquarters. Physical prices of some Middle Eastern crudes sold outside the Gulf have softened somewhat as traders grow more adept at routing cargoes through the strait, but the structural premium in global oil prices reflects the absence of any credible near-term de-escalation. Trump has signaled the possibility of additional strikes after the November 3 midterms, and Netanyahu has explicitly framed the goal as regime change in Tehran. Israeli elections on October 27 add a further variable, with polls suggesting no bloc will achieve a parliamentary majority.

**AI, Capital Markets, and Global Monetary Divergence**
The week's most consequential cross-asset development was the simultaneous sell-off in AI-exposed equities and rally in sovereign bonds across multiple markets — a pattern that suggests investors are beginning to question whether AI capital expenditure will generate returns commensurate with the investment cycle. Global hedge funds recorded losses consistent with a major risk-off episode. Central bank policy is diverging in meaningful ways: the BOJ appears to be moving toward December tightening, the PBOC is easing through structural reform, Turkey has begun cutting, and the Fed remains on hold. This divergence is shaping currency and capital flow dynamics globally, though the net direction of those flows — particularly toward or away from emerging markets — remains difficult to read cleanly against the backdrop of elevated oil prices and geopolitical uncertainty.
