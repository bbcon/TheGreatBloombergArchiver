The US-China tech rivalry 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-us-china-ai-race/?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
 leads us now to Egypt. That’s because Cairo wants an AI data center, and 
Huawei — which has had a presence in the north African nation for more than two 
decades — is eager to help.

Washington caught wind and really doesn’t want to see Huawei spread its AI 
chips around the world. So now President Donald Trump’s team is working with 
American companies including Nvidia on a counteroffer.

The Huawei bid is small, at just 2,008 total AI chips, but it could become the 
first known export of Huawei’s Ascend accelerators 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/huawei-egypt-ai-ascend-chips-test-us-tech-diplomacy-nvidia-amd-microsoft?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
 after more than a year of attempts. That would be an early milestone in the 
Chinese tech titan’s efforts to challenge Nvidia.

The bid echoes the playbook Huawei used to become the world’s preeminent 
telecoms-equipment provider. The company started that rise around the early 
2000s with small initial shipments designed to gain a foothold in key markets.

China may get a chance to make its AI chip case with Egypt in person during 
President Xi Jinping’s upcoming visit to Cairo 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/xi-to-make-first-egypt-trip-in-decade-attend-kyrgyzstan-summit?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-08-26/huawei-pitches-egypt-with-ai-chips-data-center-buildout-video?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-08-26/huawei-pitches-egypt-with-ai-chips-data-center-buildout-video?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
If the Egypt project comes to pass, Huawei would become a key AI provider to 
Africa’s second-largest economy — and establish its Ascend semiconductors in 
the Middle East, where the US has aggressively invested in the more lucrative AI
data center markets of the UAE 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-09/uae-s-g42-ai-champion-pushes-on-with-data-center-for-openai?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
 and Saudi Arabia.

The situation is a good illustration of several years of China’s tech progress 
and shortcomings, as well as Washington’s policies and hand-wringing.

Huawei and its main manufacturing partner have been effectively unable to buy 
crucial semiconductor production equipment since Trump’s first term. Washington 
has also worked with allies to tighten the noose since then, with far from 
perfect alignment.

This presented a major challenge for Huawei just as the AI boom was set to 
take off because China doesn’t produce these top-end tools itself. A key 
question has been whether, or when, it will manage to build an indigenous AI 
chip supply chain capable of fulfilling its own needs — and the world’s.

So, what to make of a bid to export just 2,008 chips, a tiny fraction of what 
one US hyperscaler deploys in a week? It depends who you ask.

Last year, when we wrote about Malaysia announcing (and then quickly 
distancing itself from 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-05-20/malaysia-downplays-huawei-deal-as-us-aims-to-curb-china-ai-power?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
) a national AI system with 3,000 of Huawei’s AI chips, reactions in the US 
were divided.

Those skeptical of China’s semiconductor progress, and in favor of tougher 
restrictions from Washington, tended to see a 3,000-chip system as evidence of 
Huawei’s still-fledgling capabilities.

Those who’d been banging the drum about the risks of Huawei’s global ascent — 
in part to urge Trump to loosen the reins on Nvidia’s global sales — said the 
Malaysia project was precisely what they’d been warning about.

The Egypt pitch has drawn a similar response, though with a caveat. There’s no 
denying that Huawei has made tech progress in the past year. The proposed 
export numbers remain small, but the chips are better.

The Trump administration, meanwhile, has a multi-pronged approach to making 
sure that Huawei doesn’t use those better chips to dominate AI infrastructure 
like it did telecoms.

First, it wants to make it easier for Nvidia to sell its accelerators 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-26/nvidia-estimate-topping-forecast-fails-to-wow-investors?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
, which remain the industry standard for training and running AI models. This 
has happened in key places like the UAE and Saudi Arabia, albeit with many 
bumps along the way.

Trump’s team has also warned the world that using Huawei AI chips could 
violate US regulations. This is a big part of why Malaysia downplayed its 
Huawei announcement, though it also caused some problems with Beijing.

Finally, it is promoting American AI throughout the Global South, and using 
government funds to make projects more financially viable for US tech giants. 
This is an ongoing process, with no announced deals yet.

The combination of these strategies varies by market, and Trump’s team is 
largely figuring it out as it goes.
A Huawei system at an AI conference in Shanghai in July.Photographer: Long 
Wei/VCG/AP Photo
In Egypt’s case, shipments of American AI chips have required US permission 
since 2023 and that hasn’t changed under Trump.

But because the country’s data center market is quite small, this hasn’t 
necessarily been a hindrance to Nvidia’s business in the way chip curbs have 
presented a challenge for other parts of the Middle East. It does mean that 
Washington can impose conditions on the sales to Egypt, something Trump’s team 
has done in the Gulf.

It is unclear whether the US administration has invoked its anti-Huawei 
warning in conversations with Cairo, though it definitely lingers in the 
background as the stick to an Nvidia-export carrot.

When I asked a State Department spokesperson about how the Egypt situation 
jibes with the broader AI export promotion program, they touted the 
announcement of that effort but didn’t directly answer more detailed questions, 
such as whether US government financing is on the table.

It’s also taken more than a year to get the program up and running, to the 
frustration of some in the US government and industry.

Huawei is trying to fill the gap.

The Taxman Cometh

No one seems to be immune to China’s global tax hunt.

This time, authorities are going after listed companies 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-25/xi-s-unpredecented-tax-clawback-hammers-chinese-listed-companies?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
, which are getting hit with back-tax bills that are sometimes so large that 
they’re wiping out earnings.

With tax receipts down from a 2023 peak and income from selling land — once a 
cash cow — shrinking, the government is intensifying a campaign to find new 
revenue sources and turning the page on a decade of supporting businesses 
through tax cuts championed by the late Premier Li Keqiang.

More than 100 companies were stuck with clawbacks and charges for delayed tax 
payments totaling around 7.7 billion yuan ($1.1 billion) in the first six 
months of the year.

Among them was Heilongjiang Agriculture, which got smacked with a bill that 
amounted to 120% of its net income in 2025. The listed arm of China’s 
third-largest farming conglomerate is now in line to record a first-half loss 
for the first time in more than two decades as a public company.

Heilongjiang Agriculture was accused of enjoying perks that it wasn’t eligible 
to claim. For others, the alleged misdeeds range from the misapplication of tax 
relief and over-claimed VAT deductions to the revocation of designations that 
enabled qualified companies to pay a favorable rate.

Many public firms have benefited from tax breaks over the past decade. 
Analysis by Citic Securities found that nearly two-thirds of A-share listed 
companies enjoy a preferential corporate income tax rate.

In recent months, authorities have also put a bulls-eye on the nation’s rich, 
announcing plans totax cross-border stock brokerage accounts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-05-25/china-cracks-down-on-illegal-cross-border-trading-video?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
, offshore trusts and overseas insurance policies. The trusts have been used by 
Chinese entrepreneurs and wealthy families forasset protection and succession 
planning 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-31/china-targets-offshore-trusts-in-tax-crackdown-on-ultra-rich?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
.

While those moves have been taken as part of the government’s broader effort 
to strengthen oversight of cross-border wealth and improve tax transparency, 
they have also helpeddiminish the spending appetite of wealthy Chinese 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/luxury-sales-plunge-in-china-as-tax-push-hits-wealthy-shoppers?cmpid=BBD082826_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260828&utm_content=8231>
, hitting luxury shopping, casinos and the Hong Kong stock market.

Sales at the 25 biggest luxury labels in China dropped more than 10% in July, 
coinciding with Beijing’s campaign to reclaim tax revenues and stem capital 
outflows.

Local governments are “in a difficult bind” as they search for a new source of 
revenue, said Jean Oi, a professor of China studies at the National University 
of Singapore.

The government is “trying to get everybody to pay up and follow the rules, but 
is it enough? I really worry.”

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

Himalayas but what is clear so far is thata huge chunk of a glacier collapsed

The disaster struck a chord with China’s social media users, prompting an

“With communication disrupted on the high-altitude border and along roads,

who are missing be safe, and may those who went against the tide be unharmed,” 
the post added,referring to rescuers

The high-altitude region — with a large population spanning parts of China, 
India, Nepal and several other countries across the Tibetan plateau — is 
warming at nearly double the global average pace.

That is leading to rapid glacial retreat

the previous decades, according to a 2019 study.

“The frequency of ice and rock avalanches in the high mountains and deep 
valleys surrounding the Qinghai-Tibet plateau is increasing significantly due 
to climate change, requiring us to have more comprehensive countermeasures,” 
wrote a popular science blogger.

Another Weibo user wrote that “as the climate shifts, let us remain humble 
before nature and pray for safety ahead.”
