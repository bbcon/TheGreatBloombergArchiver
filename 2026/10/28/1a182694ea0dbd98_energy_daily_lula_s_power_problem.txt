Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, Rio 
de Janeiro-based reporter Peter Millard looks at a key challenge for Brazil’s 
reelected president: the country’s strained electricity system.

Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva prides himself on his 
environmental credentials. Renewable-energy capacity expanded during his latest 
term and the country last year hosted — at great expense — theUN Climate Change 
Conference in the Amazon <https://unfccc.int/cop30https://unfccc.int/cop30>.

And yet Lula’s legacy on clean energy is in question as he heads into a 
surprisefourth term 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-25/lula-wins-a-record-fourth-term-in-brazil-in-a-surprise-comeback?cmpid=BBBXT102826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10636>
.

That’s because Brazil can’t get all of that booming wind and solar power 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-29/brazil-s-rooftop-solar-boom-tests-power-grid-stability-during-world-cup?cmpid=BBBXT102826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10636>
 to where it’s needed. It has failed to build sufficient transmission lines and 
hasn’t added enough batteries to store excess electricity.

The lack of infrastructure is increasingly leading to “curtailment,” when 
clean energy producers are told to reduce generation because grids can’t cope. 
That’scrimped revenues 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-06-25/brazil-s-green-energy-industry-is-falling-victim-to-its-own-success?cmpid=BBBXT102826_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261028&utm_content=10636>
 for many in the sector, while some also cite high interest rates as a key 
source of their struggles.
President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva speaks during a press briefing at the 
Announcement Ceremony for World Environment Day at the Planalto Palace on June 
10, 2026, in Brasília.Photographer: Ton Molina/Getty Images South America
Earlier this year, Lula’s government did make an effort to fortify the grid. 
In amajor auction 
<https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/brazil-energy-auction-results>, it 
secured as much as 19 gigawatts of electricity capacity from thermal and 
hydropower stations, equivalent to 19 traditional nuclear reactors.

That will provide more back-up for periods of high demand or scant supply. But 
many of the projects are expected to use natural gas; none will help store the 
wind and solar power that’s being wasted.

To be clear, the problem for the country isn’t too much renewable power but 
more a failure to find a home for the electrons, saysBen Backwell 
<https://www.gwec.net/meet-the-team/ben-backwell>, who heads the Global Wind 
Energy Council, a trade association. The solar buildout, he says, happened in 
an “uncoordinated way.”

Renewables investment peaked in the second half of 2023 at $13 billion, 
according to BloombergNEF. In this challenging landscape, it hasn’t hit $10 
billion in the five half-year periods since.

Lula won’t be able to solve the curtailment problem quickly. Transmission 
lines, after all, take years to develop and build. An auction for big batteries 
is scheduled for December, but it’s unlikely to lead to an appreciable change.

—Peter Millard, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Ukraine could make it through the heating season without importing gas, 
supported by higher inventories and domestic production, according to
BloombergNEF <https://about.bnef.com/>. BNEF estimates the country would end 
winter with stockpiles around 10% full without imports, assuming consumption 
remains in line with the 2022-25 average and production holds at last year’s 
levels. Severe output losses due to the war with Russia remain the main risk.
