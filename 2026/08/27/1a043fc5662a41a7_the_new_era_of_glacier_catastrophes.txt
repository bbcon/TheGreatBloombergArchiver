What triggered Nepal’s deadly flood?

By Lili Pike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXt16hTqABg/lili-pike?cmpid=BBD082726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8233>
,Lou Del Bello 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWe3VxBwoBc/lou-del-bello?cmpid=BBD082726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8233>
 andLaura Millan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASzdyi2b6bE/laura-millan?cmpid=BBD082726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8233>

Scientists are pointing to a glacier collapse as the cause 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-27/nepal-flash-floods-what-causes-them-and-why-the-country-is-so-vulnerable?cmpid=BBD082726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8233>
 of a devastating flash flood that killedat least 350 people 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-27/deadly-nepal-flood-kills-at-least-157-with-india-on-high-alert?cmpid=BBD082726_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260827&utm_content=8233>
 and left many more missing near Nepal’s border with China, a mountainous 
region that’s increasingly imperiled by the effects of man-made climate change.

Initial assessments based on seismic and satellite data, as well as videos of 
the event, indicate that a very large section of a glacier detached and 
collapsed. That in turn caused seismic waves equivalent to a 5.2 magnitude 
earthquake, according to the US Geological Survey. Ice from the glacier may 
then have blocked the Lhende Khola river, causing water to quickly accumulate 
and ultimately surge downstream.

“What we’ve certainly confirmed – there was a large ice avalanche, probably a 
mixture of ice and rock, which detached and then became fluid and then 
transformed into this devastating flood,” said Simon Allen, a research 
associate at the University of Zurich who studies glacial hazards in the 
Himalayas.

Nepal is particularly vulnerable to flash floods because of its mountainous 
terrain and rapidly melting glaciers. Such events are becoming more frequent as 
global warming accelerates the ongoing thaw in the Himalayas, an area that 
holds more snow and ice than any place on Earth besides the Arctic and 
Antarctica. The development has a cascading effect — from mudslides to water 
scarcity — for the nearly 2 billion people living downstream from Himalayan-fed 
rivers, according to the United Nations.

Himalayan glaciers melted 65% faster between 2010 and 2020 than during the 
previous decade, the UN said, while the risk of glacial lake-related flooding 
across the broader Hindu Kush Himalaya region is expected to roughly triple by 
the end of the century.

“When you put together all the physical understanding that the glaciers are 
retreating, permafrost is thawing, it certainly adds up,” said the University 
of Zurich’s Allen. Experts are concluding “that we are seeing and we are 
expecting these events to become more frequent,” he said.

While a definitive cause of the Aug. 26 floods has yet to be established, 
analysis so far indicates the snout of a glacier at an elevation of roughly 
5,200 meters broke away and began to make a steep descent to the valley floor, 
melting ice and gathering sediment as it did so, said Dan Shugar, a 
geomorphologist and an associate professor at the University of Calgary. The 
mountains have been shrouded in clouds, meaning the precise chain of events is 
likely to be better understood once satellites can do a clearer examination.
A flooded section of a highway in Nuwakot, Nepal on Aug. 26.Photographer: 
Sanjit Pariyar/NurPhoto/Getty Images
“It operated like an inland tsunami, traveling nearly 170 kilometers (106 
miles),” and impacting a major highway, said Ruth Gamble, director of La Trobe 
Asia and lead author ofRivers of the Asian Highlands: from Deep-Time to the 
Climate Crisis. “It is a popular route for international tourists travelling 
from Kathmandu to Lhasa.”

The high-altitude region is warming nearly twice as fast as the global 
average, with higher temperatures leading to a rapid glacial retreat. Glaciers 
across the globe have been shrinking every year for more than three decades, 
according to the World Glacier Monitoring Service. The recent acceleration in 
ice loss leaves no doubt that global warming caused by man-made emissions of 
greenhouse gases into the atmosphere is a leading cause, according to the 
UN-backed research group.

“I really hope this will be a turning point,” Allen said. “That there will be 
more focus, more emphasis now on reducing the risks for these communities.”

The World’s Mountains Are Ticking Time Bombs

From the Apostle Paul to Ira Gershwin, mountains have for millennia been a 
symbol of immovable stability. A warming planet is undermining that, leaving 
landslides, floods, and avalanches in its wake. The apocalyptic scenes after 
Wednesday’s flash floods on the border between Nepal and China’s Tibet region 
are the latest example of the devastation that can result.

Global emissions tick up

0.2%

Emissions increase for the first half of 2026, compared with the same period 
last year

Greenhouse gas emissions remained almost flat for the first six months of this 
year, according toClimate Trace 
<https://climatetrace.org/news/climate-trace-data-show-marginal-increase-in-global-emissions-in-the-first-half-of-2026>
, an independent group monitoring emissions, as the war in Iran and the closure 
of the Strait of Hormuz disrupted oil and gas markets.

Emissions from China and the US declined slightly, while India and Brazil saw 
large increases, effectively offsetting gains made by the world’s two largest 
economies. Emissions from the power, manufacturing and shipping sectors 
declined, while road transport emissions rose.
