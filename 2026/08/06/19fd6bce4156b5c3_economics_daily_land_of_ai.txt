‘Lord Hynix’

In a recent episode of Saturday Night Live Korea 
<https://www.chosun.com/english/national-en/2026/04/28/ECGBNNPSKZCDBLWCXZFVRDRHNY/>
, a salesperson at a luxury boutique dismisses a poorly dressed customer, until 
the man unzips his jacket to reveal an SK Hynix company vest underneath. The 
clerk immediately changes her tone, greeting him as “Lord Hynix.”

The joke says a lot about how the global race to build data centers powering 
AI is reshaping South Korea’s economy and society and fuelingmassive swings 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/stock-loving-korean-president-under-fire-over-wild-market-swings?cmpid=BBD080626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7368>
 in the stock market. We wrote about theK-Shaped nature 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-07-03/how-the-ai-boom-is-carving-a-k-shape-into-asian-economies?cmpid=BBD080626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7368>
 of the boom recently in this newsletter, so today we’ll focus on some of the 
broader social implications.
SK Hynix Inc. 12-layer HBM4E memory chips on a LPDDR5X CAMM2 memory module 
arranged at the company’s office in Seongnam, South Korea, on Friday, July 24, 
2026.Photographer: SeongJoon Cho/Bloomberg
Starting with dating. As we examine in this feature story 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-08-06/ai-sk-hynix-samsung-rewire-south-korea-s-careers-dating-and-culture?cmpid=BBD080626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7368>
 for Bloomberg Markets Magazine, the AI boom is creating a new social elite 
with chip workers at SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics vaulting toward the top. 
With some set for bonuses of as much as $400,000, all of a sudden they’re in 
demand, matchmakers say.

The shake up is reaching classrooms, too. For decades, Korea’s brightest 
students pursued medicine. In the 2026 admissions cycle, it was harder to get 
into five semiconductor programs backed by Samsung and SK Hynix than those in 
the natural sciences at Seoul National University, the country’s equivalent of 
Harvard University, according to Jongro Academy, a private college-preparatory 
institute that tracks publicly available admissions data.

Seunghyun Jeong, 25, is betting his future on chips. After graduating with an 
electrical engineering degree, he enrolled in a semiconductor training program 
jointly run by KAIST, a Korean sciences and technology university, and Hwaseong 
City. He doesn’t hesitate to say where he’d want to work.

“I’d prefer SK Hynix,” he says. “From what we’ve heard, the performance 
bonuses are better.” Yet even as he tries to ride the AI wave, Jeong is 
mindful. “I am optimistic about the industry,” he says. “But we still have to 
think about what happens when this boom eventually cools.”

While export numbers suggest there’s no let up in demand for the advanced 
memory chips, investors have been on a wild ride of late — which is backfiring 
on President Lee Jae Myung who haschampioned stock investing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/stock-loving-korean-president-under-fire-over-wild-market-swings?cmpid=BBD080626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7368>
 over property as a way to take steam out of elevated real estate prices.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-06-25/korea-s-stock-mania-warning-video?cmpid=BBD080626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7368>
As in much of the world, AI is roiling Korea’s cultural industries too. K-pop 
bands such as BTS and shows like Squid Game have been an economic boon before 
the meteoric rise of large language models. Music, TV, films, video games and 
other cultural exports generated 161 trillion won in revenue and more than $15 
billion in exports last year. They employ about 690,000 workers, more than 
double the combined number at Samsung and SK Hynix.

Korea’s film industry crossed a milestone this summer with the commercial 
release of its first AI-generated feature films, and more AI and hybrid 
productions are scheduled to premiere in theaters later this year.

“Just as ‘silent,’ ‘color’ and ‘digital’ eventually disappeared from how we 
describe movies, AI will also be dropped,” says Cho Yang-il, the AI adviser to 
this year’s Bucheon International Fantastic Film Festival.

Need-to-Know Research

Among the many parallels 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-06-27/why-china-s-economic-stagnation-needs-its-own-name-new-economy?cmpid=BBD080626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7368>
 between China today and Japan in the past — from a property-market crash, 
fiscal deterioration and population decline to sustained weak consumer 
sentiment — perhaps the most troubling for the rest of the world is Beijing’s 
large trade surpluses.

“The global implications are amplified by China’s scale. Japan’s surplus 
peaked at 0.5% of world GDP in 1986 and has declined consistently,” Richard 
Yetsenga, chief economist at ANZ Bank, wrote in a note Wednesday. “Conversely, 
China’s surplus has doubled from around 0.5% to 1.0% over the past six years. 
Put another way, China’s exports are 15% of the world total, while its imports 
are only 9%”

“China’s external surplus will shrink only when it saves less and/or consumes 
more,” Yetsenga said. “Neither seems likely until its hesitation around using 
substantial stimulus dissipates.” Perhaps alarmingly, Yetsenga noted that 
“Japan’s Abenomics only emerged in 2012, some 21 years after the 1991 peak” in 
the nation’s economy. Meantime, trade, tariffs andexchange rates 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-08-01/bessent-misses-chance-to-coordinate-with-a-china-wary-europe?cmpid=BBD080626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260806&utm_content=7368>
 are likely to remain points of geopolitical tension, he said.
