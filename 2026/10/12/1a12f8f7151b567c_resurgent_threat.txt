Saudi Arabia has spent years trying to wind down its war against Yemen’s 
Houthis. Now, as its warplanes back a renewed offensive, attacks on Riyadh are 
showing how hard the group is to defeat.

An attack on the capital’s main airport Saturday killed 12 people and injured 
309. Itwas hit again 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-11/saudi-arabia-s-main-riyadh-airport-comes-under-further-attack?cmpid=BBD101226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9966>
 yesterday andmissiles were intercepted 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/houthis-deal-saudi-arabia-its-deadliest-airport-strikes-yet?cmpid=BBD101226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9966>
 overnight. Hundreds of flights have been grounded, schools have moved online 
and offices in the financial district have shifted to remote work.

The Saudi-led coalition blames the Houthis, who haven’t claimed Saturday’s 
strike but have warned offurther assaults 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/why-saudi-arabia-s-aim-of-defeating-yemen-s-houthis-is-a-tall-order?cmpid=BBD101226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9966>
 on the kingdom’s airports.

Saudi warplanes are backing Yemeni government forces seeking to reverse a 
Houthi advance 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-05/yemen-says-it-s-retaken-mocha-as-battle-for-bab-el-mandeb-looms?cmpid=BBD101226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9966>
 near Bab el-Mandeb, a critical shipping chokepoint at the southern entrance to 
the Red Sea. With their latest hits, the militants appear determined to 
pressure Riyadh into halting its air campaign.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-12/what-houthi-airport-attacks-mean-for-oil-market-trump-video?cmpid=BBD101226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9966>

The problem for Saudi Arabia is that its adversary has emerged from years of 
fighting with formidable capabilities. Despite repeated bombing campaigns, the 
Houthis retain Iranian-designed ballistic missiles and cheap attack drones 
capable of striking deep inside the country.

For Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, the timing is especially damaging. 
Riyadh is preparing to host its flagship Future Investment Initiative, where it 
will court global investors. Executives from BP and Shell have already pulled 
out of a major energy conference in the capital.

Riyadh faces growing pressure to hit back harder. But an intensified campaign 
could invite further attacks on the kingdom withouteliminating the Houthi threat
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/reclusive-houthi-leader-wreaks-havoc-from-his-yemen-stronghold?cmpid=BBD101226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9966>
.

The escalation also presents difficult choices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-11/houthi-attacks-on-saudi-arabia-raise-risk-of-wider-war?cmpid=BBD101226_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9966>
 for its allies. An American was among those killed Saturday, and President 
Donald Trump says the US may join strikes against the Houthis. Turkey and 
Pakistan, which recently signed a defense pact with Saudi Arabia, face 
questions over how far they’re prepared to go in its defense.

Ultimately, the Saudi leaders may conclude that the only path to peace is more 
war. — Peter Martin
