A Japan Double-Whammy?

The Federal Reserve policy decision isn’t the only one keenly awaited 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-09-07/fed-latest-warsh-s-speech-contrasts-with-new-york-fed-president-s-take?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
 next week. The Bank of Japan’s Sept. 18 decision is another with particularly 
high stakes, after a barely concealedpressure campaign 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-02/bessent-leaves-boj-in-no-win-situation-over-japan-rate-hikes?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
 from US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent in favor of raising interest rates.

Some market participants assumed an unwritten quid pro quo for the Treasury’s 
unusual joint intervention to prop up the Japanese yen on July 31, which might 
be further reinforced by remarks overnight. Bessent, speaking in Texas,dared 
traders 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/bessent-dares-traders-to-bet-against-yen-i-am-the-house-now?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
 to test his resolve by declaring: “you can bet against me if you want.”

The “quo” for such support would namely be an acceleration in the BOJ’s 
rate-hiking tempo which is now largely expected.Bloomberg reported 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/boj-is-said-to-favor-quarter-point-hike-flexible-on-future-path?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
 last week that Japan’s central bank was leaning toward a quarter-point move 
Sept. 18.

A stronger — or at least, less-weak — yen would help Japan contain inflation, 
and temper a surge in the cost of imported energy since the Iran war broke out. 
For Bessent, a stable yen would free Tokyo from having to intervene with 
operations that involveselling US Treasuries 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-06/japan-likely-sold-treasuries-to-fund-record-yen-intervention?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
. Given that Bessent lately has focused on interveningto buy Treasuries 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/bessent-s-fever-quelling-debt-buybacks-put-wall-street-on-edge?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
, that would be welcome.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-09-09/bessent-tells-yen-traders-i-am-the-house-now-video?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
Lately, the yen has been cooperating. Japan’s currency climbed more than 2% 
last week, despite no obvious sign of intervention. Some traders have been
focusing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/yen-s-103-billion-shorts-risk-unraveling-below-155-jpm-says?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
 on the potential for Japan to take a page out of an old playbook.

Back in 2014, when a too-strong yen was still a concern, optimism over Japan’s 
Abenomics reflation project was waning, due in part due to a sales-tax hike 
that hadwalloped 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2014-09-01/japan-auto-sales-fall-to-lowest-in-three-years-on-tax-increase?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
 consumer spending. Then, financial markets were given a double-jolt that 
helped turn things around.

The BOJ unveiled a surprise expansion in its monetary-easing campaign. The 
same day, Nov. 1, the nation’s giant Government Pension Investment Fund 
announced that it would double its allocation to the stock market — a massive
vote of confidence 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2014-10-31/abenomics-revived-as-boj-pension-fund-spur-global-stock-rally?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
 in riskier assets. The yen’s move in response still stands out on an 
historical chart. (For the record, the GPIF denied it had collaborated with the 
BOJ back then, a detail traders largely set aside.)

Today, the GPIF is again looking at its allocations. The $2 trillion fund 
manager gathered last month in anunusual meeting 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/gpif-still-examining-if-allocation-review-needed-minister-says?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
 to discuss its portfolio. What market participants are on watch for is any 
shift toward government bonds, after Japanese yields recently jumped to the 
highest in decades.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s government has given signals 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-07-27/from-us-to-japan-policymakers-aim-to-get-capital-moving-where-they-want?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
 that an allocation shift might be a good idea. For his part, Bessent has 
expressed concern more than once since he took office about spillovers of 
volatility from the Japanese bond market to US Treasuries. So even if a GPIF 
reallocation moved some money out of the US, it might still be in Washington’s 
interest.

A twin announcement of a BOJ rate hike and a GPIF allocation adjustment could 
offer a one-two punch thatboosts the yen 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/japan-s-rising-yields-stir-debate-over-growing-repatriation-risk?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
. Chris Turner at ING says, indeed, “global macro accounts are speculating on 
some kind of grand bargain,” this month or next, between the BOJ and GPIF. 
Something to keep on watch for.

Need-to-Know Research

Artificial intelligence may offer hopes for greater economic productivity, but 
it’s not helping when it comes to training up the future workforce. That’s what 
the OECD suggests.

A flagship assessment of global education released this week 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-08/school-students-who-use-ai-get-worse-test-scores-oecd-warns?cmpid=BBD090926_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260909&utm_content=8671>
 by the Paris-based club of industrialized countries found that students who 
don’t use AI chatbots for schoolwork are outperforming those who do.

The results provide some of the widest-ranging evidence yet that a technology 
on which economies are pinning hopes for a transformation of growth could be 
hurting children’s training.

“In the same way that we do not become fit by watching sports but by doing 
sports, learning does not occur through the consumption of content, but as a 
productive cognitive struggle of the mind with new material,” wrote OECD 
Director for Education and Skills Andreas Schleicher.
