Monetary Policy and the Communication Breakdown

The dominant US macro story of the period is not the data itself but the damage done by clumsy official communication. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's opaque July 29 press conference was followed by a hawkish Jackson Hole speech in late August, cementing a rate hike last month — the first in three years. Treasury Secretary Bessent compounded the dislocation by warning of a bond market "fever" and declaring "I am the house now," which markets interpreted as signaling further tightening was imminent. Fed funds futures at their peak priced a terminal rate near 4.86%, roughly a full percentage point above where expectations stood in late July. Jefferson's Thursday remarks — that the Fed may need more time — triggered a 21bp repricing lower in the October 2027 Fed funds contract, with the 10-year yield dropping 12bp intraday. Core PCE at 3.0% and trimmed-mean at 2.3% offer no obvious inflation justification for the market's most extreme hawkish pricing.

Equity and Credit Markets

The S&P 500 index level flatters a deteriorating picture underneath. Small-caps, banks, and utilities have been materially pressured by the rate backup since the Fed's hike last month. The AI complex continues to provide index-level support: Broadcom is arranging $60 billion in financing for AI chip infrastructure benefiting Anthropic, while Amazon is exploring off-balance-sheet structures for $8 billion in Nvidia chips. Paramount Skydance's $52 billion leveraged debt deal — the largest Hollywood buyout ever — immediately traded below par, with eight-year junk bonds at 96 cents on the dollar, generating over $100 million in day-one losses for investors and reflecting broader credit market caution. ---

China: Stimulus Underwhelms

Beijing rolled out its most significant stimulus effort since September 2024, combining mortgage subsidies, expanded central bank lending for infrastructure and technology, and pledges of more targeted fiscal support via interest-rate subsidies and deployment of unused local government bond quotas. The package appears calibrated to defend the official 4.5%-5% growth target rather than address underlying demand weakness. First-half GDP of 4.7% comfortably met the target, but Q2 decelerated sharply, and broad government expenditure fell 3.5% in the first eight months — consistent with the historic decline in fixed-asset investment that began in mid-2025. Tax revenues, meanwhile, rose 6.6% in the first eight months — more than double the full-year projection — reflecting aggressive collection from wealthy individuals and corporates, including a major back-tax campaign and new rules on offshore trusts. The structural diagnosis remains unchanged: China's constraint is demand for credit, not its cost.

US-China Trade

Following last week's Trump-Xi summit, both sides agreed to tariff reductions on approximately $30 billion in two-way trade. The scope is narrow — 77 US product entries versus 1,619 Chinese items — and excludes soybeans, the single largest US agricultural export to China, which Beijing appears to be preserving as political leverage ahead of US midterm elections. ---

France: From Managed Decline to Acute Stress

The OAT-Bund spread approaching 1.5 percentage points reflects a market that has stopped treating France as a routine muddle-through credit. The proximate trigger was the global bond sell-off originating from Fed policy missteps, but French spreads have now decoupled from that dynamic — widening even as US yields rallied Thursday. The 2027 budget proposal, unveiled this week, targets a deficit reduction of approximately €54 billion, but the government's economic watchdog immediately flagged overly optimistic growth assumptions. With presidential elections next spring and hard-left and hard-right candidates polling strongly, the political backdrop undermines confidence in fiscal consolidation. The ECB's Transmission Protection Instrument remains the structural backstop, though its deployment would be politically sensitive, particularly as President Lagarde's tenure may be shortened. European equities have now surrendered nearly all gains recorded since President Trump's inauguration.

Brazil: Election Overhang

The first round of Brazil's presidential election takes place Sunday, with President Lula and right-wing challenger Flávio Bolsonaro deadlocked. If neither candidate secures an outright majority, a runoff follows October 25. In the final campaign stretch, Lula reinforced the Bolsa Família social program and banned online gambling — the latter aimed at addressing elevated household debt concerns. The electoral outcome carries material implications for Brazilian fiscal policy, the trajectory of the real, and commodity sector investment, particularly in agriculture and mining. Markets will be closely watching first-round vote shares for signals on runoff dynamics.

Hormuz: The Structural Supply Constraint

The Strait of Hormuz remains the central node of global energy risk. Tanker captains are navigating the waterway at night, without lights or radio, using coastal landmarks and lighthouses for navigation — a first-hand account illustrating the fragility of flows that once represented approximately one-fifth of global LNG supply. Japan's Jera, the world's largest LNG buyer, does not expect Qatari supply to normalize in the near term. The IEA's upward revision of the 2026 supply deficit to 1.7 million barrels per day — alongside similar revisions from the EIA and BloombergNEF — reflects the cumulative impact of Hormuz restrictions and Saudi Red Sea export disruptions. European nations are reportedly in discussion with the Trump administration about a coordinated strategic reserve release to address diesel shortages, with a proposed release of 100 million barrels of crude and diesel under consideration at a G7 call.

Global Rate Contagion and Monetary Policy Divergence

The week's defining cross-regional theme is how a US-originated bond sell-off — driven by Fed and Treasury communication missteps rather than fundamentally worse inflation data — has transmitted asymmetrically across sovereign markets. France has absorbed the sharpest damage. Japan and Australian sovereign bonds rallied as haven flows intensified. The dollar is on track for a third consecutive weekly gain. Global credit markets are beginning to show strain: record corporate issuance, persistent inflation, and geopolitical premia are eroding the asset class's safe-haven appeal. The tension between central banks that have resumed hiking cycles and fiscal authorities managing large deficits is the defining macro fault line of this period.
