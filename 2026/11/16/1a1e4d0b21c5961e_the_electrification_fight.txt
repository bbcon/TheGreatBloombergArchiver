Everything’s on the table

By Jennifer A Dlouhy 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ASlQFjfALa0/jennifer-a-dlouhy?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>
,John Ainger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AS5AOj_Ky2g/john-ainger?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>
, andLili Pike 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXt16hTqABg/lili-pike?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>

Organizers helming annual global climate talks advanced a sprawling draft 
decision text that covers contentious issues from curbing fossil fuels to 
electrifying economies, indicating tough negotiations will be required to reach 
any agreement.

The initial 18-page draft 
<https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/dt_cop31_cover_decision.pdf?download>
 declaration includes ideas on tackling global warming and its effects that 
have previously drawn deep opposition among negotiators, or are seen as 
scientifically and politically unfeasible.

“There are elements in this draft that not all parties will agree on. That is 
a given,” Chris Bowen, the Australian energy and climate minister leading 
negotiations at COP31 in Antalya, Turkey, told reporters Monday. “What we’ll be 
concentrating on in coming days are those issues where people can find common 
ground.”
Attendees at the COP31 climate conference in Antalya, Turkey, on Friday, Nov. 
13, 2026.Photographer: Chris Ratcliffe/Bloomberg
Energy and environment ministers, including from Japan, Germany and Saudi 
Arabia, will be on hand to inject more political heft into negotiations that 
are scheduled to be completed by Friday — though almost always continue into 
overtime to seek a deal.

The draft document is expansive, covering some 144 paragraphs, an 
all-encompassing approach that puts off tough decisions until later this week. 
“It is incredibly long and the approach looks to be putting it all in and 
letting parties fight for the trade-offs,” said Cosima Cassel, program lead for 
climate diplomacy and geopolitics at the E3G consultancy.

The initial draft text calls for nations to contribute to efforts to raise 
electricity’s share of global final energy demand to 35% by 2035, from just 
over 20% currently. Turkish organizers of the summit had already gathered 
support for a voluntary pledge, and are seeking to include the target in a 
final declaration, which would give it the backing of nearly 200 nations.

However, the Like-Minded Developing Countries bloc, which represents two dozen 
nations including China, India and Bangladesh, has made clear it will not 
support any recognition of the pledge in the final text, according to people 
familiar with the matter who asked for anonymity to detail private negotiations.

Some nations have been resistant to new top-down targets; others are concerned 
the plan doesn’t appropriately reflect differing national circumstances.

Representatives of the negotiating bloc didn’t respond to requests for comment.

Reinventing China’s factories

By Lili Pike 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXt16hTqABg/lili-pike?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>

For decades the world has run on goods pumped out of Chinese factories, from 
smartphones to solar panels, and those factories have run on coal. Now 
officials in Beijing are aiming to not just manufacture the world’s green 
products but also make manufacturing itself green – without sabotaging a sector 
that generates about a quarter of GDP.

Their proposed solution: the zero-carbon industrial park. To better manage 
pollution and boost output, China has long clustered factories into vast 
industrial zones; imagine a small city dedicated entirely to theproduction of 
socks <https://www.gojilin.gov.cn/2024/01/10/c_12124.htm>. Now these industrial 
behemoths are getting a low-carbon makeover, trading fossil fuels for wind and 
solar power.
Solar panels at Envision’s Ordos industrial park.Photographer: Qilai 
Shen/Bloomberg
The first ones sprouted up in China about five years ago, and last year, the 
growing trend went national, with the central governmentapproving 
<https://zfxxgk.ndrc.gov.cn/web/iteminfo.jsp?id=20579&> 52 official zero-carbon 
industrial park pilots. When Chinese leaders released theirfive-year plan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-06/china-s-five-year-plan-downplays-solar-after-rapid-deployment?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>
 in March, itincluded a goal 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-09/china-climate-plan-pushes-renewables-in-factories-data-centers?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>
 of building 100 by 2030. Together with projects undertaken at the provincial 
and corporate level, hundreds of these industrial park experiments are slated 
for development across China.

The movement marks the beginning of a deeper phase of China’s decarbonization 
drive. The number-two economy has already plucked many of the low-hanging, 
low-carbon fruits, withelectric vehicles 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-03-23/china-s-ev-boom-sees-byd-geely-leapmotor-rewriting-global-auto-industry-rules?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>
 and renewables now pervasive, but at athird 
<https://rmi.org/app/uploads/dlm_uploads/2026/01/rmi-january-2026-accelerating-chinas-green-transition-through-zero-carbon-industrial-parks.pdf>
 of the country’s total emissions, industry looms large as the next target. If 
all of the country’s more than 15,000 industrial parks were to meet the new 
national standards, it would yield annual emissions reductions the size of 
India’s carbon footprint, according to the Rocky Mountain Institute.

“For China’s goal of carbon neutrality 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-05/china-aims-to-cut-carbon-emissions-per-unit-of-gdp-17-by-2030?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>
 before 2060, the real test is whether these parks deliver verified emissions 
reductions and affordable solutions that can spread,” said Kevin Tu, a 
non-resident fellow at Columbia University’s Center on Global Energy Policy.

China isn’t the only country where high-emission industries are starting to go 
green — hundreds of individual decarbonized plants are operating or have been 
financed across the world, according toMission Possible Partnership 
<https://tracker.missionpossiblepartnership.org/>. A key goal of the UN climate 
conference convening in Turkey is to push that momentum forward, by getting 
nations to sign on to a pledge to rapidly increase their electrification rate. 
But the economic giant stands out in its effort to create a nationwide program 
and because of the sheer scale of its manufacturing base — and the 
corresponding emissions.

The first batch of China’s test subjects has just a few short years before 
deadlines ranging from 2027 to 2030 to meet the national targets. The core 
requirement isreducing carbon emissions 90% 
<https://www.ndrc.gov.cn/xxgk/jd/jd/202507/t20250708_1399064.html> from the 
national industrial park average. It’s a significant drop if not quite the 
“zero-carbon” in the program’s official title. Another important indicator is 
reaching 90% green power consumption, including sourcing at least half of that 
power locally.

Chinese businesses that got a head start in building zero-carbon parks, like 
Envision Group, one of China’s leading clean technology companies, are now 
serving as blueprints. The critical ingredient to their success, both 
economically and environmentally, has been building direct power lines to 
capture abundant wind and solar locally and turn them into cheap electricity.

“At the very beginning, this model was not well recognized. So Envision, we 
put a lot of our own money into the first one,” said Alex Sun, the company’s 
chief sustainability officer. “But nowadays, I think the government— they like 
it, and even the external investors, they also calculate, ‘Wow it’s good 
investment.’”
Envision’s plant in Chifeng is the world’s largest green ammonia facility.
Source: Envision Group
In Chifeng, located in the eastern plains of Inner Mongolia, a sprawling 
network of pipes, towers, and tanks churns out ammonia — a molecule found in 
fertilizers, shipping fuels, and chemical production — using some of the 
planet’s most abundant elements instead of fossil fuels.

Wind turbines spin in the surrounding hills, sending electricity through a 
dedicated transmission line to powerelectrolyzers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2022-07-22/the-key-technology-behind-the-green-hydrogen-economy?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>
 in the park, entirely off the country’s main grid. These electrolyzers, 
machines that fill a whole workshop, have an important yet elegantly simple job 
in the clean-energy plant. They split water, itself purified from neighboring 
factories’ waste streams, into hydrogen and oxygen. Uphill, a silo-sized tower 
pulls nitrogen from the air. It’s then sent to be combined with thehydrogen 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2021-06-19/why-hydrogen-is-the-hottest-thing-in-green-energy-quicktake?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>
 to form the final green ammonia product. As if to underscore the plant’s 
balance with nature, a herd of cows ambles by as the sweltering August day 
ends, seemingly unbothered by their industrial neighbor.

Editorial: a rethink on the climate fight

By The Editorial Board 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/authors/ASB62QVt-d0/the-editors?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>

This is the first of three editorials about why the fight against climate 
change is failing, and what to do about it.

With the United Nations’ annual climate conference underway in Turkey, one 
thing is clear: The human and economic costs of rising temperatures and extreme 
weather events are mounting even as the political will to combat the problem 
has been stalling.

The world needs a new approach.

More than a decade ago, in Paris, 195 nations agreed on a common mission: Keep 
the global average temperature from rising by anywhere near 2C — and preferably 
no more than 1.5C — above pre-industrial levels. That’s already beyond the 
upper bound of temperatures the planet has experienced in the Holocene period, 
during which human civilization developed. Going further, in the estimation of 
the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, entails avery high risk of 
repercussions 
<https://www.ipcc.ch/assessment-report/ar6/?__cf_chl_jschl_tk__=pmd_e8aedb5999f121c1e35a12141e8ffc7a7ede1b16-1628499618-0-gqNtZGzNAeKjcnBszQo6>
 ranging from species extinction to widespread simultaneous crop failure.

The Paris Agreement required that each country submit national climate 
commitments, updated every five years, to reduce the carbon emissions driving 
climate change. The hope was that they’d push each other to do better. Despite 
significant progress, there’s much work to be done.

The US pulled out of the agreement in 2025, leaving its cities, states and 
businesses to try to pick up the slack. Last year,emissions rose 
<https://wedocs.unep.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/3b59cfb3-7b48-404e-9dbb-3774aaa4876d/content>
 there and in China and Indonesia, too, while staying roughly flat in India. In 
much of the rest of the Group of 20, they have declined, just not fast enough. 
Even the most ambitious parties — such as the European Union and the UK — 
aren’t on track to meet their pledge of reaching net zero emissions by 2050.

Meanwhile, the planet keeps warming. Cumulative human carbon dioxide emissions 
since 1850 add up to about 2.8 trillion tons. That’s about three-quarters of 
Earth’s estimated “carbon budget 
<https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/3889/2026/>” — the amount beyond which 
global warming will probably exceed 2 degrees. On current trends, the world may 
cross that threshold within a few decades.


This isn’t a problem only for future generations. It’s affecting lives and 
livelihoods now. Consider Europe. The brutal summer of 2026 — during which 
temperatures broke records, major rivers ran nearly dry, and wildfires consumed 
swathes of France and Spain — is hardly an outlier. In the five years through 
2025, the region endured an area-weighted average of 20 days exceeding 30C, 
more than double the amount in 1981 to 1985. A recent peer-reviewed article 
estimated that in 2021 through 2025, European cities suffered more than 58,000
heat-related deaths 
<https://www.nature.com/articles/s44360-026-00209-8/tables/3> attributable to 
human-caused climate change.

The economic damage is harder to measure but potentially vast. In the European 
Union,extreme weather events 
<https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/datahub/datahubitem-view/77389680-ecd2-4f56-926f-8106061a5570>
 incurred an annual average of €45 billion in direct losses from 2020 through 
2024, triple the level of the previous four decades — although some of that 
increase reflects greater exposure as population, development and asset values 
have grown. Meanwhile, drier conditions threaten crucialwaterborne trade routes 
<https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/analysis/publications/europes-critical-infrastructure-under-climate-stress-lessons-from-2026>
, including the Rhine, the Danube and thePanama Canal 
<https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2025GL117038>.

A data center test case

By Jennifer Duggan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AWhagToXyc8/jennifer-duggan?cmpid=BBD111626_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261116&utm_content=11436>

With its temperate climate, sparse population and pro-business government, 
Ireland seemed for years like a perfect location for building data centers. 
Now, the country is becoming a test bed for legal opposition to those centers 
on climate grounds.

This week, Ireland’s energy and water regulator faces a case in the High Court 
over its rules for new data centers. The case, brought by environmental law 
charity ClientEarth and Irish environmental groups, argues that the regulator’s 
latest policy for connecting large energy users to the electricity grid fails 
to comply with Irish and European law and risks locking Ireland into years of 
fossil-fuel use, undermining its ability to meet legally binding emissions 
targets.
Cables link servers inside a data center on the outskirts of Dublin, Ireland.
Photographer: Paul Faith/AFP/Getty Images
It’s part of a recent wave of action against data centers on climate grounds 
in Ireland — one of Europe’s largest data hubs — and around the world. Concern 
and anger about the energy and land demanded by the buildout is already high, 
but with global energy use by these centers expected to almost quadruple by 
2035,according 
<https://about.bnef.com/insights/data-centers/data-center-power-demand-is-set-to-surge-raising-new-climate-challenges-bloombergnef/>
 to BloombergNEF, the potential impact on emissions and man-made climate change 
is huge.

The Irish case is significant because it targets overall policy rather than an 
individual development. It could also have implications for data-center 
construction in Europe more broadly. While European laws won’t change off the 
back of a domestic case, its outcome may guide decision-making in any similar 
challenges that come up in other EU states.

“Ireland has a really robust legal framework when it comes to its climate 
targets,” said Eoin Jackson, a researcher at the London School of Economics 
whose work identifies Ireland as a hotspot for litigation against data centers 
on climate change grounds. Its data-center boom was also early, so “there were 
already a lot of challenges underway to data centers that kind of precede the 
concerns driven by AI.”

As a result, he said, “Ireland has become a little bit of a canary in the coal 
mine when it comes to data-center litigation taken on climate grounds.”

<https://www.parliament.gov.fj/members-of-parliament/hon-inia-seruiratu/>, 
offered delegates a personal story as he aims to drive home messages about the 
importance of prioritizing temperature increases on the seas.

Long before the 1992 Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro — a forerunner event to 
COP — or the Paris Agreement in 2015, “we were already doing adaptation in my 
village,” Seruiratu said. “I remember the first sea wall in my village was in 
1976.” Another followed a few years later, and a third in 2016, he told 
delegates at the Australia pavilion. “That’s the reality, and that is why 
global warming is so important because 1.5C is not a goal — it is a critical 
threshold for us Pacific Islanders.”

On Sunday, delegates took in the sights of Antalya during an officially 
mandated break in negotiations. Organizers offered attendees suggestions for 
visits, including the picturesque Kurşunlu Waterfall and the region’s famed 
archaeological sites.
The ancient stadium in Perge, Antalya, Turkey.Photographer: John 
Ainger/Bloomberg
At the ancient city of Perge 
<https://www.pergeancientcity.com/perge-ancient-city-stadium-and-architecture>, 
a Roman-era stadium once held more than 10,000 spectators and was the site of 
violent, gladiatorial combat. COP-goers touring the ruins were left hoping 
negotiations this week will prove far less adversarial.
