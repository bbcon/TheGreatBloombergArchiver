Robert Mugabe ran Zimbabwe for almost four decades, feeding his subjects a 
steady stream of Marxist rhetoric, keeping business people at arm’s length from 
politics and telling the liberation-war veterans he ruled with to enjoy their 
wealth quietly.

Emmerson Mnangagwa, who succeeded him as president after a 2017 coup, was 
quick to pronounce Zimbabwe “open for business.” He’s since abandoned all 
pretense of running an administration based on socialist principles.
Robert Mugabe at his private residence in Harare in July 2018, the year before 
his death.Photographer: Waldo Swiegers/Bloomberg
That couldn’t have been clearer than at a polo-club wedding reception 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-10/who-really-runs-zimbabwe-this-20-million-wedding-holds-the-answer?cmpid=BBD081026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7543>
 in the capital Harare in late May.

The son of Kudakwashe Tagwirei, a tycoon sanctioned by the US for corruption 
who’sbuilt his fortune 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-03-23/tycoon-seen-scoring-from-zimbabwe-s-3-4-billion-off-budget-debt?cmpid=BBD081026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7543>
 on state contracts and assets, was showered with gifts including $17.5 million 
in money and land from his father, an expensive bull from the president and 
stacks of cash from fellow tycoons and government ministers.

Mnangagwa gave a speech and Boyz II Men, an American doo-wop band, gave a 
surprise performance.

Among the wedding guests were some who helped fund a campaign to extend the 
rule 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-12/zimbabwe-president-says-term-extension-decision-was-collective?cmpid=BBD081026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7543>
 of the 83-year-old president until 2030. The constitutional change enacted 
also means that the country’s leader will no longer be elected by direct vote 
and will instead be chosen by parliament.

The business people concerned have shown how money can sway politics, 
including winning senior positions for themselves in the ruling Zanu-PF party 
and in parliament.
A banner at the Zanu-PF headquarters in Harare on July 8, a day after 
Mnangagwa signed the amendment to extend his term.Photographer: Jekesai 
Njikizanan/AFP/Getty Images
Not everyone is happy. 

Coup leader and vice president, Constantino Chiwenga, has been critical and 
revived the liberation war use ofZvigananda to describe the tycoons, a 
reference to rich business people who betray revolutionary ideals. The term 
loosely derives its origin from a Shona word for blood-engorged ticks, the 
parasites that plague cattle in Zimbabwe.

For now though, Mnangagwa and a wealthy cohort of financiers are running the 
country — and everyday Zimbabweans are stillwaiting for the promised recovery 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-11/zimbabwe-seeks-150-million-african-development-bank-loan-for-debt-clearance?cmpid=BBD081026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7543>

A diesel squeeze spurred by wars in the Middle East and Ukraine is setting the 
stage for aneven worse crunch 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-08/war-brings-winter-of-discontent-for-the-world-s-workhorse-fuel?cmpid=BBD081026_politics&utm_campaign=bop&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260810&utm_content=7543>
 as demand rises ahead of the Northern Hemisphere winter, with Europe 
especially vulnerable. Disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz and damage to 
Persian Gulf refineries, as well as a wave of Ukrainian attacks on Russian 
plants have severely constrained exports from regions that together accounted 
for around a third of global exports last year of the workhorse fuel that’s 
used in transport, industry and heating.

In struggling Bolivia, the government of President Rodrigo Paz is betting on 
commodity crops for much-needed economic growth, while farmers in Brazil are 
increasingly looking beyond their own borders for new land and investment 
opportunities. Clearing land for cattle and soybeans is the main driver of 
deforestation in the Amazon, which often follows the development of roads, and 
Brazil has stepped up enforcement. But as deforestation has plummeted in
