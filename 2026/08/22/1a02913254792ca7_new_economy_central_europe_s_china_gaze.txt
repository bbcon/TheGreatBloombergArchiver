When I began covering Slovakia’s economy in the mid-2000s, the small European 
nation was humming. Using pro-business reforms to lure new projects, for 
several years it posted the fastest growth in central Europe — earning the 
nickname “Tatra Tiger,” a mashup of the country’s primary mountain range and 
the “Asian tiger 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2023-07-08/bloomberg-new-economy-the-asian-tiger-economy-that-never-quite-roared?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260822&utm_content=7850>
” conceit.

Crucial to the story was a flood of investment from the nearby global 
manufacturing powerhouse: Germany. In 1991, Volkswagen AG laid the symbolic
cornerstone <https://sk.volkswagen.sk/en/company/history/1991-1995.html> of 
Slovakia’s post-communist economy when it acquired Bratislava’s main car plant, 
turning it into a flagship operation with 11,000 employees.

From Poland to the Czech Republic and Hungary, Germany was the dynamo behind 
the region’s post-USSR economic rebirth. The symbiosis was such that, in 
Bratislava, we used to say, “When Germany catches a cold, Slovakia gets 
pneumonia.” During the financial crisis and later the Covid-19 pandemic, 
carmaker assembly lines and the business of a wide network of subcontractors 
and other exporters slowed when trouble surfaced in Berlin.
Volkswagen factory outside Bratislava, SlovakiaPhotographer: Sean 
Gallup/Hulton Archive/Getty Images
Fast forward to today, and one might argue Germany itself is now battling with 
pneumonia, with its chemical andautomotive industries 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-27/vw-may-double-job-cuts-to-100-000-as-german-auto-industry-struggles?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260822&utm_content=7850>
 suffering from intense Chinese competition and its policymakers desperately 
trying toengineer a growth revival 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-01/germany-s-elusive-economic-revival-is-within-touching-distance?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260822&utm_content=7850>
. That’s something that would have been tough to foresee two decades back.

But perhaps more far-fetched would have been Slovakia and its neighbors 
finding ways to offset part of that drag by pragmatically leaning into 
investment from China itself. In this way, central-eastern Europe may be able 
to cushion the fallout from the “China Shock 2.0 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-24/china-unfazed-by-world-s-ire-on-trade-as-premier-touts-openness?cmpid=new-economy&utm_campaign=new-economy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260822&utm_content=7850>
” phenomenon, making the impact more manageable than it appears today.

At first glance, the Visegrad Group <https://www.visegradgroup.eu/home> — 
Poland, Hungary, Slovakia and the Czech Republic — might be considered “among 
the most exposed” in Europe to China’s trade policies, August Gudmundsson, an 
economist at research group Gavekal, wrote in a note earlier this summer.

After all, state-subsidized Chinese exporters are flooding European markets 
with cheap electric vehicles and advanced machinery. So this shockwave is 
targeting high-value manufacturing — the backbone of economies including those 
of the Visegrad four.

“Yet the evidence,” Gudmundsson wrote, “points to a more nuanced story.”

The region chose to cooperate with Beijing, hoping to ride its economic 
momentum rather than compete with it. Hungary, under former Prime Minister 
Viktor Orban, moved first, securing billions of euros in Chinese investment and 
positioning itself as Beijing’s gateway into the European Union. Slovakia is 
following suit, and a similar approach is emerging under the new Czech 
government.
The construction site of Chinese battery manufacturer CATL near Debrecen, 
HungaryPhotographer: Attila Kisbenedek/AFP/Getty Images
Regional leaders believe collaboration can help them weather the shock better 
than their neighbors. This approach was on display last month during Slovak 
President Peter Pellegrini’s state visit to China. Rather than taking sides in 
the broader competition between China and the EU, Pellegrini argued that 
Slovakia’s interest lies simply in capturing the investment and jobs the 
rivalry generates — regardless of which side they come from.

The goal is straightforward: attract more Chinese investors. One of China’s 
largest automakers is currently selecting a location for a new plant in Europe, 
and Slovakia wants to host it. The country’s two largest investments in recent 
years already carry Chinese backing — Volvo, majority-owned by Chinese group 
Geely, is building a €1.3 billion ($1.5 billion) electric vehicle plant near 
Kosice, and China’s Gotion High-Tech is investing more than €1.2 billion in a 
battery plant.

Poland has been more of a China-skeptic, showing little appetite for Beijing’s 
investment and imposing measures such as banning Chinese-made cars from 
military bases — a move unlikely to improve relations. Despite this political 
stance, avoiding Chinese commerce is nearly impossible, as its technology and 
trade are increasingly becoming entrenched in the Polish economy.

That said, the latest China shock may also be stoking incentives for major 
European manufacturers to shiftwithin the continent to boost competitiveness, 
with Poland and its neighbors potentially benefiting. “Germany’s 
competitiveness pressures may ultimately spur firms to shift more production 
east,” Gudmundsson said.

Despite political risks and rising labor and energy costs, central Europe 
still stands to benefit from the resulting structural evolution, playing an 
increasingly vital role in integrated manufacturing, research and advanced 
operations.

According to a KPMG survey 
<https://kpmg.com/de/en/media/press-releases/2026/02/german-companies-are-optimistic-about-central-and-eastern-europe.html>
 earlier this year, every fourth German company is considering relocating 
production activities to the central-eastern European region, and 41% are 
planning to invest there.

In multiple ways, then, China is “helping to push the region up the value 
chain,” Gudmundsson wrote. The latest data from the Organization for Economic 
Cooperation and Development show a rising share of central Europe’s 
manufacturing exports contain Chinese contributions (including those from 
Poland), which is significantly above the European average.

A decade ago, a broad, pro-China push among multiple central European leaders 
would have seemed unthinkable. Today, it reflects the economic reality of 
Germany no longer being the massive engine of growth it once was.

It’s also emblematic of Beijing’s need to find competitive beachheads inside a
