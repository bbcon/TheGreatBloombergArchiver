Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Nathan Risser looks at the potential for a rebound in US oil shipments 
as the war with Iran reignites.

American crude oil exports have fallen almost 50% from wartime records, but 
they appear to be poised for a comeback as buyers once again look to the US for 
backup supply.

Before the Iran war, about 4 million barrels a day of crude were sent out from 
ports along the Texas and Louisiana coasts. That soon spiked to almost 6.5 
million barrels a day in late April as Asia was largely cut off from its 
largest suppliers by Iran’s near-closure of the Strait of Hormuz.

As oil exports from the Persian Gulf picked up after the June peace deal, the 
flow of US cargoes slowed to about 3 million barrels a day, according to 
government data released on Wednesday.

But now, the US and Iran are escalating attacks 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-22/us-widens-strikes-on-iran-as-trump-downplays-prospects-of-talks?cmpid=BBBXT072326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260723&utm_content=6888>
 and signaling they aren’t ready to return to the negotiating table. 
Furthermore,Houthi rebels  
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-23/houthis-open-new-front-in-iran-war-by-targeting-ships-in-red-sea?cmpid=BBBXT072326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260723&utm_content=6888>
in Yemen that are allied with Tehran have started attacking tankers in the Red 
Sea — Saudi Arabia’s backup export route when Hormuz is closed.

There have also been shipping disruptions at the Caspian Pipeline Consortium 
terminal on Russia’sBlack Sea coast 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/kazakhstan-to-stop-piping-oil-to-black-sea-after-tanker-attacks?cmpid=BBBXT072326_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260723&utm_content=6888>
, a vital conduit for Kazakhstan’s crude. All of these risks are thrusting 
American producers back into their role as the suppliers of last resort.

Any surge in buying of US oil by Asian and European fuel makers will likely 
not be evident until August, said Kpler Director of Commodity Research Matt 
Smith. July exports are set to be near pre-conflict levels, after record 
shipments depleted US inventories, he said.

“Chatter in the market is that a lot of oil got booked out of the US 
yesterday,” Scott Shelton, an energy specialist at TP ICAP Group Plc, said on 
Wednesday.

Indeed, fixtures data complied by Bloomberg show a flurry of ships, including 
three supertankers, booked for August-loading exports from the US Gulf Coast on 
Wednesday.

South Korean refiner GS Caltex bought 2 million barrels of US crude for 
loading in late August at roughly an $18 per barrel premium to the October 
Dubai benchmark. Earlier this week, the company bought a similar cargo for a 
premium of $14.

Any incremental exports could keep oil prices in the US higher for longer in 
the wake of the largest energy supply disruption in history.

Some of that extra demand may already be showing up in prices for the key US 
export grade, Mars. The medium-sour crude traded at a $2-a-barrel premium to 
WTI futures this week, the highest level in over a month.

Rising crude prices will trickle through to fuels, goods and the broader 
American economy.

—Nathan Risser, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

Southeast Asia’s liquefied natural gas demand growth increasingly hinges on 
the execution of planned gas-fired power projects. BloombergNEF expects 
regional LNG imports to triple by 2035, driven by new gas-fired power plants 
and declining domestic production. Yet delays to gas power projects could 
reduce LNG import growth, with persistently high prices for the fuel 
potentially encouraging some governments to reconsider coal-fired power 
additions beyond 2030.
