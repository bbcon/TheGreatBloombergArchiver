China’s ‘Grabbing Hands’

Local Chinese governments have for years been under enormous funding strains, 
thanks to the nation’s real-estate meltdown — which took away a key source of 
revenue for municipal officials. As they resorted to delaying payments to 
contractors, imposing hefty fines and dinging companies with tax bills dating 
back decades, Nomura economists dubbed it the “grabbing hands 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-09-12/china-s-attempt-to-boost-demand-is-stifled-by-wall-of-austerity?cmpid=BBD081226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7621>
” campaign.

Beijing in time became so alarmed at the economic implications of firms and 
even employeesfailing to get paid 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-02-23/chinese-city-at-risk-of-losing-buses-shows-growing-fiscal-strain?cmpid=BBD081226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7621>
, that the central government rolled out a series of fiscal support packages 
for local authorities, helping them work down their debt.

More recently, Beijing appears to have launched something of a grabbing-hands 
campaign of its own. In May, officials stepped up efforts totax offshore trusts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-31/china-targets-offshore-trusts-in-tax-crackdown-on-ultra-rich?cmpid=BBD081226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7621>
, clamping down on a structure the country’s mega-rich used to invest billions 
abroad. Then in July, the longstanding loophole for such trusts wasofficially 
closed 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-24/china-targets-offshore-trusts-in-sweeping-tax-clampdown?cmpid=BBD081226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7621>
.

Authorities have also been tightening the designation of “high-tech 
enterprises,” which gives qualifying companies a 15% income-tax rate versus the 
standard 25%. Since 2025, more than 6,000 firms have been stripped of that 
status, Beijing-based advisory firm Trivium China wrote in a note last month.

Trivium described it as “part of a broader hunt for wasteful incentives and a 
more selective approach to tax cuts.”

At least 80 companies listed in mainland China or Hong Kong were ordered to 
pay back taxes and penalties in the first half of this year, compared with 89 
such cases in all of 2025, a Bank of America review of media reports shows.

Last week, a slew of insurance providers including Prudential Plc and AIA 
Group saw their stocks tumble as their policies purchased by mainland Chinese 
were targeted forincreased tax collection 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/aia-hong-kong-insurer-shares-tumble-on-tax-collection-risk?cmpid=BBD081226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7621>
.

“China’s recent tax measures are not isolated policy adjustments, in our 
view,” a team of Bank of America analysts including Winnie Wu wrote in a note. 
“Together, they point to a shift in policy priorities from tax relief toward 
fiscal sustainability.”

In the near term, tighter taxation policies may weigh on corporate profit 
margins, incomes and consumer spending, the BofA team wrote. But “it should 
strengthen fiscal sustainability, tax fairness and the social safety net over 
time.”

Need-to-Know Research

On the face of it, the drop in unemployment the US recorded for July (good 
news) and thedecline in payrolls 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-07/us-employers-unexpectedly-shed-jobs-unemployment-rate-falls?cmpid=BBD081226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260812&utm_content=7621>
 (bad news) seem to conflict. But the key reason for the drop in joblessness 
has everything to do with people giving up on being part of the labor force — 
meaning bad news all round.

Since the start of the year, the US labor force participation rate has been in 
almost continuous decline. Alexander Bick, an economist at the Federal Reserve 
Bank of St. Louis, highlighted in a recent note that the rate is the lowest 
since 1976 once you strip out the Covid-year distortions. Part of that stems 
from the aging population — old folks retire and don’t look for new jobs.

The 2026 decline also has to do with updating population records, which 
resulted in an “older population structure,” Bick wrote in a note earlier this 
month. More concerning is the potential for a deep change in the behavior of 
workers in the 25-to-54 prime working age group, he said. This group had 
steadily expanded participation in the years after Covid, but the July rate — 
83.4% — leaves it heading for its first drop since 2020.
