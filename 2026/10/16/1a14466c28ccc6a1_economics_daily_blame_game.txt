Blame Game

At the last major summit 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/china-us-spat-at-g20-largely-came-down-to-dispute-over-one-word?cmpid=BBD101626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10158>
, Scott Bessent worked hardto tag China 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-01/bessent-says-china-prevented-g20-communique-over-imbalance-spat?cmpid=BBD101626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10158>
 as the cause of dangerous fault lines in the world economy. With the US 
Treasury secretary absent from Bangkok this week, it was America and its allies 
in the spotlight.

Steep borrowing costs were top of mind for government and business leaders at 
the International Monetary Fund’s meetings in the Thai capital. The climb began 
in the US and other advanced economies, the blocaccusing China 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-08/china-central-bank-defends-currency-policy-before-eu-trade-talks?cmpid=BBD101626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10158>
 of fueling a giant export surplus that’s damaging everyone else.

That threat hasn’t gone away, and may even be growing. Beijing posted new 
trade numbers 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/china-s-trade-surplus-nears-940-billion-as-export-growth-surges?cmpid=BBD101626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10158>
 during the meetings that point to another record-busting year.

But at the panels and cocktail parties across the congested Southeast Asian 
metropolis, conversations focused on the surge in debt costs and questions 
around the policies of US President Donald Trump — a shift that bolsters 
China’s side of the great global imbalances debate.
Georgieva and colleagues in BangkokPhotographer: Sirachai 
Arunrugstichai/Bloomberg
“Bond yields, bond yields, bond yields,” is how Frederic Neumann, chief Asia 
economist at HSBC Holdings Plc, summed up the Bangkok chatter. What everyone 
wants to know, he said, is when and where they’ll start to bite: “If they keep 
climbing at this pace, the global growth resilience over the past year will 
quickly start to fray.”

In the six weeks since Group of 20 finance chiefs met in Asheville, North 
Carolina, borrowing costs in the US,France 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-10/europe-s-indebted-nations-are-starting-to-blink-at-market-wrath?cmpid=BBD101626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10158>
 and Japan have hitmultidecade highs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-10-11/france-bond-crisis-revives-ghost-of-europe-s-sovereign-debt-crisis?cmpid=BBD101626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10158>
. Yields have eased in the past week or so, but remain far above levels the 
world had gotten used to.

Trump’s war in Iran has been the main driver of higher inflation and interest 
rates this year. Many Bangkok participants including IMF chiefKristalina 
Georgieva 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/imf-chief-alarmed-at-slow-moving-tsunami-of-ai-for-jobs-taxes?cmpid=BBD101626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10158>
 argued that large budget deficits and debts fan the flames. She called 
advanced economies the “worst offenders” and urged them to fix their finances.

That message appeared particularly directed at the US, which in Asheville led 
the charge to condemn China’s flood of exports.Georgieva suggested 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-14/imf-says-us-growth-not-enough-to-fight-high-debt-deficit-levels?cmpid=BBD101626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10158>
 Bessent’s plan for America to grow its way out of debt and deficit trouble 
likely won’t work — posing a problem for the rest of the world.

“Growth, of course, helps,” she said. “But it cannot deliver fast enough the 
necessary adjustments.”

Need-to-Know Research

When you think of which economies are seeing big boosts to exports from trade 
tied to the artificial intelligence boom, you might naturally gravitate toward 
east Asia. Indeed, Taiwan, China and South Korea are star performers. But the 
AI surge is also reshaping things for Mexico — the southern neighbor that’s 
long been a key source of USautomobile imports 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/sheinbaum-vows-to-buy-more-goods-from-the-us-to-sign-trade-deal?cmpid=BBD101626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10158>
.

“The buildout of data centers and the tech-related boom in the US are already 
showing a positive spillover for some Mexican industries,” HSBC economists led 
by Jose Carlos Sanchez wrote in a recent note. “Mexican exports of hardware to 
the US have surpassed exports of autos for the first time in history. This 
trend will likely prevail, as some hardware-related companies in Mexico have 
flexibility to adjust their production lines.”

There’s some provisos. Autos remain the nation’s largest manufacturing sector 
by output. AsFelipe Hernandez 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/ABuMnI4f3Jg/felipe-hernandez?cmpid=BBD101626_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261016&utm_content=10158>
 of Bloomberg Economics wrote in the Mexico Country Primer earlier this month, 
“AI’s contribution to domestic value-added remains considerably smaller as 
production relies more heavily on imported components.” That may well be why 
the US is reportedlypushing Mexico <> to accept new rules to prevent third 
nations from circumventing tariffs.
