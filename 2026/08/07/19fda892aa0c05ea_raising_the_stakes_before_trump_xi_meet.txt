Hello, this is Philip Glamann in Shanghai.

There are some sure-fire ways to know a summit involving Chinese leader Xi 
Jinping and President Donald Trump is in the works.

For starters, their lieutenants hit the phones to work out details and remind 
the other side of their red lines.

That box was ticked late last month when Vice Premier He Lifeng, who handles 
economic affairs with the US,held a video call 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/china-shares-concern-over-restrictive-us-measures-with-bessent?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Trade Representative Jamieson Greer.

Underscoring what each side really cares about, the US used the chat to raise 
issues like flows of critical rare earths and agricultural purchases. Beijing 
complained about “economic and trade restrictions” that Washington has deployed 
against China.
China’s Xi Jinping and President Donald Trump review an honor guard in Beijing 
in May.Photographer: Kenny Holston/Getty Images
Which brings us to our other indicator that a big meeting is approaching: the 
two sides are again trading blows over, well, economic and trade issues.

This week, Beijing used its dominance in drone production to hit back at the 
US for recent curbs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-05/china-hits-back-at-us-with-tighter-drone-export-curbs-sanctions?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
, including some related to tech.

As part of that, Beijing tightened controls on drone exports — a move it 
linked to the US’sban on some robots made overseas 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-29/us-risks-broadening-china-tech-clash-with-robot-inverter-curbs?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 and power inverters, which allow for solar and battery power to become usable 
electricity. Beijing also mentioned Washington’s blacklisting of more than 40 
Chinese firms over allegations of links to forced labor.

China rolled out other sanctions, as well, and said it would launch a national 
security probe into imports of goods like printers and copiers. Later, it 
started a security review of products sold by the American tech firm Palo Alto 
Networks.

Taken together, the moves were a pretty stark reminder that China is building 
up its toolkit for retaliating 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/xi-flexes-expanding-legal-arsenal-to-warn-trump-ahead-of-summit?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 against the US when the need arises.

We’ve seen similar rounds of testiness between China and the US in the run-up 
to big meetings. Before Xi and Trump met in South Korea in October last year, 
the Trump administrationdramatically expanded sanctions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-29/us-hits-sanctioned-firms-subsidiaries-drawing-chinese-rebuke?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 to capture subsidiaries of blacklisted firms.

Days later, China rolled out broad new curbs 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-09/china-tightens-exports-of-rare-earths-and-related-technology?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 on shipments of rare earths and other critical minerals. Those materials are 
vital to a range of high-tech products like smartphones, fighter jets and
missile interceptors 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-31/pentagon-seeks-18-2-billion-in-iran-war-funds-for-interceptors?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
, which are in great demand during the US’s war with Iran.

Then as Xi and Trump met in Busan, both sides said they would hold off on the 
moves for a year.

Might we see China and the US back down in similar fashion when Xi and Trump 
meet as expected in September?

It’s entirely possible.

Both nations have made it clear they want to keep the world’s most important 
bilateral relationship on track.

The meetings that Xi and Trump held late last year and in Beijing this May 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-15/winners-and-losers-from-trump-and-xi-s-two-day-beijing-summit?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 have greatly steadied ties, especially compared to where things stood when 
Trump levied tariffs on nations around the world.

A successful summit this coming fall would go a long way toward keeping their 
truce in place, possibly even extending it.

When it rolled out its latest measures, the Ministry of Commerce in Beijing 
signaled it valued the better relations, calling its retaliation “generally 
restrained” and urging the US to talk through any differences.

And while the US has done some things to irk China, it has been fairly quiet 
lately on an issue that has the potential to really upset Beijing: Taiwan. The 
best example of that isa planned $14 billion arms sale 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2026-05-28/a-14-billion-taiwan-arms-sale-is-trump-s-litmus-test-on-china?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 to the democratic island remains on ice.

For all the frictions over things like tech, Xinjiang and more, it is clear 
neither country really wants the relationship to fall apart.

Yuan Intervention?

The US and Japan recently shook up the world of currency trading with the first
coordinated purchase of the yen 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-05/japanese-yen-intervention-why-bessent-wants-fed-to-expand-fima-backstop?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 in decades.

While Trump called the move “a signal of friendship,” he had a host of reasons 
for doing it, like helping out Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, a fellow 
conservative leader.

Afterward, Bessent suggested 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-04/bessent-says-yen-level-problematic-for-japan-other-currencies?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 “a stable yen is not only important for the US but very important for” Asia. 
That caused some chatter about China’s yuan, which both the US andEurope have 
complained 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-22/eur-cny-why-china-s-yuan-is-drawing-scrutiny-in-europe-what-it-means-for-euro?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 is too weak, providing what they say is an unfair trade advantage.

So, would Beijing ever work out a currency deal with trade partners? Let’s 
just say that snowballs have a better chance of surviving in certain warmer 
regions of the imagined world.

Still, that won’t stop people from making their case that the yuan is too weak.

Last month, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz reinforced his call for dialogue 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-13/merz-urges-currency-dialogue-with-china-as-trade-worries-rise?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 with China about the currency, saying it has been undervalued by 25% “for 
years.” The worry amongMerz and others in Europe 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-28/germany-maps-china-s-weaknesses-in-preparation-for-a-trade-war?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 is their sizable trade deficit with China.

For one thing, Chinese electric vehicles are entering the continent 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-22/chinese-brands-snap-up-record-15-of-europe-s-electric-car-sales?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 at prices so low that companies from other nations are having a hard time 
competing. That raises big worries about job losses in manufacturing.

This is the main reason that trade is becoming an increasingly important issue 
in the 27-member European Union, though policymakers there are being careful to 
avoid triggering a conflict with China.

A stronger yuan would help Europe with its trade problem, but China seems 
very, very unlikely to budge. It often pushes back on the idea that the 
currency is undervalued, saying its level is mostly set by market forces.

And while China has officially said next to nothing about the US’s 
intervention in the yen market, it’s easy to imagine the disdain. Beijing has 
long held the view that countries like Japan made a huge mistake when they 
agreed in 1985 to revalue their currencies to fix trade deficits with the US.

In a reminder that perspective still holds sway in China, in June the 
state-run Global Times said that the deal, known as the Plaza Accord, was the 
result of “political pressure” and the world does not need a new version of it.

Some economists say that focusing on the yuan’s level misses the point. They 
argue that China needs to shift the structure of its economy from one focused 
on investment and manufacturing to consumption — a move that should help 
address the trade imbalances causing friction.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/games/pointed?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
This point has been driven home lately by the performance of the world’s 
second-biggest economy. Exports are the lone bright spot, while the consumer 
sector is stuck in the doldrums, largely because ofa yearslong property downturn
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-24/china-property-crisis-why-market-is-a-mess-what-stimulus-measures-are-planned?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
.

Policymakers in Beijing have indicated they do want to boost domestic 
consumption,making it a priority for 2026 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-12-08/china-s-politburo-vows-to-keep-fiscal-monetary-policy-stances?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
. But the change would take years and involve mammoth tasks like improving the 
social safety net for a population of 1.4 billion people.

In other words, it will take a lot more time and effort than the yen 
intervention the US and Japan pulled off. And it’s a job that officials in 
Beijing seem intent on handling themselves.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/account/newsletters/next-japan?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>

Behind the Great Firewall

A weekly look at the big water cooler news in China.

A funny trend has swept Chinese social media this summer: people showing off 
the ways they are using big green melons to beat the scorching heat.

Over the past few weeks, people have shared photos of themselves, their 
children and even their pets dozing off while wrapped around waxy winter melons.

It’s unclear how the fad got started but it has now gone viral, drawing 
millions of views on platforms including Xiaohongshu and Weibo. It comes as 
countries around the world deal with roasting heat that isdrying rivers in 
Europe 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-04/rhine-set-for-more-disruption-after-hitting-lowest-on-record?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
, sending the overheated intospecial refrigerators in Japan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-04/japan-human-sized-refrigerator-cooling-booth-sees-demand-as-heat-waves-intensify?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
 and leading towarnings in New York 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/new-york-city-is-set-for-hot-and-steamy-end-to-working-week?cmpid=BBD080726_CN&utm_campaign=china&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260807&utm_content=7423>
.

Hugging a room-temperature or even slightly chilled Benincasa hispida to stay 
cool apparently dates back to days gone by in China, back before electric fans 
and air conditioning units.

“The ancients were right; hugging a winter melon really does help you sleep 
soundly,” wrote one Xiaohongshu user who posted a photo of her youngster asleep 
with a melon almost as big as she was. The gourd weighed more than 9 kg (20 
pounds) and cost about 14 yuan ($2).

“The kid fell in love with it the second she touched it!” the mother wrote. 
“This isn’t just a winter melon — it’s basically her designated chill-out 
buddy.”
A giant panda in Chongqing inspects a winter melon, possibly for its cooling 
properties.Photographer: Wang Chengjie/VCG/Getty Images via Visual China Group
There’s a yucky downside to the fad. Some people have woken up to melons that 
broke open, whether by accident or, as some have speculated, because they 
absorbed too much heat.

Photos shared online show bedsheets and floors splattered with a messy liquid 
after the gourds burst during the night.

“Don’t think a winter melon has no temper,” a Weibo user quipped. “It’s got a 
huge attitude.”

So if the AC and fan aren’t cutting it during these hot summer nights, maybe 
try hugging a big green gourd, too.
