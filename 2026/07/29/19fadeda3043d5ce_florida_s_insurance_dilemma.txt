A contentious insurance fix

By Olivia Raimonde 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/AUdOpjWq8Dk/olivia-raimonde?cmpid=BBD072926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7110>

Florida lawmakers knew they had a billion-dollar insurance problem.

The state is a magnet for hurricanes, and the cost of damages from extreme 
weather has risen sharply over several decades. Scientists say this is a 
consequence of climate change, and adynamic that will accelerate 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-18/us-home-insurance-prices-set-to-keep-rising-with-severe-weather?cmpid=BBD072926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7110>
; many in the insurance industry say homeowners were weaponizing natural 
catastrophes to file fraudulent claims. Everyone agreed insurers were straining 
to keep up, with companies dropping hundreds of thousands of homeowners and some
leaving the state altogether 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-24/flood-prone-us-counties-lose-residents-as-risk-costs-mount?cmpid=BBD072926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7110>
.
The home of Jennifer and RJ Garbowicz was demolished after Hurricane Helene 
severely flooded their property.Photographer: Michael Adno/Bloomberg
Worried about who would be left to insure Floridians, the legislature passed 
laws in 2023 that changed the way property insurance works there. Three years 
since the laws went into effect, some homeowners, lawyers and insurance company 
insiders say the reforms created a new set of predicaments.

Florida changed how the legal system applies to insurance companies, making it 
more onerous for homeowners to use the courts to challenge denied claims or low 
payouts. That has emboldened insurers to limit payouts on claims, according to 
critics as well as current and former employees at three different private 
homeowner insurance companies within the state. The current and former 
employees requested anonymity because they weren’t authorized to speak about 
company litigation.

Raymond Powers, a trial attorney in Florida who represents property owners, is 
seeing more claim denials and lower payouts since the new laws went into 
effect. “The pendulum has swung way too far in the other direction,” he says.

Michael Yaworsky, Florida’s insurance commissioner, says the laws fixed an 
enormous problem. “The market was near collapse toward the end of 2022,” he 
said, “and if something was not done to handle the outsized litigation 
challenge we would have found ourselves in a place where we would be looking at 
very drastic or dire options.”

Yaworsky and others who supported the laws don’t talk about climate change. (A 
representative from his office said Yaworsky is “aware” of reports about 
climate change’s link to severe weather events, but he’s focused on “the direct 
impacts of catastrophic weather.“)

Planetary warming 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-extreme-heat-climate-change-data/?cmpid=BBD072926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7110>
, though, is increasing the severity of storm weather, and Florida’s a place 
where you can see this up close. The inflation adjusted, five-year average cost 
of severe storms, flooding and tropical cyclones in Florida ismore than $36 
billion 
<https://www.climatecentral.org/climate-services/billion-dollar-disasters/time-series?state=FL&types=flooding%2Csevere-storm%2Ctropical-cyclone>
, according to data from Climate Central. In 2016 that figure was under $1 
billion. Experts also point to increased property development and population 
growth as contributing factors to the explosion in costs.

The state currently has the highest home insurance premiums in the nation—an 
average of $8,292—according to Insurify, an insurance comparison site. (The 
average premium cost across the country in 2025 was $2,948.)

After mounting losses, several property insurers pulled back from the state or 
stopped writing new policies. This accelerated in 2022 after Hurricane Ian 
dealt an estimated$67 billion 
<https://www.moodys.com/web/en/us/insights/announcements/rms-estimates-us67-billion-in-insured-losses-from-hurricane-ian.html>
 in private market insured losses.

Florida’s dilemma is one that many states are facing. Other parts of the 
country such asCalifornia 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-16/california-rivals-have-starkly-different-plans-to-remake-home-insurance?cmpid=BBD072926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7110>
, Louisiana,Texas 
<https://unlockingamericasfuture.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Unlocking-Americas-Future-_-Report_-Texass-Insurance-Crisis-Dec.-2025.pdf>
 andNorth Carolina 
<https://kenaninstitute.unc.edu/kenan-insight/the-home-insurance-crisis-comes-to-north-carolina-rising-costs-heightened-risk-and-a-changing-climate/>
 have also struggled with strained insurance markets as the impact of extreme 
weather accelerates.

And states are paying attention to the new playbook. Since Florida passed the 
rules, Georgia, Louisiana, North and South Carolina have also considered 
similar measures aimed at reducing litigation against insurers, making it more 
enticing for the companies to turn a profit and stay in the state.

It’s a strain that’s spreading, according to Carolyn Kousky, a member of the 
Coalition for an Insurable Future <https://coalitionforaninsurablefuture.com/>, 
a nonpartisan group working to mitigate the insurance market collapse in the US.

“There is a tension between availability of insurance and affordability of 
insurance,” Kousky says. “Climate changes are making insurance ever more 
necessary because, as the planet warms, more people are having to suffer 
through disaster. Unfortunately, those same trends are also making insurance 
harder to provide, making it less available and more expensive at the moment we 
need it more than ever.”

Fanning the flames

By Sabrina Nelson Garcinuño 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AXeC6LJ3eas/sabrina-nelson-garcinuno?cmpid=BBD072926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7110>
 andAlan Katz 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AE90xk5Fsuc/alan-katz?cmpid=BBD072926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7110>

France fears that shifting winds and rising temperatures could fan the flames 
of massive wildfires in the southwest, even as Spain experienced some relief 
from the blazes near Madrid.

The situation in France’s Gironde remained stable overnight, after regional 
officials ordered the evacuation of about 4,000 people from campsites and other 
tourist accommodation in the town of Lacanau on Tuesday. The size of the fire 
and winds expected to change direction multiple times were among the concerns 
prompting the decision, said Lacanau Mayor Laurent Peyrondet.
A Dash aircraft flies over the wildfire on the Camp de Souge military training 
ground, near Martignas-sur-Jalle, Gironde, on July 28.Photographer: Gauthier 
Bedrignans/Hans Lucas/AFP/Getty Images via Hans Lucas/AFP via Getty Images
“We’re still facing very, very harsh weather conditions,” Peyrondet told 
reporters, as he explained the latest evacuation.

The fourth heat wave to engulf western Europe since the end of May will bring 
temperatures as high as 41C (106F) to southwest France on Wednesday. Wind 
speeds in Bordeaux are set to increase to 20 kilometers (12.4 miles) per hour 
with gusts twice as strong, according to Meteo-France.

Walmart’s electric pumps

By Kyle Stock 
<https://origin.www.bloomberg.com/authors/ARKz8x17rNs/kyle-stock?cmpid=BBD072926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7110>

Since the start of the year, four high-speed charging stations have opened in 
Bentonville, Arkansas, even though the state has been a laggard in 
transitioning to electric vehicles.

Bentonville, however, is Walmart Inc. country and these are Walmart chargers — 
three at stores near the company’s headquarters and one at the airport.
An EV charging station at a Walmart parking lot in Duarte, California.
Photographer: Frederic J. Brown/AFP/Getty Images
In a matter of months, Walmart has become a major player in the burgeoning 
business 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-04-08/us-ev-fast-charging-network-grows-amid-high-gas-prices?cmpid=BBD072926_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260729&utm_content=7110>
 of quickly fueling electric vehicles, speeding past Costco as well as more 
established charging companies with its in-house network of electron pumps. 
Walmart has hosted chargers from brands like Electrify America since 2018. But 
now the company is building its own network — and quickly. As of March of last 
year, every EV station at a Walmart is under the company brand.

While the blitz of infrastructure will fuel in-store sales, it will also 
redraw the American charging map, making public electrons cheaper and easier to 
find.
