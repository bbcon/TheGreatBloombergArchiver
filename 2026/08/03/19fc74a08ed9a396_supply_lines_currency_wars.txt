Trade wars are simmering 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/china-shares-concern-over-restrictive-us-measures-with-bessent?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>
 and major powers are bogged down in real wars, but the guns of August are 
turning to currency markets.

With their joint intervention to support a yen that last week tumbled to 
levels against the dollar last seen in the 1980s, the US and Japan are heading 
into a summer showdown against foreign-exchange speculators.

It’s a fight prompted by interest-rate differentials and market bets on 
diverging directions in monetary policy. It’s motivated by a desire not to see 
Japan’s problems spillover into the US and theTreasuries market 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-02/yen-traders-brace-for-more-intervention-with-us-at-japan-s-side?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>
. Yet it’s one that also says a lot about both the Trump years and American 
power and the rolling fallout from policymaking in Washington.

Read More: US, Japan Move to Transform Yen Landscape With Joint Action 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-02/katayama-to-say-japan-us-acted-together-on-yen-reuters-reports?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>

The domestic case for intervention in currency markets by Japan’s government 
is simple. A weaker yen means higher import prices and reduced buying power for 
Japanese consumers and voters. All of which risked undermining Prime Minister 
Sanae Takaichi’seconomic plans 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-07-30/japan-pm-takaichi-faces-biggest-test-yet-as-yen-sinks-approval-ratings-drop?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>
.

The US case is more complicated. Group of Seven governments usually intervene 
in markets on each other’s behalf only in times of crisis. The last time the US 
acted to help Japan it was to help weaken a surging yen after a devastating 
earthquake in 2011.

But President Donald Trump on Sunday framed the US intervention as an act of 
“friendship” 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-02/trump-says-yen-intervention-is-signal-of-friendship-with-japan?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>
 on behalf of an ally. “They wanted a little bit of help, and we’re always 
there for Japan,” he told reporters.

Trusted Partners

Confirming the US involvement on social media, Treasury Secretary Scott 
Bessent offered a blunter assertion of power. “The Trump Administration 
delivers for America’s trusted partners,” hewrote <>, adding that “economic 
security is national security. And the US-Japan alliance is built on both.”

Other American allies (hello, Canada and Europe) would be forgiven for 
scratching their heads at that message. Since last year Washington has been 
signaling it’s having second thoughts about the transatlantic security 
umbrella. Trump has in recent weeks been keener to threaten tariffs on Canada 
and the European Union than bolster alliances. That the USreportedly 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-31/yen-s-intervention-rally-stalls-as-traders-alert-for-more-action?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>
 sold euros to buy yen feels like a symbolic gesture.

But the move is also a consequence of Trump’s own actions. The yen likely 
wouldn’t have tested its lows this year if he and Israel hadn’t launched war on 
Iran and caused thesurge in energy prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-01/rising-fuel-prices-hit-americans-now-without-tax-refunds-to-tap?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>
 and a related strengthening in the dollar.

<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-02/trump-says-iran-talks-to-begin-monday-after-calling-off-strikes?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>

Japan also isn’t the only net energy importer that’s been affected. The South 
Korean won, Indonesian rupiah and other currencies have all had volatile years.

There is also American self-interest behind Team Trump’s actions.

Bessent, who made a fortune speculating on currencies under George Soros’ 
tutelage, has been vocal about the dangers of what he has called an undervalued 
yen. As Bloombergreported 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-11/bessent-s-unprecedented-grasp-on-japanese-policy-tests-takaichi?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>
 earlier this year, he was direct in expressing US concerns to his Japanese 
counterpart in Davos in January and travelled to Tokyo in May on his way to 
Beijing to reinforce the message.
Polymarket on Twitter / X <https://x.com/Polymarket/status/2083390480088236247>
That concern and Friday’s intervention 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-03/bessent-seen-rebuffing-wall-street-on-guidance-for-us-debt-sales?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>
 were driven partly by fears a disorderly weakening of the yen and a Japanese 
scramble to respond would lead to the sale of US Treasuries from Japan’s 
official holdings to fund the defense of the currency. The risk being that 
would lead to higher yields and interest rates in the US.

In the background is also another Trump concern: the president’s tariff wall. 
Economists and CEOs will tell you that the uncertainty caused by Trump’s 
tariffs and their chaotic rollout has acted as a damper on lots of economies 
and increased the vulnerability to shocks.

Related: Dollar Logs Worst Week in Over Three Months Amid Fed Doubts 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-31/dollar-heads-for-worst-week-in-three-months-as-fed-doubts-grow?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>

In a textbook world, economists will also add, one country raising tariffs on 
goods from another will lead to the target’s currency adjusting by weakening. 
Which means the yen has been behaving as economic textbooks say it should in 
the wake of Trump’s trade attacks.

The advent of global supply chains and the reality most exports today rely on 
imported inputs has made those links more complicated. But there is no doubt a 
weaker yen has helped offset the cost of tariffs for big Japanese automakers 
and other exporters who report their profits in yen and harvest sales in 
dollars, as should become clear this week as theyreport earnings 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/weak-yen-offers-toyota-an-earnings-respite-from-tariffs-war?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>
.

Persistent dollar strength and weakness in the yen and other currencies, in 
other words, could easily undermine Trump’s campaign for an American 
reindustrialization. Or at the very least revive complaints from domestic 
manufacturers who have argued for years that currency manipulation by US 
trading partners is a competitive challenge.

It may be tempting to look at the yen’s sharp rally 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-02/yen-traders-brace-for-more-intervention-with-us-at-japan-s-side?cmpid=BBD080326_TRADE&utm_campaign=trade&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260803&utm_content=7230>
 today after the US and Japanese announcements and see a temporary win for an 
old alliance, or a reminder of American financial might. But like every other 
Trump shock to global economic order, there’s a lot more going on than it first 
seems.
