Bangkok Blues

The headline numbers in the IMF’s latest World Economic Outlook suggest things 
aren’t too bad. The report itself, though, is full of foreboding.

Even as it raised the 2026 growth forecast a fraction, and maintained its 
prediction that the global economy will gain speed next year, the fund spent
dozens of pages 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/world-economy-holds-up-but-nears-limits-of-resilience-imf-says?cmpid=BBD101326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9993>
 outlining the risks that are now building.

As my colleague Jorgelina Do Rosario 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AThOFgBL0B8/jorgelina-do-rosario?cmpid=BBD101326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9993>
 reported, the IMF has outlinedthree big things 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/world-faces-risk-cocktail-from-ai-oil-and-debt-says-imf-chief?cmpid=BBD101326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9993>
 to focus on at its annual meetings in Bangkok this week: AI, energy and fiscal 
stress.

The first is what’s powering growth in many economies, and counts as a 
“positive demand shock” in the IMF’s terms. Still, the benefits so far have 
been confined to a smallish group of countries, and haven’t yet lifted 
productivity.
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-13/imf-forecasts-2027-global-gdp-boost-but-risks-remain-video?cmpid=BBD101326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9993>
That could still happen, the IMF said. “The potential upside should not be 
dismissed.” But equally “the current technology tailwind could stop abruptly if 
expectations about AI-related profits and productivity gains are reassessed.”

On the energy front, the fund sounds impressed by how well the world has 
weathereddisruptions 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/supertankers-turn-from-gulf-oil-transfer-hub-amid-iran-s-attacks?cmpid=BBD101326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9993>
 resulting from the US attack on Iran — and also worried about how long that 
can last. Inventories have gotten drawn down, there’s no sign of an end to the 
conflict, and the “margins of adjustment are not unlimited.”

As for the debt problems plaguing developed economies in particular, the WEO 
issued a call for urgent action — although IMF fiscal advice to those countries 
hasn’t been much heeded in the past. Meanwhile a separate IMF report on
financial stability 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-13/surging-bond-yields-are-top-risk-to-financial-system-imf-says?cmpid=BBD101326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9993>
 was also published Tuesday, and singled out surging bond yields as the top 
risk,Laura Noonan 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AW2mJwb1Qak/laura-noonan?cmpid=BBD101326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9993>
 reports.

Boiling it all down, the IMF is essentially painting a picture of global 
growth in which one big thing has gone right — the AI investment boom. That’s 
papered over a lot of cracks. And it’s helped distract investor attention from 
the long list of things, AI included, that could go wrong.

Need-to-Know Research

Chinese automobile exporters may have to rethink their ambitions in the wake 
of the Beijing-Brussels deal thatsets a quota 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/china-says-hybrid-car-deal-with-europe-to-ensure-export-space?cmpid=BBD101326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261013&utm_content=9993>
 for hybrid vehicles. But another part of the China’s industrial base can look 
forward to the future with confidence, according to Natixis economists: 
electrical-equipment makers.

“Electricity demand from AI data centers is on track to double globally within 
the next five years,” Natixis economists led by Alicia Garcia-Herrero wrote in 
a note Monday. “Chinese producers are well positioned for this emerging 
industrial upturn,” after having steadily improved their competitiveness in 
recent years, they wrote. China’s share of global power equipment exports rose 
to 33% by last year from 22% in 2017, they estimate.

“We thus expect the global AI power demand to help turn the tide for at least 
part of China’s industrial companies,” Natixis wrote. “The change is already 
underway as we see corporate capex starting to grow again,” the team said. “All 
in all, Chinese power equipment makers are becomingan apparent winner in the 
global AI race.”
