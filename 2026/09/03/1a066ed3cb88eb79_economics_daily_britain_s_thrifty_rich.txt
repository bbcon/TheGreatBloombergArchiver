Britain’s Thrifty Rich

Greetings from Richmond, a leafy London suburb where you can spot deer in the 
park, multi-million pound mansions — and their owners buying second-hand 
clothes, getting free repair jobs and refilling their washing liquid in a local 
church dubbing as a council-run community hub.

Call them Britain’s thrifty rich. As cost-of-living pressures continue to bite 
and “old” becomes synonymous with tasteful, even well-off families are
increasingly buying used goods 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-03/uk-s-thrifty-rich-are-shopping-second-hand-and-trading-down?cmpid=BBD090326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8468>
 and repairing rather than replacing.

About 40% of those earning more than £83,000 ($112,000) a year shop 
second-hand, fix items and do their own home improvements more frequently than 
they did three years ago, according to a Retail Economics survey, compared with 
a third of households overall.

Savings intentions among the top 20% have almost doubled since Covid, 
according to a Bloomberg analysis of GfK data, while their appetite for 
splashing out items hasn’t really changed.

It’s a worrying signal for the economy, as the top 20% of earners drive about 
40% of spending.

The Labour government has already asked the well-off to shoulder more of the 
UK’sfiscal burden 
<https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESqQEB6zswFb1oZOr4qw4iRzh7h_NpYdNkh_iJXXbLOQObw1SWGByK-fAcESTGYYXumwJ8vtoOvqF32oCmbYeK4ng5i8PlcQ2A9DiYJOWaLz-KRWfCakH5AdFjT37FiustuoSt9sWrvlC48A_cV9M9uVSnBlRPeO-DMIDfmQ7Wbpflqm_WUG1DzwmqxoNVN6JCOF10GJY2VF7390HMdm-_p1z66GPVB3RF4trQ>
, with taxes on private school fees, a levy on expensive homes and, at the very 
top end, a clampdown on allowances used by the internationally mobile 
super-wealthy.

It may very well have to come back for more. Chancellor John Healey’s debut 
budget math 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-02/healey-bind-deepens-as-markets-chip-away-at-uk-fiscal-buffer?cmpid=BBD090326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8468>
 is getting trickier by the day as borrowing costs rise and the war in Iran 
intensifies. with economists estimating he’s already lost about half of the 
£23.6 billion he inherited from his predecessor.

Either way, the rich can count on their favorite brands to accommodate their 
taste for thrift — even if they don’t live in Richmond. Selfridges now sells 
second-hand designer clothes, Church’s loafers can be fully resoled again and 
again, and Rimowa will fix suitcases in its New Bond Street store.

Need-to-Know Research

A Deutsche Bank look back at the development of artificial intelligence since 
the term was coined about seven decades ago offers some key takeaways for the 
current phase. The first: “progress is non-linear — and exponential growth is 
almost invariably underestimated.”

That’s evident looking at computing power over the last decade and a half or 
so, Adrian Cox, a thematic strategist at the bank, wrote in a recentnote 
<https://deutschebank.scnem.com/goto.php?l=2omd8.q2ga9a,u=fdffb465dbeb696f8b60394dcacc7606,n=2lwg.8ehb8k,art_id=2lwk.16l4qmn>
. Conventional computing capability doubled every 18 to 24 months, “but bigger 
and better systems, memory and algorithms have accelerated capabilities far 
beyond that.” Capabilities are roughly quadrupling each year now, versus 
multiplying at a 1.4 pace before the “deep learning” era, he wrote.

Another lesson: tech revolutions take time to pay off, and generate costs 
before resulting in an acceleration in productivity (though the US Treasury 
chief, for one, has been saying the benefits willcome faster 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-02/bessent-blasts-us-hyperscalers-for-failure-to-tout-ai-benefits?cmpid=BBD090326_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260903&utm_content=8468>
 this time). One takeaway that should be familiar to all, after the Covid 
supply-chain woes several years back: “hardware is often a bottleneck.” With 
AI, it’s specifically about connecting consumers to online hardware 
(semiconductors).
