A road cuts through it

by Polly Mosendz

When I visited the proposed Ambler Mining District, it was an unseasonably 
warm 6 degrees, balmy for the region of Alaska above the Arctic Circle where 
there are no roads in or out. It gets warmer every winter, says pilot Dirk 
Nickisch. He flew my video crew and I around the region for four days in a 
70-some-odd-year-old military plane named Pumpkin. The permafrost here is 
squishier and softer than it should be, a byproduct of climate change.

I’ve come about fifty miles north of the Arctic Circle to see the proposed 
route of the Ambler Road, a 211-mile-long gravel path that would snake past 
copper and zinc mines. It is a silent and idyllic place where bush planes are 
the primary form of transportation. When outsiders come here, it’s usually to 
take in the Gates of the Arctic National Park, one of the nation’s least 
visited.
The 211-mile industrial road will pass through untouched Alaska wilderness, 
including land around the Koyukuk River.Photographer: Emily Sullivan for 
Bloomberg Businessweek
Depending on whom you ask, the Ambler Road has been under discussion for 10 
years, or 20, or about 200, whenManiilaq 
<http://www.alaskool.org/language/maniilaq/webhtm/Maniilaq_Intro.htm>, an 
Inupiaq prophet in the region, predicted the small village of Ambler would 
become a major trade hub.

Permissions required for construction have been debated for years, pitting 
some politicians and the local community against one another. The Biden 
administration sided with those concerned about the road’s impact on the region 
and had declined to issue a permit for the road, but when Donald Trump 
reentered office, the issue became open for debate.

In October, the Trump administration announced the federal government would 
take a 10% equity stake in Trilogy Metals, the mining company behind the Ambler 
project. The deal fully vests when the road is completed.

The Trump administration knew the miners needed their permit. In exchange, it 
asked for the US government to get equity in the company developing the region, 
according to a person familiar with the negotiation who was not authorized to 
discuss it publicly. The deal was cut in October 
<https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/10/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-approves-ambler-road-project-to-unlock-alaskas-mineral-potential/>
, and the miners had the federal government’s backing to alter the Alaskan 
wilderness. It’s among the variety of deals the administration has struck, with 
the government now investing in private companies in a way it previously 
hadn’t. This particular deal, sources say, came together quickly.

With the federal government on board, Trilogy’s stock popped: It traded at 
$11.29 per share the week of the deal’s announcement, up from the prior 
quarter’s average of $1.78 per share.

During my visit to Alaska, mixed feelings about the road persisted. In the 
small villages along the road, “Protect the Kobuk” and “No Ambler Road” signs 
are common, popping up on trucks and outside houses. Most everyone hunts in the 
region and hunters worry how an industrial road will impact their food supply.
“I’m scared for that road to come,” says Eva Cleveland, who lives in Kobuk, 
just a few miles from where the road has been planned.Photographer: Emily 
Sullivan for Bloomberg Businessweek
Others, though, hoped the road could lower prices. These rural Alaskans rely 
on air freight for everything they can’t grow or hunt — if fuel, food and 
medical supplies could come by road, some locals told me they hoped the cost of 
living would fall. Most villages have a single convenience store in town, 
reselling Costco goods, like bananas for $3 to $4 a pop.

I arrived in this corner of the Arctic a few months before Interior Secretary 
Doug Burgum, who came to see milepost zero of the Ambler Road in May. He landed 
in Coldfoot, where Dirk lives half the year, an old gold mining town now mostly 
known for its proximity to Dalton Highway and Northern Lights tourism.

Even though the road may take years to build, and the litigation remains 
ongoing, the event was a groundbreaker of sorts. “We’re getting as much built 
as we can while President Trump is in office,” Burgum said.

A postponed reckoning

By Todd Woody

The US government released a plan 
<https://www.usbr.gov/ColoradoRiverBasin/post2026/final-eis/index.html> on 
Friday that for now staves off catastrophic cuts to the water supply in 
Arizona, California and Nevada as theColorado River climate crisis 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2021-opinion-us-drought-southwest-arizona-water-crisis/?cmpid=BBD080126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260801&utm_content=7214>
 leaves reservoirs at record lows, threatening a resource supporting 40 million 
people and more than $1 trillion in gross domestic product.

The federal government will determine the size of specific reductions in water 
allocations for the next two years before October, but its plan is based on a
proposal 
<https://www.azwater.gov/sites/default/files/2026-05/2026.05.01%20LB2YearPlanFINAL.pdf>
 from the affected states that sought to avoid deep cuts.
Visitors look out at a white “bathtub ring” of mineral-stained rocks left by 
higher water levels in the Lake Mead National Recreation Area, Arizona, on July 
25.Photographer: Ethan Miller/Getty Images via Getty Images North America
Experts have described the proposed curtailments as a short-term fix amid 
rising temperatures and plummeting precipitation 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2022-08-16/drought-ravaged-states-face-deep-water-cuts-interior-announces?cmpid=BBD080126_GREENDAILY&utm_campaign=greendaily&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=260801&utm_content=7214>
. But the plan noted that cuts nearly twice as large as those advocated by the 
states could be necessary.
