Welcome to our guide to the commodities driving the global economy. Today, 
reporter Elena Mazneva looks at the political implications of the jump in 
European gas prices.

Natural gas in Europe has surged to prices last seen during the 2022 energy 
crunch. The policy response is nowhere near crisis levels yet.

European governments have pledged 
<https://www.bruegel.org/dataset/2026-european-energy-crisis-fiscal-response-tracker>
 about €12 billion of incremental measures to cushion the impact of the US-Iran 
war on energy bills, according to Brussels-based think tank Bruegel. After 
Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine four years ago, assistance to consumers 
such as price caps and subsidies totaled about €650 billion, itestimated 
<https://www.bruegel.org/dataset/national-policies-shield-consumers-rising-energy-prices>
.

This discrepancy reflects the fact that the current supply disruption has, so 
far, had a less severe impact on the market. Europe’s gas benchmark has more 
than tripled this year to about €97 per megawatt-hour, but that’s still far 
below the extreme high of €345 seen in 2022.

The region has taken action since that crisis to reduce its vulnerability to 
external shocks, boosting its renewable energy capacity and reducing gas 
demand. But with gas storage only about 75% full, compared with a seasonal 
average of 90%, price risks remain.

If this winter proves to be colder than average and supplies are tight, the 
benchmark could spike above €200 per megawatt-hour, according to consultant 
Energy Aspects Ltd. That would be a competitive disaster for European industry, 
especially with the equivalent price in the US near €8 per megawatt-hour.

If that happens, governments with stretched budgets and rising borrowing costs 
may still have little appetite to revive measures used during the last energy 
crisis.

In France, which just suffered its worst violent street protests in decades, 
Finance Minister Roland Lescure has acknowledged the government’s response to 
grievances including rising fuel prices and the high cost of living is “
constrained financially 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-26/macron-bargaining-chips-paris-protests-violence-student-union-unef?cmpid=BBBXT102626_ENERGY&utm_campaign=energy&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261026&utm_content=10579>
.” He has floated the idea of advancing by a couple of months the annual 
increase in the minimum wage, while also offering subsidies to high-mileage 
drivers.

The European Union still has some policy levers it could pull to boost supply. 
The bloc is already moving to delay newmethane-emissions rules 
<https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20261021IPR48321/methane-rules-parliament-backs-longer-delay-for-energy-importers-and-producers>
 that could have hindered gas imports.

A full ban on the purchase of Russian liquefied natural gas is still due to 
come into force in January, and there is pressure from some parties torethink 
such measures 
<https://www.politico.eu/article/afd-alice-weidel-urges-quick-ukraine-talks-and-a-return-to-cheap-russian-energy/>
 in order to ease the cost of living.

—Elena Mazneva, Bloomberg News

BNEF today

The number of containerships powered by LNG is set to nearly double by 2030, 
shifting the center of dual-fuel growth away from traditional carriers, 
according to BloombergNEF. Their rapid expansion is concentrating marine LNG 
demand on high-volume liner routes, providing the steady volume needed to 
support investment in bunkering infrastructure for the fuel.
