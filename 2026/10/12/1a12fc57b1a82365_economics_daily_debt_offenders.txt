Debt Offenders

The IMF is no stranger to debt distress, but typically with a focus on the 
emerging world. At this week’s annual meetings, it’s in the most advanced 
economies thatwarning lights are flashing 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-10/rich-countries-are-now-on-front-lines-as-world-s-risk-map-shifts?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
.

A government bond selloff has sent yields in the US, Europe and Japan to 
longtime highs — adding to already acute budget strains, and threatening to 
spill into emerging economies.

IMF chief Kristalina Georgieva has already flagged the world’s fiscal challenge
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-10/european-debt-crisis-risks-have-risen-sharply-bundesbanker-says?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
 as one of thethree top issues 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/world-faces-risk-cocktail-from-ai-oil-and-debt-says-imf-chief?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
 for the Bangkok summit —along with AI 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/imf-chief-alarmed-at-slow-moving-tsunami-of-ai-for-jobs-taxes?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
 and the energy shock — and points to developed nations as the “worst 
offenders.”
 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-07/imf-chief-warns-governments-to-act-now-on-record-debt-video?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
The IMF has been warning for years about the need for more budget discipline 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-10-07/imf-chief-warns-governments-to-act-now-on-record-debt-video?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
, without getting much traction in richer countries. With inflation now rising 
too as a result of the US war on Iran, she’s been sounding more concerned 
lately about the world economy’s resilience – and highlighting political risks 
as well as economic ones.

Governments facing bigger interest bills will struggle to afford other things, 
like helping their citizens with the high cost of living, she told a New York 
event last month. “We should prepare for people being more unhappy. Maybe on 
the street.”

French students 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-10-11/france-doesn-t-have-a-plan-to-calm-student-protests?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
 are there already, with more than a quarter-million joining recent protests to 
demand more public resources for education.

Poorer countries are vulnerable to the bond-market turmoil too, but so far 
they’ve have weathered it pretty well.

The fund and the World Bank haven’t seen a rush of new borrowers, and the 
yield gap between emerging- and developed-market bonds is at a two-decade low.

If there’s more resilience in the emerging world now, it’s partly because 
policymakers have learned from bitter experiences. Thailand’s capitalhosts the 
IMF this week 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-07/thailand-puts-bangkok-on-24-hour-flood-watch-for-imf-meetings?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
, but 30 years ago it was ground zero for the Asian financial crisis. Countries 
have built up financial buffers since then, and generally run a tighter 
economic ship.

“Several emerging markets have done their homework,” says Adriana Dupita 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/authors/AT2AwuhSypM/adriana-dupita?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
 at Bloomberg Economics, “and arrive at this episode of market turbulence in 
better economic shape.”

The Week Ahead

Higher gasoline prices 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-11/trump-says-russia-and-ukraine-agreed-to-energy-ceasefire-mv3wxxro?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
 probably led to quicker overall US inflation in September even as underlying 
price pressures moderated, supporting views that the Federal Reserve will 
refrain from raising interest rates this month.

The closely-watched consumer price index is seen rising 0.6% after a 0.4% 
increase a month earlier, based on the median projection in a Bloomberg survey 
of economists ahead of Wednesday’s Bureau of Labor Statistics release. That 
would mark the largest advance in five months.

Excluding fuel and food, the so-called core measure probably rose 0.2%, a step 
down from August. Compared with a year earlier the core CPI is seen rising 
2.5%, a touch firmer than the prior month when the annual increase was the 
smallest since 2021.

Need-to-Know Research

It’s no wonder the EU was interested in securing a deal with China to limit 
its exports 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-09/eu-says-accord-with-china-could-cut-hybrid-car-exports-by-half?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
 last week — at nearly 2.5% of GDP, the region’s rolling 12-month trade deficit 
with the nation is nearly €400 billion ($448 billion), Wells Fargo calculates. 
As a share of GDP, that’s 2.4%, a record outside of Covid.

“The European labor market is showing continued evidence of the effects of 
Chinese competition,” Marcus Jennings, a Wells Fargo macro strategist, wrote in 
a note Friday. “The shedding of industrial jobs is gradually feeding through to 
industrial employment at the EU level, with industrial employment having 
moderated from the highs in the last two years despite total EU employment 
continuing an upward trend.”

“A near-term agreement is unlikely to materially improve the process of trade 
rebalancing, and more significant changes probably require more direct action 
from the EU side,” Jennings wrote just before the announcement of an 
export-limiting deal onhybrid cars 
<https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-10/eu-plans-measures-to-cut-imports-of-chinese-hybrid-cars-by-half?cmpid=BBD101226_NEF&utm_campaign=nef&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter&utm_term=261012&utm_content=9933>
. “Risks of more forceful EU action and therefore China retaliation will build 
into mid-to-late 2027 should progress on trade rebalancing fall short of 
expectations,” he wrote.
