• Central European economies — the Visegrad group of Poland, Hungary, Slovakia and the Czech Republic — are deepening ties with Chinese capital as Germany's industrial slump erodes the region's traditional growth engine, Bloomberg's New Economy newsletter reported.
• Slovakia has secured its two largest recent foreign investments from Chinese-linked capital: a €1.3 billion ($1.5 billion) EV plant near Kosice from Volvo (majority-owned by China's Geely) and a €1.2 billion-plus battery plant from Gotion High-Tech.
• KPMG survey data cited in the report: one in four German companies is considering relocating production to Central and Eastern Europe, and 41% are planning to invest there.
• Poland remains comparatively skeptical of Beijing, banning Chinese-made vehicles from military bases, even as Chinese trade and technology grow more embedded in its economy.

**Germany's Slowdown Reshapes Regional Investment Flows**
Germany's chemical and automotive industries continue to struggle against Chinese competition, complicating Berlin's efforts to engineer a growth revival. That weakness is prompting Central European governments to pragmatically court Chinese investment rather than compete with it, following a path Hungary under former PM Viktor Orban established by positioning itself as Beijing's EU gateway. Gavekal economist August Gudmundsson noted that despite the Visegrad group's high exposure to Chinese EV and machinery competition, OECD data show a rising Chinese-content share in the region's manufacturing exports, above the European average — evidence, he argued, that China is helping push Central Europe up the value chain even as it pressures high-value manufacturing.

**Divergent National Strategies**
Slovakia and, increasingly, the Czech Republic are actively pursuing Chinese manufacturing investment, with Slovak President Peter Pellegrini framing the country's interest as capturing jobs and capital regardless of origin. Poland has taken a more cautious political stance, though its economy remains difficult to insulate from Chinese trade and technology penetration. Gudmundsson added that Germany's competitiveness pressures may further spur European manufacturers to shift production eastward.
